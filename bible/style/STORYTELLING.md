## On screen

- **No title cards or quote cards,** for now. Scenes fade in from black and out to black.
- **No montages for passing time.** Cut straight to the later moment and let the change show. For a long jump, fade to black and caption it: *Ten years later.* *Exception (2026-10-03): the Opening and visions.*
- **It's headed for live action.** The 3D animation is the placeholder, so write every shot as something a camera and real actors could do (ROADMAP §15). Expect moments of bright, vibrant color and some scenes that need advanced effects. *Exception (2026-10-03): the Opening and visions.*
- **Episodes run about an hour** (2026-10-03).
- **A slower start is fine; a lurch isn't.** An episode can open more slowly, but the change of pace has to be smooth, and the end mustn't feel rushed. The way to do it: let story time slow down gradually while screen time holds steady, and start the slowdown before the act break, not at it. (Ruth: twelve years in Moab move fast, and the parting on the road slows into real time before Act Two begins. See BEATS §3.)
- **Make the main character's arc visible at every stage.** Mark each step with something we see or hear: a laugh, a dance, a name refused and then accepted, a song. Echoes work best when nobody points at them. (Naomi's laugh, dance and lullaby.)
- **Links between episodes live at the edges.** Anything that ties one episode to another, such as a flash-forward or a callback, goes only at the beginning or end of an episode or scene, as a small change. Each episode stays whole on its own. (Your note on the David connection at the end of Ruth.)

## The Opening, visions, and the primordial

*From your notes of 2026-10-03. These apply to the Opening, to visions, and to every later moment where the Opening's world breaks through.*

- **No words, and the world speaks.** In the Opening nothing is spoken. The sound and the picture do it.
- **Psychedelic means connected.** In your words: "not only the variation in sensations/perceptions, but also the inter-connectedness of each sensation/perception." Sound and picture are designed as one thing. Both are lush and colorful.
- **Sound carries, picture strikes.** A sudden image should feel "sudden, surprising, and powerful." It's "the audio that carries the audience through the scene smoothly by building tension."
- **Always a face.** There's always one thing in focus, and it's "a kind of face": clear, understandable and steady, "a sort of stability in a sea of change."
- **The sea is what the face is.** When the surround is a sea of particles, they're the same kind of thing as the one in focus, and they change when it does.
- **It comes down.** The surround grows "more and more still and orderly" until the world "makes normal sense." The earliest, most abstract stages are where the imagery runs freest.
- **It never fully goes.** The fantastical look stays "in glimpses all the way through past Abraham's and Moses' lives, and further." It stands for the primordial, so it's "referenced, connected with, and hinted to throughout the entire series (with a fulfilling conclusion)." Where those glimpses might fall is proposed in the [Opening's treatment §6](../story/arcs/01-beginnings/opening/TREATMENT.md#6-glimpses).
- **Words can be placeholders.** You read "the heavens and the earth," "light," "the waters" and God's speech in Genesis 1 as reaching for things their writer had no names for. On screen that reading is shown and never stated.

## People

- **Real family dynamics.** Keep the humor and warmth, and add the friction a modern eye recognizes: brothers who annoy each other, parents who tell them to settle down. (Ruth: Chilion's "House of bread," Mahlon's irritation, Elimelech's "Enough.")
- **Group scenes need real interaction.** At departures, arrivals and gatherings, people talk to one another: friends, neighbors, officials, kin. They don't just look on.
- **Follow more than one person.** Give the other leads scenes from their own point of view, with their own homes, families and lives. (Ruth gets her own scenes in Moab before she joins Naomi's story.)
- **Big choices need visible reasons.** When a character does something extraordinary, build up what's pulling her before she does it. (Ruth's loyalty is earned across Act One.)
- **Let faces carry layers.** Many feelings can pass across one face in one line. Your example: Naomi struggles to get her farewell out, tearing up, with calculation, pain, exhaustion and acceptance all passing over her face. Her characters are colorful, layered and determined.
- **Small acknowledgments matter.** A slight, direct nod can say more than a speech. (Naomi's nod after Ruth's oath.)
- **Show what carrying a family costs.** A protective parent's hardships belong on screen: the swallowed pride, the risks taken, what it does to them. (Elimelech in Moab: holding Mahlon back, the short measure, working through the fever.)
- **Leave some gaps open.** Not every relationship needs filling in. A gap can be room to explore later, in this story or another. (Orpah and Chilion's marriage stays one line through a wall, by your choice.)
- **Let the body speak.** A gesture often replaces a line. (The neighbor's single chuckle and his wave at the dead fields; Naomi's hand sweeping across the ground and up to the sky; her holding back tears.)

## Peoples and cultures

- **No people is all good or all bad.** Where history gives a people reason for hostility or coldness, show it, alongside the kind and curious among them. (Moab's suspicion and its old grudge, a hard employer, a sincere mother, a girl who brings bread.)
- **When cultures differ, let characters notice.** Let them ask and explain the way people really would, briefly and in character. (The boys ask why the Moabites cut themselves in mourning; Naomi answers in a sentence.)
- **Check the custom before staging it.** For example: would they really mourn an outsider the same way as one of their own? (No, so nobody comes to Elimelech's burial except Ruth.)

## Dialogue

- **It must sound real, not scripted.** People interrupt, trail off, joke at the wrong moment and don't say everything they mean.
- **Don't say what we can already see or know.** Cut the line that restates the obvious ("It was empty yesterday"; the neighbor's "Nobody's paying") or reports what we've just watched (the foreman retelling Ruth's request).
- **Fewer vocatives.** Most of the "my daughter(s)" go. Use a name, or nothing. Keep one where it carries weight ("Go back, my daughters"; "Wait, my daughter").
- **Names where people would say them.** "Orpah has gone back," not "your sister-in-law has gone back."
- **No written-sounding openers.** Lines don't start with "Look," and the like.
- **Relay speech indirectly.** When one character passes on what another said, she says it in her own words ("He even told me to stay close to his young men"), not as a quote inside a quote.
- **Plain, present, everyday wording.** "You comfort me," not "You've comforted me." "God be with you," not "God go with you." "Wait," not "Sit still." "How it plays out," not "how it falls out." Fragments are fine: "Hardly sat down in the shelter." "Naomi?"
- **Trust the audience, but not with plot words.** Keep explanations short ("That man is family"). When the plot hangs on a word, though, it still needs one clear beat ("One of our redeemers").
- **Avoid tidy sentiment.** Confirmed in your words: Mahlon's deathbed song was "too neat... too perfect and 'disney-movie ish.'" A beautiful moment like that "can and should be able to" happen, just not where it feels arranged. What replaced it is small and real: a cup he can't hold, and a joke about his brother.
- **The right kind of song for the moment.** A work song belongs in a field; a baby gets a lullaby. If a song is used, it has to be the kind people would actually sing there. (Naomi sings Obed a lullaby, not the harvest song.)
- **Once we've seen it, the line can let it go.** Your 1:15, "You know you should go back. Go after her," drops "to her people and her gods" because we've just watched Orpah walk home with the goddess. My reading of the change, to confirm with you. The rule I'd draw: a recorded phrase can move from the dialogue into the picture, as long as the picture really carries it.
- **Speak to what the other person already knows.** "You know you should go back" persuades by appealing to Ruth's own judgment, not by arguing. It sounds more like a real person than a reason does.
- **When the text is ambiguous, use the most likely reading.** Note the others, but don't hedge in the dialogue. (Your instruction on 1:13.)
- **Every scene should flow into the next.** Look at the transition as well as the scene.
- **God's name is used sparingly.** Characters say "God" or "the Lord." "Yahweh" is kept for oaths and for places where the Name itself is the point (ROADMAP §5.5).
- **English always stands for Israel's own language.** Sister dialects such as Moabite are accents. Everything else is spoken as itself, by your three tiers (ROADMAP §5.7).
