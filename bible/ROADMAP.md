## 0. The short version

**What we're making.** A dramatization of the whole biblical narrative, one arc at a time. It stays faithful to the text and uses modern dialogue. The text comes first: research, then treatments, then screenplays. 3D animation comes later, once the stories are written and locked.

**The core idea: modern words, ancient minds.** Characters speak the way people speak now, so the audience hears them as people rather than statues. But they *think* like people of their own time. Their fears, loyalties, beliefs and knowledge belong to their world, not ours. Most weak Bible adaptations fail on one side or the other. Either the words are archaic, which makes the story feel distant, or the characters have modern minds under their robes, which makes it false.

**The core mechanism: provenance.** Every element of a scene carries a label saying where it came from: Scripture, history, tradition, scholarly inference, your own conviction, or invention. Every line of dialogue also carries a tier saying how much freedom we took with it. The labels live in the working files and don't appear in the finished product. They let us add and reframe freely without losing track of what the Bible actually says.

**How we start:**

1. ~~You answer the foundational questions.~~ Done; see [`canon/FOUNDATIONS.md`](./canon/FOUNDATIONS.md). A few follow-up questions remain in §19.
2. We settle the language approach (§5.7) and how to handle God's name in Old Testament dialogue (§5.5). Ruth needs both.
3. I turn the rules in this roadmap into standalone working documents: principles, a language guide, a lexicon and a positions register.
4. We write the **Ruth** pilot all the way through (§11): a research dossier, a treatment, scene cards, a script, your rewrite and an audit. Ruth doesn't depend on the big discussions about how God speaks or about Jesus, so it can go ahead while those conversations continue.
5. We update the rules with what the pilot taught us. Then we lay out the full series and start the first long arc.

**Two tracks run alongside the writing the whole time:**

- **Discussions** ([`discussions/`](./discussions/)): your framings and the theological questions, taken as they come up. Each one is settled before any arc that depends on it.
- **The extra-texts study track** (§9.5): learning what the Ethiopian canon, the Apocrypha and related texts actually contain, so you can decide what, if anything, to use.

3D production (§15) starts only after the pilot script is locked, and it starts with an *animatic* (storyboards timed to rough audio), not with 3D models.

## 1. What we're making

### 1.1 The product

- A **multi-part dramatized series** covering the biblical narrative from creation to Revelation. It is organized into arcs such as Joseph, the Exodus, David and the Gospels, and each arc stands on its own.
- It is written first as **screenplays**. A **series bible** (world, characters, timeline) and a **research dossier** for every passage support the scripts.
- It is eventually produced as a **fully rendered 3D animated series**.
- **Its purpose is an accurate and powerful expression of Christianity, as you understand it.** It is a creative project, not a historical one. It stays bound to the texts, but not to convention. It doesn't need to match any denomination's framework.
- **Its audience, for now, is you.** The test is whether you think it's right, true and good. There's no rating to aim at.

### 1.2 What it is not

| Not this | Because |
|---|---|
| A new Bible translation or paraphrase | We dramatize the text; we don't replace it. What the text records someone saying, they still say (§4.2). |
| A modern-day retelling | The *language* is modern; the setting and the characters' minds are ancient. A modern-parallel version could be its own project, but it never gets mixed into this one. |
| A sermon series | Theology comes through the story and through the words the text records. It is never delivered in speeches we invent (§3, principle 9). |
| A "hidden history" or a debunking | The story is told the way the text tells it: miracles happen, God acts, and prophecy comes true. Some interpretations will be unconventional and will "expand the view" beyond the Bible, but the spirit stays the same. |
| A historical documentary | It's a creative work. Historical evidence informs it and the texts bind it, but the creative judgment is yours. |
| A one-year project | The Bible has 66 books. We build it one arc at a time and prove the method on a pilot first. |

## 2. Why modern dialogue is the right instinct

### 2.1 The Bible was written in the language of the people who heard it

Your instinct isn't a departure from the Bible. It's closer to how the Bible was originally written and heard than archaic English is.

- **The New Testament is written in Koine Greek.** *Koine* means "common." It was the everyday trade language of the eastern Mediterranean, not the literary Greek of the educated elite.
- **Jesus taught in Aramaic, the everyday spoken language,** using images from the farm, the kitchen and the market: seed, yeast, lost coins, day laborers and unpaid debts.
- **William Tyndale was the first to translate the New Testament into English from the Greek.** He said his aim was that "a boy that driveth the plough" would know the Scriptures. Being in the common language was the whole point.
- **The King James Version's "thee" and "thou" were already going out of everyday use in 1611.** The translators kept them partly because they distinguish singular "you" from plural "you," as Hebrew and Greek do. Archaic English isn't what the originals felt like. It's what a 400-year-old translation feels like.
- **The Bible updates details for its own readers.** In Mark 2:4, the friends of a paralyzed man "dug through" a roof of mud and branches. In Luke 5:19, they lower him "through the tiles." Many commentators think Luke adjusted the detail for Greco-Roman readers who knew tiled roofs.
- **The Bible also stops to explain things.** Mark pauses to explain Jewish washing customs to Gentile readers (Mark 7:3–4). The narrator of Ruth explains a custom that had already died out: "Now this was the custom in former times in Israel…" (Ruth 4:7).

### 2.2 But the Bible's register is not casual everywhere

- Hebrew narrative is terse and restrained, and the narrator rarely comments.
- Poetry is elevated even in the original. That includes the Psalms, most of the prophets and Job.
- Speech varies. God's speech, oracles, blessings and prayers are formal. Everyday exchanges are plainer.

So "modern" doesn't mean "casual everywhere." It means **matching the register of the original in today's English.** Where the original is plain, we're plain. Where it's lofty, we're lofty too, in modern elevated English rather than King James English.

### 2.3 Precedents

| Production | Approach | What to take | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| *The Visual Bible: Matthew* (1993), *The Gospel of John* (2003) | Each narrates a modern translation word for word | They prove the text alone can carry a film | They leave no room for implied action or connecting scenes, so they can feel like illustrated readings |
| *The Ten Commandments* (1956) | Epic scale with elevated, archaic-flavored dialogue | Scale and a sense of awe | The distance: the "museum piece" failure described in §2.4 |
| *The Prince of Egypt* (1998) | Animated. Opens with a disclaimer about artistic license and invents freely around the core story | Animation can carry real emotional weight and spectacle, and the disclaimer is honest about the license taken | Its central invention, Moses and Rameses raised as brothers, reshapes what the story means |
| *The Passion of the Christ* (2004) | Subtitled dialogue in Aramaic, Hebrew and Latin | Historical texture | Prolonged graphic violence, and non-biblical material drawn from later devotional visions |
| *The Chosen* (2019– ) | Modern conversational dialogue, extensive invented backstory, crowdfunded | Proof that modern dialogue makes these people vivid to a huge audience | The sheer amount of invention, and modern sensibilities leaking in |
| *The King of Kings* (2025) | Animated life of Jesus, framed as Charles Dickens telling the story to his son | A recent animated Gospel, and a precedent for frame stories (§10.2) | — |
| *Noah* (2014) | A heavily reimagined Genesis 6–9 | — | Shows how far a story drifts when the additions carry it instead of the text |
| BibleProject (2014– ) | Short animated explainers of biblical books and themes | Excellent on literary design and on how the whole Bible fits together | It's an explainer format, not drama |

### 2.4 Three ways to fail

| Failure | What it looks like | What causes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Museum piece** | Thee and thou, stiff speeches, everyone solemn all the time. The audience admires it but doesn't feel it. | Mistaking faithfulness to the original's form for faithfulness to its meaning |
| **Costume drama** | Characters with modern psychology, values and humor, dressed in robes. "Follow your heart," therapy vocabulary, 21st-century assumptions about family and gender. | Modernizing the characters' minds instead of their words |
| **Sermon** | Characters explaining theology they couldn't know yet, and every conversation turning into a lesson | Writing from hindsight instead of from inside the moment |

The target is **living history**: the ancient world seen from the inside. It should feel familiar enough to connect with and strange enough to be true.

## 3. The accuracy contract

There are eleven principles. Everything else in this document expands on one of them.

1. **Scripture is the spine, and framing is where our freedom lies.** Nothing we write contradicts the biblical text as we establish it in §9. How we interpret, frame and expand on the text is open, after discussion. The aim is the spirit and nature of the story, not reproducing every word. Some details can flex the way the Bible itself adapts details for its readers (§2.1). What a text actually says cannot be overridden.
2. **Everything has a provenance.** Every significant element is labeled by its source (§4.1), and every line of dialogue by its tier (§4.2). If we can't say where something came from, it doesn't go in.
3. **Every addition passes the addition test** (§4.3). It must be consistent, plausible, purposeful, subordinate and recognizable. "Purposeful" means it grows out of the context, the evidence, or the spirit of the story. Nothing is ever added just to get more theology in.
4. **The recorded words are the default.** What the text records someone saying, they say, in faithful modern English. Changing the substance of a recorded line happens only after we've discussed it, and the change gets logged. That applies above all to God's words and Jesus's. You've said you never want to add to God's word, and this is how we keep that promise while leaving room for framing.
5. **Modern words, ancient minds.** The wording is contemporary. The characters' knowledge, values, motives and worldview belong to their own time (§5).
6. **Make it land the way it landed.** For every scene we ask what the original audience knew and felt, then work out how to produce the same effect in a modern audience (§7).
7. **Tradition is not text.** Popular images such as the apple, the three kings, or Mary Magdalene as a prostitute are checked against Scripture. We use them only on purpose, and we label them when we do (§6).
8. **Full intensity when the story calls for it.** The Bible holds violence, pain, grief, beauty, love and desire at full strength, and so can we. The ceiling is high for gore, pain, beauty and pleasure. Intensity is a tool, not a default. It's used when the story calls for it and matched to what the text is doing (§7.4). The firm limit: no explicit sexual content. Desire and intimacy can be powerful on screen without being explicit, as the Song of Songs shows.
9. **Show, don't preach.** Theology comes through events and through the teaching the text itself records. Invented dialogue never exists just to deliver doctrine.
10. **This is your project, and decisions get written down.** It expresses your understanding. I gather information, check it and challenge it; you decide. When your framing and the text seem to pull apart, I'll say so, with the passages, and we'll talk it through before anything is built on it. Decisions are recorded with their reasoning in [`canon/FOUNDATIONS.md`](./canon/FOUNDATIONS.md) and the Positions Register (§8.2), so the series stays consistent over years of writing.
11. **Explore before deciding.** Unconventional framings are welcome. Each one gets a fair hearing, a check against every relevant text (the fit check, §8.8), the strongest case against it as well as for it, and a recorded decision.

## 4. The provenance system

This is where your freedom to add things lives, along with the safeguards that keep additions honest. It follows the same instinct as the [Primordia plan](../../primordia/IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md): uncertainty is recorded as data, not waved away with a disclaimer.

### 4.1 Content provenance: where did this come from?

| Level | Source | Example | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Scripture | Four men dig through a roof to lower their paralyzed friend to Jesus (Mark 2:1–12) | Never contradicted. Cite the book, chapter and verse. |
| **P2** | History and archaeology | Houses in Capernaum were built of black basalt, with roofs of beams, branches and packed mud | Use freely and cite the source. |
| **P3** | Ancient tradition | Peter was crucified upside down (Origen, as preserved by Eusebius) | Label it and use it on purpose. Never stage it as if it were Scripture. |
| **P4** | Scholarly inference | The house in Mark 2 belongs to Simon Peter (inferred from Mark 1:29 and 2:1) | Record the reasoning and who holds the view. |
| **P5** | Your conviction | Something you believe is true or missing, without outside evidence | Record it and your reason. It must pass the addition test. |
| **P6** | Invention | Simon muttering "That's my roof." | It must pass the addition test. |

P5 is where "only if I think it's true" lives. "Missing from the current Bible" can mean several different things, and each has a different place in the system:

| What kind of "missing" | Example | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Text that survives in some ancient manuscripts but not in most Bibles | The oldest Hebrew manuscript of Samuel (from the Dead Sea Scrolls) has a paragraph before 1 Samuel 11 explaining that Nahash the Ammonite king had been gouging out his subjects' right eyes. Josephus knew it too, and the NRSV includes it. | A text-critical decision (§9). If accepted, it becomes P1. |
| Books the Bible mentions that are now lost | The Book of Jashar (Josh 10:13; 2 Sam 1:18), the Book of the Wars of the LORD (Num 21:14), an earlier letter from Paul to Corinth (1 Cor 5:9), and a letter from Laodicea (Col 4:16) | We don't know what they said. We can show them *existing* (a scroll, a scribe) but we can't quote them. |
| Books in some canons but not others | Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Sirach and Wisdom are in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. 1 Enoch is in the Ethiopian canon and is quoted in Jude 14–15. | The canon decision (§9). Otherwise they count as P3. |
| Gaps the text says nothing about | Jesus between the ages of 12 and 30; Israel's centuries in Egypt; Moses's forty years in Midian; Paul's time in Arabia (Gal 1:17) | P4 to P6, and each addition passes the addition test |
| Something you're convinced the text implies | — | P5 |

### 4.2 Dialogue tiers: how much freedom did we take?

| Tier | What it covers | How much freedom | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — Locked** | Words the text records from God or Jesus, from angels delivering a message, and from prophets speaking an oracle | Faithful modern English by default, with nothing added or removed inside the quotation. Staging, pauses, delivery and the lines *around* it are free. Changing its substance requires a discussion and a logged decision. | "Son, your sins are forgiven." (Mark 2:5) |
| **B — Recorded** | Words the text records from anyone else | Modern wording, with the same meaning, the same claims and the same order. A line can be split, interrupted or trimmed of repetition as long as nothing is lost. | The scribes' objection (Mark 2:7) |
| **C — Implied** | Speech the text says happened but doesn't record ("he taught them many things," "they argued among themselves") | Invented, but consistent with the text's summary and with the surrounding passage | What Jesus was teaching before the roof opened (Mark 2:2: "he was speaking the word to them") |
| **D — Invented** | New speech in new or expanded moments | Must pass the addition test and must not carry doctrine | "That's my roof." |

Three refinements:

- **Iconic lines.** Some lines are so familiar that a fresh rendering distracts the audience. Examples are "Let there be light," "It is finished" and "Father, forgive them." **Keep the familiar wording when it's accurate and not archaic. Modernize it when the familiar form is archaic or misleading.** The KJV's "Suffer the little children to come unto me" only confuses a modern listener, because "suffer" meant "allow." It becomes "Let the little children come to me."
- **Parallel passages.** When two or more Gospels record the same saying in different words, a Tier A line follows one Gospel's wording. We don't blend phrases from different Gospels into one sentence unless that decision is recorded in `HARMONY.md` (§9.4). Also, most of Jesus's words reach us as Greek translations of what he said in Aramaic. Tier A means faithful to the Greek text we have. The few Aramaic words the Gospels preserve are the exception (§5.7).
- **Invented lines for Jesus and for God.** *Decided:* case by case, with no new sermons or teachings. An added line has to make sense in its context, with evidence or the spirit of the story behind it, and is never there just to add theology. Expect Jesus to have real Tier D dialogue: ordinary talk, humor, friendship, fear, tenderness, and toughness. The open question is how far his humanity goes, and that's [Discussion 002](./discussions/002-jesus-humanity.md).

### 4.3 The addition test

Every P5 or P6 element and every Tier C or D line has to pass all five questions:

1. **Consistent.** Does it avoid contradicting Scripture and the Positions Register?
2. **Plausible.** Could it have happened, historically and culturally? (Check it against P2 sources.)
3. **Purposeful.** Does it grow out of the context, the evidence, or the spirit of the story? Does it serve the passage by clarifying something, setting something up, or helping the audience feel what the text intends? Decoration fails. So does anything added just to get more theology in, and anything that pulls against the text's message.
4. **Subordinate.** Does the text's own moment stay the climax? An invented subplot must never outshine the event the Bible records.
5. **Recognizable.** Would someone who knows the passage well watch the scene and say "yes, that's Mark 2," rather than "where did that come from?"

**Tier mix.** Every episode reports what share of its lines are Tier A, B, C and D, and how many P5 and P6 elements it contains. There's no hard cap. But the numbers are always visible, so invention can't creep up without anyone noticing.

### 4.4 Where the labels live

- **In scripts:** as Fountain notes such as `[[A: Mark 2:5]]`. Fountain is a plain-text screenplay format (§13.5), and its notes don't appear in the rendered screenplay.
- **In scene cards and character files:** as a provenance field for each element.
- **In the finished product:** nowhere, unless you later want an optional viewer companion that shows what comes from the text.

### 4.5 Is dramatizing "adding to Scripture"?

Deuteronomy 4:2, Proverbs 30:6 and Revelation 22:18–19 warn against adding to God's words, and that deserves to be taken seriously. This plan rests on a distinction:

- **Adding to Scripture** means presenting new material as God's word, or changing what the text says.
- **Dramatizing** means imagining around the text in a way everyone knows is imagination.

The provenance labels, the locked Tier A lines and the addition test exist to keep us on the dramatizing side of that line. People have been doing this for a very long time, sometimes well and sometimes badly:

- **Jewish midrash** filled gaps in the biblical story imaginatively for centuries, while keeping a clear line between the text and commentary on it. One well-known midrash has the young Abraham smashing his father's idols (Genesis Rabbah 38:13).
- **Tatian's *Diatessaron*** (around AD 170) wove the four Gospels into one continuous narrative. It was the first Gospel "harmony," and in a sense the first screenplay.
- **The *Infancy Gospel of Thomas*** (2nd century) is the cautionary tale. It filled Jesus's silent childhood with a boy who curses a playmate to death and brings clay sparrows to life. The early church rejected it, partly because it wasn't historical, but also because the Jesus it invented wasn't the Jesus of the Gospels. An addition that changes who a character *is* fails the test, however vivid it is.

We'll probably come back to this question more than once. It belongs in `discussions/`.

## 5. Language rules

### 5.1 Two questions for every line

The translation theorist Eugene Nida distinguished **formal equivalence**, which reproduces the original's form (word order, idioms, grammar), from **dynamic equivalence**, later called **functional equivalence**, which reproduces the original's effect on the reader. Our dialogue aims for functional equivalence, within the limits set in §4.2. We ask two questions of every line:

1. **What did it mean?** Nothing may be lost or added.
2. **How did it land?** Was it warm, rude, funny, shocking, formal or intimate? We aim for the same effect in modern English.

### 5.2 The dial

How far along the scale from archaic to colloquial should the dialogue sit? Rather than debate that in the abstract, we'll test it. Below is Naomi urging her widowed daughters-in-law to go home (Ruth 1:11–13) at four settings.

**Setting 0 — Museum piece** (King James Version, 1611)
> Turn again, my daughters: why will ye go with me? are there yet any more sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands? Turn again, my daughters, go your way; for I am too old to have an husband. If I should say, I have hope, if I should have an husband also to night, and should also bear sons; would ye tarry for them till they were grown? would ye stay for them from having husbands? nay, my daughters; for it grieveth me much for your sakes that the hand of the LORD is gone out against me.

**Setting 1 — Close modern**
> Go back, my daughters. Why would you come with me? Do I still have sons in my womb who could become your husbands? Go back, my daughters, go. I am too old to have a husband. Even if I thought there was hope for me — even if I had a husband tonight and bore sons — would you wait for them to grow up? Would you keep yourselves from marrying? No, my daughters. It is more bitter for me than for you, because the hand of the LORD has gone out against me.

**Setting 2 — Plain modern**
> Go home. Both of you. Why would you come with me? Am I going to have more sons for you to marry? Go home, my girls. I'm too old for a husband. And even if I weren't — say there was still hope — say I married tonight and had sons — would you wait until they were grown? Stay alone all those years? No. No, my daughters. This is more bitter for me than it is for you. The LORD has raised his hand against me.

**Setting 3 — Too far**
> Girls, seriously, go home. What's the plan here — I pop out a couple more sons and you marry them? I'm way past that. Even if I hooked up with someone tonight, you'd wait twenty years? Come on. God's got it out for me, and I'm not dragging you down with me.

Setting 3 fails in instructive ways:

- "Hooked up" brings in modern sexual slang and changes the meaning. Naomi talks about *marrying*.
- "God's got it out for me" turns a lament that uses God's covenant name into a shrug.
- "Seriously," "what's the plan here" and "way past that" are 2020s phrasing that will sound dated in ten years.

It is funny, and Naomi really is being bitterly sardonic in the original. But it's the wrong kind of funny.

**Decided: setting 2 is the baseline.** Lines can go more colloquial, even into slang, when a character or moment calls for it, and they lift toward setting 1 for prayer, oracle and lament (§5.3). Setting 3 stays here as a reference for what failure looks like.

### 5.3 Register

- **Baseline:** plain, contemporary English that will last: words that sounded normal thirty years ago and will still sound normal thirty years from now. No current slang ("vibe," "lowkey," "literally" as an intensifier) and no memes.
- **Vary it by speaker.** A Galilean fisherman, a Jerusalem scribe, a Roman officer, a palace official and a shepherd boy should be distinguishable with your eyes closed. Each character or group gets entries in the voices document (§16).
- **Vary it by moment.** Prayer, blessing, lament, prophecy, song and formal speech all go up a level, into modern elevated English and never King James English. Market talk, family arguments and jokes come down a level.
- **Follow each book's voice.** Mark is fast, blunt and breathless; "immediately" is his favorite word. Luke is polished. John is simple on the surface and deep underneath. Hebrew narrative is terse. One option worth considering: let the adaptation of each book carry a little of its author's style.

### 5.4 The anachronism checklist

These are the "many little rules," sorted into seven categories from easiest to catch to hardest.

#### 1. Sacred names used as swear words

Your example, "Jesus Christ!", fails in three ways at once:

- It breaks the commandment against misusing God's name (Exod 20:7). Every observant character would be shocked by it.
- "Christ" is a title meaning "Messiah" or "Anointed One," not a surname.
- The exclamation only exists in a culture shaped by Christianity, which doesn't exist yet.

The same goes for "Oh my God," "God damn it," "for God's sake," "Holy —," "Hell!" and "damn."

The ancient world had its own swearing, though, and it gives us period-true substitutes:

| Ancient form | Where it appears | Use it where a modern character would say… |
|---|---|---|
| "As the LORD lives…" | 1 Sam 14:39; Ruth 3:13 | "I swear —" (a solemn vow) |
| "May the LORD do so to me, and more also, if…" | Ruth 1:17; 1 Sam 3:17 | The strongest possible oath |
| Swearing by heaven, by the earth, by Jerusalem, by the Temple, or by its gold | Matt 5:34–35; 23:16–22 | Everyday oaths in the New Testament era, which is exactly the habit Jesus criticizes |
| "Far be it from me!" / "May it never be!" (Greek *mē genoito*) | Gen 18:25; Rom 6:2 | "No way!" or "Absolutely not!" (The KJV renders Rom 6:2 as "God forbid," adding a "God" that isn't in the Greek.) |
| "Woe is me" / "Woe to you…" | Isa 6:5; Matt 23 | Dismay, cursing, denunciation |
| Tearing one's clothes, putting dust on one's head | Gen 37:34; Josh 7:6 | Grief and horror shown visually, often stronger than any word |

#### 2. Words that don't exist yet, or that mean something else

Whether a word is forbidden, allowed or even required can depend on *who* says it and *when*. "Jesus Christ" is the clearest example:

- **As an exclamation:** never, by anyone.
- **As a name, before the resurrection:** no. People call him Jesus (Yeshua), Rabbi, Teacher, "the Nazarene," "the carpenter's son" or "Son of David." Whether he is the Messiah is the question the Gospels are built around, so nobody uses the answer as his name.
- **As a confession of faith, after Pentecost:** yes. Peter uses it in his first sermon (Acts 2:38).

More terms that depend on era and context:

| Term | Not before | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Christian | Acts 11:26 (Antioch) | The term was first used there, possibly by outsiders |
| church | Pentecost (Acts 2) | Before then, *ekklēsia* just means "assembly." Use "the assembly," "the brothers" or "the Way" (Acts 9:2). |
| Jew / Jewish | Late in the monarchy, and the exile | The word comes from "Judah." Earlier, use "Israelites," or "Hebrews," which appears mostly in the mouths of foreigners (Gen 39:14; 1 Sam 4:6). In John's Gospel, *hoi Ioudaioi* often means the Judean authorities specifically, and dialogue should make clear who is meant. |
| Bible, Old Testament, New Testament, "Gospel" as the name of a book | — | These are later terms. In the New Testament era, people said "the Scriptures," "the Law and the Prophets," or "Moses and the Prophets." |
| Trinity, sacrament, rapture, "born again" (before John 3), "accept Jesus into your heart" | — | Later theology and later vocabulary |
| Sunday, Saturday, Easter, Christmas | — | Use "the first day of the week," "the Sabbath," "Passover" and "the day of Preparation" |
| Satan, as a proper name, in the early Old Testament | — | In Job 1–2 it is *the satan*, "the accuser," with a definite article. How that figure relates to the later Satan is a question for the Positions Register (§8.2). |
| Palestine | — | Politically loaded, and the Roman province of that name dates from after AD 135. Use Canaan, Israel, Judah, Judea, Galilee or Samaria as the era requires. |

#### 3. Idioms that come from the Bible itself

Hundreds of English idioms were born in Scripture. The linguist David Crystal counts more than 250 from the King James Version alone. That means nobody can say one before the moment that coins it. **The rule: no idiom before its origin.** The scene where an idiom originates can plant it quietly for the audience to catch.

#### 4. Technology, time and measurement

- **Machines:** no "recharge," "wired," "on the same wavelength," "blow off steam," "full steam ahead," "like clockwork," "hit the brakes," "on my radar," "reboot," "trigger," "bite the bullet" or "fast-track." Nothing involving engines, electricity, guns or screens.
- **Sports:** no "ballpark," "slam dunk," "drop the ball," "curveball" or "touch base." The exception is Paul's world, which had Greek athletics (races, boxing, the victor's wreath), and he uses them in his letters (1 Cor 9:24–27).
- **Time:** there are no minutes, seconds or o'clocks.
  - Daylight is divided into twelve hours. "The third hour" is about 9 a.m. (Mark 15:25).
  - Night is divided into watches, such as "the fourth watch" (Mark 6:48).
  - Days don't have names. People say "the first day of the week."
  - Years are counted by reigns: "in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar" (Luke 3:1).
- **Distance:** cubits, "a day's journey," "a Sabbath day's journey" (Acts 1:12). Roman miles only in Roman settings (Matt 5:41).
- **Money:** shekels, talents and minas. In the New Testament, also the denarius, a day's wage (Matt 20:2), and the lepton, the widow's coin (Mark 12:42).

#### 5. Therapy-speak and modern psychology

This is probably the most common failure in modern-dialogue adaptations. Characters "process trauma," "set boundaries," need "closure," feel "anxious," "validated" or "triggered," and call someone "toxic" or "a narcissist." The emotions are real and universal, but the vocabulary is from the last fifty years.

The Bible's own emotional vocabulary is physical and vivid. Use it, translated into plain modern English:

| Biblical idiom | Modern rendering |
|---|---|
| "His face fell" (Gen 4:5) | He looked crushed, or sullen |
| "My bones wasted away" (Ps 32:3) | I was sick with guilt |
| "Stiff-necked" (Exod 32:9) | Stubborn; won't be told |
| "Hardened his heart" (Exod 7:13) | Refused to be moved |
| Greek *splanchnizomai*, literally "moved in the gut" (Matt 9:36; Luke 15:20) | Overcome with compassion |
| "Gird up your loins" (1 Kings 18:46; Job 38:3), literally tuck your robe into your belt | Get ready; brace yourself |
| "The LORD make his face shine on you" (Num 6:25–26) | Look on you with favor |

In Hebrew, the "heart" means the mind and the will as much as feelings, and the "kidneys" and "bowels" carry emotion. When a modern idiom relies on an anachronistic image, drop the image and keep the force of the line, or find an image from the period.

#### 6. Modern minds: values, motives and assumptions

This is the hardest category, because it isn't a list of words. It's about what characters *want* and why. Check these defaults in every scene:

| Modern default | Ancient default |
|---|---|
| The individual, self-expression, "be true to yourself" | The family, the clan and the people; honor and shame; loyalty to the covenant |
| Marrying for romance | Marriages arranged by families, with love expected to follow. Isaac married Rebekah, "and he loved her," in that order (Gen 24:67). |
| Privacy | Almost none. Rooms and courtyards were shared, and the village knew everything. |
| Most people can read | Most people couldn't. Commonly cited estimates for Roman-era Judea and Galilee run from about 3% to 10%. Scripture was *heard*. |
| Night is lit | Night is dark: oil lamps, fires and the moon. Most people went to sleep at dusk. |
| Religion is a private opinion | Religion is public, national and all-encompassing. Every meal, harvest, war and birth has a God-ward side. |
| Slavery means the Atlantic slave trade | Ancient slavery took several forms, including debt servitude, war captives and household slaves, and it differed from the Atlantic system in important ways without being benign. Portray it honestly and on its own terms. |
| Time is money, and progress runs in a straight line | Time moves in seasons, festivals and generations |

None of this means characters can't have inner lives we recognize. It means their inner lives are shaped by their world. Ruth's loyalty to Naomi is astonishing precisely *because* it cuts against every family and ethnic obligation she has.

### 5.5 How people referred to God

This changes from era to era. It's a small detail that makes each era sound different.

| Era | Usage |
|---|---|
| The patriarchs | *El Shaddai* ("God Almighty"), *El Elyon* ("God Most High"), "the God of my father" (Gen 17:1; 14:18–22; 31:5). Exodus 6:3 raises the question of whether the patriarchs knew the name YHWH at all, which goes in the Positions Register. |
| From Moses through the monarchy | The personal name YHWH is spoken freely, in oaths, blessings and names (Eli-*jah*, *Jeho*-shaphat). |
| Second Temple and New Testament era | People avoid saying the Name out of reverence and use substitutes instead: "Heaven" (possibly why Matthew says "kingdom of heaven"), *Adonai* ("my Lord") and "the Holy One." At Jesus's trial, the high priest says "the Blessed One" and Jesus says "the Power" (Mark 14:61–62). |

**Your decision:** "the LORD" by default, and "Yahweh" where someone would actually have said the Name.

**A correction to one assumption.** You guessed that most people wouldn't have spoken the Name. That's true of Jesus's time, but not of the Old Testament period:

- **Before the exile, the Name was in everyday use.**
  - Personal names were built on it: Elijah ("my God is Yah"), Isaiah ("Yah saves"), Jeremiah, Hezekiah, Jehoshaphat.
  - Oaths used it: "as YHWH lives."
  - So did ordinary greetings. In Ruth 2:4, Boaz greets his harvesters with "YHWH be with you!" and they answer, "YHWH bless you!"
  - Letters found at Lachish, written around 588 BC, open with "May YHWH cause my lord to hear news of peace."
- **In the Second Temple period, reverence turned into avoidance.**
  - The Septuagint translates the Name as *Kyrios* ("Lord").
  - Some Dead Sea Scrolls write it in the old Hebrew script, setting it apart from the surrounding text.
  - The Mishnah describes the high priest pronouncing it in the Temple on the Day of Atonement, with the people bowing when they heard it (Yoma 6:2). It condemns anyone who pronounces it "according to its letters" (Sanhedrin 10:1).
- **The pronunciation is reconstructed.** "Yahweh" is a scholarly reconstruction based on early Greek transcriptions. It's widely accepted but not certain. "Jehovah" is a later hybrid: the consonants of YHWH with the vowels of *Adonai*.

So your rule makes "Yahweh" common in Old Testament dialogue and nearly absent in the New Testament era, apart from the high priest on the Day of Atonement. That gives the two eras an audible difference, which I like.

**Still open (§19):** do we apply the rule fully, so that Boaz really says "Yahweh be with you"? Or do we keep "the LORD" as the default and save "Yahweh" for chosen moments? The pilot needs the answer.

### 5.6 Names

- **Jesus or Yeshua?** "Jesus" comes through the Greek *Iēsous* from the Hebrew and Aramaic *Yeshua*, a form of Joshua. "Yeshua" signals authenticity; "Jesus" signals recognition. **Recommendation:** "Jesus" in dialogue for clarity, with his family perhaps saying "Yeshua" in intimate moments if you like the effect. Whatever we choose, we choose it once and apply it everywhere.
- **Hidden identities.** James is really *Jacob* (the Greek is *Iakōbos*). Judas is *Judah*, a proud, patriotic name before it became a byword, and Jude is also Judah. It's worth surfacing these where it helps the audience hear the Old Testament echo.
- **Too many Simons and Marys.** Simon and Mary (Miriam) were among the most common names in Jewish Palestine at the time. That's why the Gospels use tags: Simon Peter, Simon the Zealot, Simon of Cyrene, Simon the Pharisee, Simon the leper; Mary Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, Mary the wife of Clopas. Characters should use those tags in dialogue the same way.
- **Names that mean something.** Many names carry puns the original audience heard instantly:
  - Isaac means "he laughs" (Gen 17:17; 18:12; 21:6).
  - Jacob is close to "heel-grabber" (Gen 25:26; 27:36).
  - Nabal means "fool" (1 Sam 25:25).
  - Naomi means "pleasant," and she says, "Call me Mara," meaning "bitter" (Ruth 1:20).

  When the text plays on a name, the dialogue should let the audience in on the joke.

### 5.7 Languages and accents

**Decided: we use the original languages, not only English.** How far to take that is still open (§19). There are four basic approaches:

| Approach | How it works | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. English, with original words kept** | All dialogue is in English, and the Aramaic words the Gospels preserve stay in Aramaic | Fully keeps the "modern speech" goal; easiest to write, revise and voice | The least texture |
| **B. Original languages throughout, with subtitles** | Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, Latin and the rest are spoken aloud, with modern-English subtitles (the *Passion of the Christ* approach) | The most authentic and immersive. The subtitles can still be written in setting-2 English. | The audience hears foreign speech, so the modern-voice connection moves from the ear to the eye. Every revision needs translating, casting gets harder, and some languages are partly reconstructed. |
| **C. Language follows point of view** | The group we're inside speaks English. Outsiders speak their own language, with or without subtitles. In the Joseph arc, the Hebrews speak English and the Egyptians speak Egyptian until Joseph's world becomes Egyptian. In the Gospels, Jesus's circle speaks English, Roman soldiers speak Latin, and perhaps Pilate questions Jesus in Greek. | Keeps the intimacy of modern speech for the people we're inside, and turns language into a dramatic tool | Needs consistent rules |
| **D. Transition** | A scene opens in the original language and then dissolves into English | Signals authenticity once and then gets out of the way | It's a device, and it feels gimmicky if overused |

**Recommendation:** C as the backbone, keeping A's preserved words, with D used sparingly. This keeps your core idea (people sounding like people) and still puts real ancient language on screen where it does dramatic work.

**The Bible itself uses language to drive the plot:**

- **Gen 31:47.** Laban names a heap of witness stones in Aramaic (*Jegar-sahadutha*), and Jacob names the same heap in Hebrew (*Galeed*).
- **Gen 42:23.** Joseph speaks to his brothers through an interpreter, so they don't know he understands every word they say.
- **Judg 12:6.** The men of Gilead identify Ephraimites by how they pronounce "shibboleth."
- **2 Kings 18:26–28.** Hezekiah's officials beg the Assyrian envoy to speak Aramaic, not "the language of Judah," so the people on the wall won't understand. He deliberately shouts at them in Hebrew.
- **Neh 13:24.** Nehemiah finds children who can't speak the language of Judah.
- **Dan 2:4.** The Chaldeans answer the king "in Aramaic," and the book itself switches into Aramaic at that point and stays there through chapter 7.
- **John 19:20.** The sign on the cross is in Hebrew, Latin and Greek.
- **Acts 2:4–11.** At Pentecost, everyone hears in their own language.
- **Acts 21:37–22:2.** A Roman officer is surprised that Paul speaks Greek, and a hostile crowd goes quiet when Paul switches to their own language.

**Who spoke what:**

| Era | Israelites | Others |
|---|---|---|
| The patriarchs | An early West Semitic language, ancestral to Hebrew. Laban's family speaks Aramaic (Gen 31:47). | Egyptian; Canaanite and Amorite dialects |
| Egypt and the Exodus | The same ancestral language | Egyptian |
| Judges to the monarchy | Classical Hebrew, with regional accents (Judg 12:6) | **Moabite was very close to Hebrew** (it's known from the Mesha Stele), so in Bethlehem Ruth might be understood easily but marked by her accent. Also Phoenician and Aramaic. The Philistines' original language is largely unknown. |
| Assyria and Babylon | Hebrew, with Aramaic for diplomacy | Akkadian; Aramaic as the empire-wide common language |
| Persia | Hebrew and Aramaic | Old Persian; Imperial Aramaic |
| Jesus's world | Galilean Aramaic day to day; Hebrew for Scripture and worship; Greek for trade and government | Greek, and Latin for Roman administration and the army |

**Translation workflow.** You write or revise every line in English, and that English is the master. For any non-English line, I provide:

1. the line in the original script and in transliteration;
2. a **literal back-translation**, so you can see what it really says;
3. notes on the choices I made;
4. a confidence level:
   - **High:** Biblical Hebrew, Koine Greek and Latin, all of which are well documented.
   - **Medium:** Aramaic. First-century Galilean Aramaic has to be reconstructed from the Dead Sea Scrolls, inscriptions and later Aramaic translations of Scripture (the Targums). Scholars disagree on vocabulary and especially pronunciation.
   - **Low:** Egyptian, Akkadian and Old Persian, whose pronunciation is largely reconstructed. Philistine is essentially unknown.

Before production, a specialist checks everything. *The Passion of the Christ* used a Jesuit scholar, William Fulco, for its Aramaic and Latin. In the script, the spoken line is written in transliteration and the English goes in a subtitle note:

Whether Boaz says the Name aloud here is exactly the open question from §5.5.

**More on languages and accents:**

- **Keep the Aramaic the Gospels keep.** The Gospel writers preserved a handful of Jesus's words in the original Aramaic. These are moments the eyewitnesses remembered in his own voice: *Talitha koum* (Mark 5:41), *Ephphatha* (Mark 7:34), *Abba* (Mark 14:36) and *Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani* (Mark 15:34). Others include *Rabboni* (John 20:16), *Corban* (Mark 7:11), *Raca* (Matt 5:22) and *Maranatha* (1 Cor 16:22). We keep them in Aramaic, followed by a translation where it's needed, just as the Gospels themselves do.
- **Accents are in the text.** Peter's Galilean accent gives him away in the high priest's courtyard (Matt 26:73). The men of Gilead kill Ephraimites who can't pronounce "shibboleth" (Judg 12:6). The voice cast will need an accent strategy that can carry both moments.

### 5.8 Emotion, insults, humor and crude language

**The Bible is not prudish, and a cleaned-up version isn't a more accurate one.**

- **Insults** in the text land hard: "brood of vipers" (Matt 3:7), "whitewashed tombs" (Matt 23:27), "you fool" (which Matt 5:22 warns about), and Saul's contemptuous attack on Jonathan's mother (1 Sam 20:30). We find the same force in modern English without modern profanity.
- **Crude language** is there too.
  - Paul calls his former achievements *skubala*, "dung," a deliberately vulgar word (Phil 3:8).
  - He says of the people insisting on circumcision, in effect, "I wish they'd go all the way and castrate themselves" (Gal 5:12).
  - Isaiah's "filthy rags" were menstrual cloths (Isa 64:6).

  Where the original shocks, our version should shock too, within the limits of principle 8.
- **Humor** is everywhere once archaic English stops hiding it. Modern dialogue is how the audience gets these jokes, which is a strong argument for your whole approach.
  - Elijah taunts the prophets of Baal that their god might be asleep, or traveling, or — in a word many commentators read as a bathroom joke — "busy" (1 Kings 18:27).
  - Balaam loses an argument to his donkey (Num 22:28–30).
  - Jesus talks about a camel squeezing through the eye of a needle, and a man with a log in his eye (Matt 19:24; 7:3–5).
  - Eutychus dozes off during Paul's marathon sermon and falls out of a window (Acts 20:9).
  - Rhoda is so excited that Peter is at the gate that she forgets to let him in (Acts 12:13–16).
  - In Ruth, the narrator refuses to name the nearer kinsman. Boaz calls him, in effect, "Mr. So-and-so" (*peloni almoni*, Ruth 4:1). The man who wouldn't preserve a dead man's name doesn't get his own name preserved.

### 5.9 Jesus's voice

This is the hardest voice to write, and the one the audience knows best.

1. **His recorded words are always Tier A** (§4.2).
2. **He is never flat.** He is:
   - warm: "Jesus looked at him and loved him" (Mark 10:21);
   - sharp: "Get behind me, Satan" (Mark 8:33);
   - ironic: he calls Nathanael "an Israelite in whom there is no deceit" moments after Nathanael has bluntly insulted his hometown (John 1:46–47);
   - weary (John 4:6), grieving (John 11:35) and angry (Mark 3:5);
   - playful: the camel and the needle, the log and the speck.
3. **He asks far more questions than he answers.** By one count, the Gospels record him asking more than three hundred. Let him ask them.
4. **He speaks in images, not abstractions.** If an invented line of his sounds like a theology textbook, it's wrong.
5. **He is a first-century Galilean Jewish teacher.** He quotes and alludes to the Scriptures constantly, argues like a rabbi ("Have you not read…?", Matt 12:3–5), and speaks from inside Israel's story.

All of this becomes a living file, `style/LEXICON.md`. Because the rules depend on context, each entry records more than the word itself:

## 6. Tradition is not text

The biggest accuracy trap isn't the Bible itself. It's our cultural memory of the Bible, built up from paintings, carols and films. Getting these details right is a chance to be *more* accurate than almost every adaptation that came before.

This table is also the clearest evidence for your sense that common framings have drifted over time. Every row is a case where centuries of retelling replaced what the text actually says. Some of your unconventional framings may turn out to be corrections of this kind. The fit check (§8.8) is how we'll tell those apart from framings that pull against the text.

| Popular image | What the text actually says |
|---|---|
| Eve eats an apple | She eats the "fruit" of "the tree of the knowledge of good and evil" (Gen 3:3–6). The apple probably comes from a Latin pun: *malum* means both "evil" and "apple." |
| Eve tempts Adam while he's somewhere else | Adam "was with her" (Gen 3:6). |
| Two of every animal | Seven pairs of each clean animal and bird, and two of the rest (Gen 7:2–3). |
| Moses has horns | Michelangelo's statue follows the Latin Vulgate's *cornuta* for the Hebrew *qaran*. The text says his face "shone" (Exod 34:29). |
| Delilah cuts Samson's hair | She calls in a man to shave it (Judg 16:19). |
| Jonah and the whale | "A great fish" (Jonah 1:17). |
| Lucifer, the fallen angel, in Isaiah | Isa 14:12 addresses the king of Babylon as "shining one, son of the dawn," and "Lucifer" is the Vulgate's Latin for it. Whether the verse also points to Satan is a Positions question. |
| Mary rides a donkey to Bethlehem | No donkey is mentioned. |
| Jesus is born in a stable because the inn was full | There was no room in the *kataluma*, the same word Luke uses for the "guest room" of the Last Supper (Luke 2:7; 22:11). Kenneth Bailey's reading is that a family home's guest room was full, and the baby was laid in a manger on the lower level of the house, where animals were kept at night. And "while they were there" (Luke 2:6) doesn't require that the birth happened on the night they arrived. |
| Three kings at the manger | They are Magi ("wise men"), and their number isn't given; "three" comes from the three gifts. They find the child in a "house" (Matt 2:11), possibly long after the birth (Matt 2:16). |
| Mary Magdalene, the reformed prostitute | The text never says this. Jesus had cast seven demons out of her (Luke 8:2). The prostitute identification comes from a sermon Pope Gregory I preached in 591, which merged several different women into one. |
| Paul is thrown from his horse | No horse is mentioned (Acts 9:3–4). |
| Saul is renamed Paul at his conversion | He is "Saul, who was also called Paul" (Acts 13:9): a Jew with a Roman name, who goes by the Roman one in Gentile settings. |
| A fair-skinned, European Jesus with a halo | He was a first-century Galilean Jew: Semitic features, very likely dark hair, a beard, olive-to-brown skin, and average height for his time (about 5'5"). Isaiah 53:2: "he had no beauty that we should desire him." |
| Angels as serene humans with wings | When angels appear, people are terrified, which is why they keep saying "Do not be afraid." Some look like ordinary men (Gen 18:2; Dan 9:21). Cherubim and seraphim are something else entirely (Ezek 1; Isa 6). |
| A red devil with horns and a pitchfork | A serpent in Eden, an accuser in Job, a tempter in the wilderness. Never horns and a pitchfork. |

Many of these traditions are harmless, and some are beautiful. The rule isn't "never use them." It's **know when you're using them** (label them P3), and never let them override the text.

## 7. Making it land

### 7.1 What they knew that we don't

Every scene card has a field for this. It is the heart of making a scene hit the way it originally hit.

| Moment | What the first audience felt | What a modern audience misses |
|---|---|---|
| Jesus says, "Your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5) | Either blasphemy or a claim to be God, with no third option. Forgiveness belonged to God, through the Temple. | It sounds like a kind sentiment. |
| "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58) | The divine name from Exodus 3:14. They pick up stones to kill him. | It sounds like odd grammar. |
| "The Son of Man" | The heavenly figure of Daniel 7:13–14, who is given everlasting dominion | It sounds like "a human being." |
| The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30–37) | The hero comes from the most despised group imaginable, after a priest and a Levite have walked past. | "Good Samaritan" now just means a helpful person. |
| Jesus eats with tax collectors | Tax collectors were collaborators who got rich squeezing their neighbors on Rome's behalf. Sharing a meal meant accepting them. | "Tax collector" sounds like a dull job. |
| Jesus touches a leper (Mark 1:40–42) | Ritual contamination, and the leper's social exile, which might now pass to him | Simple kindness |
| Palm branches and "Hosanna!" (John 12:13) | Palms had been a symbol of national liberation since the Maccabees (1 Macc 13:51), and *hosanna* means "save us!" It's a crowd greeting a king they hope will liberate them. | A nice parade |
| A king riding a donkey (Zech 9:9) | A deliberate act fulfilling prophecy, and the crowd knew the verse | Humility, at most |
| Crucifixion | The most shameful death in the Roman world, reserved for slaves and rebels. Cicero wrote that the very word "cross" should be kept far from a Roman citizen's thoughts, eyes and ears. | A religious symbol worn on a necklace |
| Women discover the empty tomb | A woman's testimony counted for little in court, so nobody inventing the story would have picked women as the first witnesses. | Nothing remarkable |
| "The gospel" (*euangelion*) | A word used for imperial announcements. An inscription from 9 BC calls the birthday of the emperor Augustus the beginning of the "good news" for the world. | A purely religious word |

### 7.2 How we get that knowledge across

- **Setup and payoff.** Show Samaritans being spat at three episodes before the parable. Show a crucifixion by the roadside long before the Passion. Then the key moment needs no explanation.
- **Reaction.** The fastest way to tell a modern audience that a line is shocking is to show a room full of people being shocked by it (Mark 2:6–7; John 8:59).
- **Staging and design.** Where people sit, who touches whom, who eats with whom, and who stands up when someone enters.
- **Explanation that has a reason to happen.** Someone who genuinely wouldn't know, such as a child, a foreigner or a Roman, asks. Never write "As you know…" dialogue between two people who both already know.
- **The text's own explanations.** Where the Bible stops to explain something (Mark 7:3–4; Ruth 4:7; John 4:9, "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans"), a narrator or a character may explain it too.

### 7.3 What we know that they didn't

This is the opposite discipline: **characters know only what they could have known at that point in the story.**

- Abraham has never heard of the Law of Moses.
- Eve doesn't know the word "Satan."
- Job never learns about the conversation in heaven (Job 1–2), and the whole book depends on that.
- The disciples don't understand Jesus's predictions of his death: "they did not understand, and were afraid to ask him" (Mark 9:32). Before Pentecost, they must not talk like Paul.
- John the Baptist, in prison, wonders whether Jesus really is the one (Matt 11:2–3).
- Old Testament characters speak of *Sheol*, not of heaven and hell in New Testament terms.

Dramatic irony is fine. The *audience* can know what's coming, but the characters can't.

### 7.4 Functional equivalence cuts both ways: the crucifixion

Functional equivalence doesn't always mean "softer." Mark narrates the crucifixion itself in four words: "And they crucified him" (Mark 15:24). That restraint worked because every first-century listener had *seen* a crucifixion, so the word did the work. A modern audience hasn't. So showing more than the text describes can be the *faithful* choice, while lingering on it for its own sake is not. The question is always what reaction the text expected, and what it takes to produce that reaction now. This will come up with every violent passage.

### 7.5 Miracles

**No less supernatural than the text, and no more spectacular.**

- The Red Sea is driven back by "a strong east wind all night" (Exod 14:21). The wind and the night are in the text, so we show them.
- A blind man is healed in two stages, and at first sees "people like trees, walking" (Mark 8:24). Don't smooth that into a single flash.
- On Mount Carmel, fire falls from heaven and consumes even the water and the stones (1 Kings 18:38). The text is spectacular here, so we are too.
- **God's voice sounds different from scene to scene, and each scene takes its cue from its own text:**
  - To the boy Samuel, it sounds like the priest Eli calling from the next room (1 Sam 3:4–8).
  - To Elijah, after wind, earthquake and fire, it is "a sound of thin silence" (1 Kings 19:12).
  - At Sinai it is terrifying thunder (Exod 19:16–19; 20:18–19).
  - In John 12:28–29, some people hear thunder and others hear an angel.

### 8.1 How I'll handle these conversations

### 8.3 Depicting God, the Spirit, angels and Satan

These are the options to decide in the register:

- **God:** voice only; or voice plus the natural phenomena the text names (fire, cloud, storm, light). A human-like figure appears only where the text itself describes one (Gen 18; Ezek 1:26–28; Dan 7:9, the "Ancient of Days"), and even then perhaps at a distance or veiled.
- **The Holy Spirit:** as the text describes, "in bodily form like a dove" (Luke 3:22), and as wind and "tongues as of fire" (Acts 2:2–3).
- **Jesus:** fully human on screen (§8.4).
- **Angels and Satan:** see the table in §6.

### 8.4 Fully God and fully man

*Status: open. See [Discussion 002](./discussions/002-jesus-humanity.md). Your answer to Q7 makes his humanity the center of the project, and that's well supported by the text. The word "fallible" needs working through.*

The Council of Chalcedon (451) set out what most Christians hold: Jesus is one person, fully God and fully human. Screen portrayals tend to fall off one side or the other:

- **Too divine.** An ethereal Jesus who floats, glows, never laughs, and always seems to be half-listening to heaven. The ancient name for denying his real humanity is *docetism*. The Gospels' Jesus gets tired, hungry and angry. He sleeps through a storm (Mark 4:38), weeps (John 11:35), grows in wisdom (Luke 2:52) and is tempted (Heb 4:15). This is the failure your vision of Jesus is built to avoid.
- **Too ordinary.** A wise, kind, merely human teacher who occasionally performs a miracle. The Gospels' Jesus forgives sins, accepts worship (Matt 14:33; John 20:28), calms the sea with a word, and claims God's name as his own.

**What the texts settle**

- He is fully human. He feels compassion, anger, grief, joy and anguish. He is afraid in Gethsemane (Mark 14:33–36; Heb 5:7). He doesn't know everything (Mark 13:32). He grows and learns (Luke 2:52; Heb 5:8). He is genuinely tempted (Heb 4:15).
- He is without sin (Heb 4:15; 2 Cor 5:21; 1 Pet 2:22; 1 John 3:5).

**What they leave open, and Discussion 002 takes up**

- How limited his knowledge was, and when.
- Whether he could be mistaken about non-moral things.
- What his being challenged and responding means in passages like Mark 7:24–30.
- How his inner life looked.

The v1 rule that he is "never wrong" went further than the texts do, so it's withdrawn until that discussion is done.

### 8.5 Hard passages

The Bible contains passages that are hard to read, let alone stage: the conquest and *herem* (the "devoting" of cities to destruction), Jephthah's daughter, the Levite's concubine (Judg 19), the bears and the boys (2 Kings 2:23–24), Uzzah, Ananias and Sapphira, the psalms that call down curses, and the sexual violence against Dinah and Tamar. Here's the guidance:

- **Don't soften them, and don't apologize for them inside the story.** Adding a character who voices modern disapproval is the costume-drama failure.
- **Narrating something is not endorsing it.** Much of Judges shows Israel collapsing: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 21:25). Where the text signals judgment, through outcome, irony or structure, the staging should carry that signal. Where the text withholds judgment, we don't invent one.
- **Do the wrestling off-screen.** The theological discussion happens in `discussions/`. It shapes the staging, not the dialogue.

### 8.6 Heroes and villains

The Bible is unsparing about its heroes:

- Abraham twice passes his wife off as his sister (Gen 12, 20).
- Jacob is a con man.
- Moses kills a man.
- David commits adultery and arranges a murder.
- Peter denies Jesus three times.

Don't clean them up. Equally, don't flatten the antagonists beyond what the text supports. The Gospels' Pilate is reluctant, while the ancient historians Philo and Josephus describe a brutal governor. Luke 13:1 mentions Galileans "whose blood Pilate had mingled with their sacrifices." Both pictures are true, and together they make a more interesting man.

### 8.7 The Passion and the Jewish people

For centuries, the Passion narratives were misused to blame "the Jews" as a whole for Jesus's death, with terrible results. Accuracy is the remedy for this, not the problem:

- Jesus, his mother, all twelve disciples and the first church were Jews.
- The conflict in the Gospels is an argument *within* Israel.
- The crowd at the trial is one crowd, not a whole people.
- Rome carried out the crucifixion.
- Christian theology itself holds that the sins of all humanity put him on the cross.

The Catholic Church's *Nostra Aetate* (1965) states that his death cannot be charged against all the Jews living then, or against Jews today. A careful adaptation can make all of this visible without changing a word of the text.

### 8.8 Your framings and the fit check

You have unconventional framings of certain ideas and passages, and you want to explore them through real discussion rather than either adopting or dismissing them. Each one goes through the same steps:

1. **State it.** You describe the framing, in a sentence or a page, and say which passages it touches.
2. **Gather the texts.** I collect every passage that bears on it: the ones that support it, the ones that pull against it, and the ones that are silent. I also check the original language and the manuscripts, because a conflict sometimes turns out to be a translation or tradition problem (§6) rather than a problem with the text itself.
3. **Present the views.** How other readers, ancient and modern, have seen it, including anyone who has proposed something similar before.
4. **Grade the fit:**

| Grade | Meaning | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **Supported** | The text points this way | Use it |
| **Compatible** | The text is silent or open, and the framing fills a gap without strain | Use it, labeled P4 or P5 |
| **Tension** | Some texts pull against it. It works only with a particular reading of them, which we name and adopt on purpose. | Discuss it; if adopted, record the reading it depends on |
| **Contradiction** | It can't stand without overriding what a text actually says | By your own rule, it doesn't go in as stated. It can still shape how we frame the surrounding material, or point to a version of the idea that does fit. |

5. **Discuss and decide.** The conversation is saved in `discussions/`, and the decision goes into the Positions Register, dated and with its reasoning.
6. **Apply it consistently.** Once adopted, a framing applies everywhere it's relevant. If it ripples into other arcs, I'll flag where.

One commitment from me, since you said you're not claiming to be right: I'll bring the strongest case *against* each framing as well as the case for it. Agreeing with you by default would make me useless to you here.

## 9. Which text, exactly?

"Accurate to the Bible" needs a precise meaning, because the manuscripts and the church traditions differ.

### 9.1 Canon

**Decided:** start with the books most widely accepted as canon. In practice that means the 66 books, which every major Christian tradition includes; the Catholic, Orthodox and Ethiopian churches add more on top of them. After that, we study the wider canons and the apocryphal texts (§9.5), and anything beyond the 66 is adopted only after discussion.

1–2 Maccabees and Josephus will likely serve as historical sources either way (P2 or P3) for the centuries between the Testaments. That period produced the Pharisees, the Sadducees, Herod, Roman rule, and the Feast of Dedication that Jesus attends (John 10:22).

### 9.2 Base text

**Old Testament.** The Masoretic Text (the standard Hebrew text) is the default. Wherever the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation) or the Dead Sea Scrolls differ in a way that affects the story, we make and record a decision:

| Passage | Masoretic Text | Other witnesses |
|---|---|---|
| Goliath's height (1 Sam 17:4) | Six cubits and a span (about 9'9") | Four cubits and a span (about 6'9") in the Septuagint, a Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel, and Josephus |
| Genealogies (Gen 5, 11) | — | The Septuagint adds well over a thousand years in total to the ages at which the fathers have their sons |
| Jacob's family entering Egypt (Exod 1:5) | 70 people | 75 in the Septuagint and a Dead Sea Scroll. Stephen follows this figure in Acts 7:14. |
| Nahash (end of 1 Sam 10) | Not present | A paragraph explaining Nahash's cruelty, in a Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel and in Josephus |

**New Testament.** A modern critical text (Nestle-Aland 28 / UBS 5) is the default. We make explicit decisions on the passages most readers don't know are disputed: Mark 16:9–20, John 7:53–8:11, and Luke 22:43–44 (sweat like drops of blood).

### 9.3 The translation behind locked lines

Tier A and Tier B lines need a base rendering. There are three options:

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Our own rendering, checked against three or four major translations and the original languages | Tuned for speaking aloud; no licensing issues; every choice documented | More work (I can draft it and you review) |
| A public-domain modern translation: the Berean Standard Bible (BSB, public domain since 2023) or the World English Bible (WEB) | Fast, citable and free to use | Written for reading, not for speaking |
| A copyrighted translation (NIV, ESV, NLT, CSB) | Familiar to audiences | Quotation limits apply, and a produced film would need permission |

**Recommendation:** our own rendering, with the BSB as the working reference text. The Mark 2 lines in §12 were done this way.

### 9.4 Harmony and chronology

The four Gospels overlap. Samuel–Kings overlaps with Chronicles. The prophets have to be placed within particular kings' reigns. All of this needs one consistent timeline, and `canon/HARMONY.md` records each ordering decision with its reason. The standard tools are Kurt Aland's *Synopsis of the Four Gospels* and Edwin Thiele's chronology of the kings.

Harmony decisions reach all the way down into production. For example, the sign on the cross reads differently in each Gospel:

| Gospel | Wording |
|---|---|
| Mark 15:26 | "The King of the Jews" |
| Matthew 27:37 | "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews" |
| Luke 23:38 | "This is the King of the Jews" |
| John 19:19–20 | "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews," written in Hebrew, Latin and Greek |

A 3D model of the cross has to show one sign. The usual reading treats the shorter versions as abbreviations of John's fuller one, but it is still a decision to record.

### 9.5 Texts beyond the canon: the study track

You want to learn what lies outside the canon before deciding whether to use any of it. "Missing from the current Bible," in your words, means anything outside the Bible we could potentially use.

**Where the New Testament itself points.** The best place to start is the traditions the biblical writers themselves drew on, because their use is a form of endorsement, of a kind we can talk about. The New Testament borrows from beyond the Old Testament more often than most readers realize:

| Passage | What it draws on |
|---|---|
| Jude 14–15 | Quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 directly, introduced as something "Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied" |
| Jude 9 | The archangel Michael disputing with the devil over Moses's body. Origen said this came from a book about Moses's death (the *Assumption* or *Testament of Moses*). |
| 2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6 | Angels who sinned, held in chains in darkness, matching 1 Enoch's account of the Watchers (1 Enoch 6–16) |
| 2 Tim 3:8 | Names Pharaoh's magicians "Jannes and Jambres." Exodus never names them; Jewish tradition does. |
| 1 Cor 10:4 | "The spiritual rock that followed them," echoing a Jewish tradition that a well traveled with Israel in the wilderness |
| Heb 11:37 | "They were sawn in two," most likely Isaiah's martyrdom as told in Jewish tradition |
| Acts 7:22–23 | Moses "instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians," and forty years old when he went out to his people. Neither detail is in Exodus. |
| Gal 3:19; Acts 7:53; Heb 2:2 | The Law delivered through angels, a Jewish tradition that Exodus doesn't state |

**The texts to study, roughly from closest to the canon to farthest:**

1. **The deuterocanon** (in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles): Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and additions to Esther and Daniel.
2. **Further Orthodox books:** 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh and Psalm 151.
3. **The Ethiopian canon:** among others, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and three books called Meqabyan that are different from the Maccabees. Fragments of both Enoch and Jubilees were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, so they were being read in Judea before Jesus.
4. **Other Jewish writings from before and around Jesus's time:** the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, the *Life of Adam and Eve*, the *Apocalypse of Abraham*, and the Dead Sea Scrolls' own writings, such as the *Genesis Apocryphon* and the *War Scroll*.
5. **Jewish interpretation and history:** Josephus, Philo, the Targums, the Mishnah, and the midrash.
6. **The earliest Christian writings outside the New Testament:** the *Didache*, 1 Clement, the *Shepherd of Hermas* and the *Epistle of Barnabas*. The last two were bound into the 4th-century Codex Sinaiticus alongside the New Testament.
7. **Gospels and Acts outside the New Testament:**
   - the *Gospel of Thomas*;
   - the *Protoevangelium of James* (the source of the names Joachim and Anne for Mary's parents);
   - the *Infancy Gospel of Thomas*;
   - the *Gospel of Peter*;
   - the *Acts of Pilate*;
   - the *Acts of Paul and Thecla*.
8. **Gnostic texts:** the Nag Hammadi library and the *Gospel of Judas*. Worth understanding, but many teach that the material world is evil or was made by a lesser god, which sits hard against Genesis's "very good."

**One brief per text or group,** each covering:

- what it is, when and where it was written, and who treats it as Scripture;
- what's in it;
- how its theology fits the canon;
- the passages relevant to our arcs;
- my honest assessment of its weight.

Then we discuss it. Anything adopted is labeled P3 and must pass the fit check (§8.8) against the canon.

### 10.2 How to structure it

### 10.5 Reframing tools

You mentioned reframing certain elements in different contexts. Here are the tools for it, plus one guardrail:

- **Point of view.** Tell a familiar event through a minor character's eyes: the servant girl at the fire, Malchus, whose ear Peter cut off (John 18:10), Simon of Cyrene, the centurion at the cross, or an Egyptian living through the plagues.
- **Intercutting.** The Bible does this itself. Genesis 38 (Judah and Tamar) interrupts the Joseph story right after his brothers sell him. As Robert Alter famously showed, the same Hebrew phrase, "please recognize," stitches the two stories together (Gen 37:32; 38:25): Judah, who helped deceive his father, is deceived the same way.
- **Non-linear structure.** Flashbacks, flash-forwards and parallel timelines.
- **Framing devices** (§10.2).
- **Placing non-narrative texts inside scenes** (§10.4).

**Guardrail:** reframing changes *how we see* what happened. It never changes *what happened*.

*Clarified:* by "reframing" you mainly meant different interpretations and understandings of a passage. Those go through the fit check (§8.8). The structural tools above stay available for later.

## 11. Where to start

### 11.1 Why not start with Genesis 1?

Chronological order is the right order for *watching*, not necessarily for *writing*. Genesis 1–11 is the most interpretively contested material in the Bible (Positions 4–7, 14 and 16). Starting there means making the hardest decisions before the method has been tested on anything. So we write a pilot first. The finished series can still open with Genesis.

### 11.2 What the pilot has to do

- Test the language rules on a lot of dialogue.
- Use every provenance level.
- Require real cultural research.
- Be small enough to finish completely, including an animatic.
- Carry little theological risk, so that we're calibrating craft rather than settling controversies.
- Serve the larger story.

### 11.3 Candidates

| Candidate | Size | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ruth** | 4 chapters, 85 verses | Well over half its verses are dialogue. Rich customs to research: gleaning, the kinsman-redeemer, the sandal ceremony, the threshing floor. God never speaks and works no miracles, so it tests providence working through ordinary events. Small cast and few locations. It leads to David (Ruth 4:17) and to Jesus (Matt 1:5). | Quiet, with no spectacle |
| Jonah | 4 chapters | Satire, comedy, a storm and a fish. It tests the humor rules. | God speaks, which forces decisions about depicting him early |
| Joseph | 14 chapters | The most novel-like narrative in the Old Testament, with family drama and big emotions | Too big for a first pass |
| Mark 2:1–12 (a single scene) | 12 verses | Tests Jesus's voice and Tier A | One scene is too small to test structure |
| David and Goliath | 1 chapter (1 Sam 17) | Iconic and dramatic | Needs Saul's and David's backstory, plus the textual question about Goliath's height |
| Esther | 10 chapters | Palace intrigue and God's hiddenness | Bigger cast; the Persian court needs designing |

### 11.4 Why Ruth, and why not just because it's short

*Decided: Ruth is the pilot.*

Being short helps, because it means we'll actually finish it. But that's the smallest reason:

1. **It's mostly dialogue,** so every language rule gets tested.
2. **It doesn't depend on the two biggest open discussions.** God never speaks in Ruth and Jesus doesn't appear, so we can write it while Discussions 001 and 002 continue.
3. **It's a test case for your own theology.** You believe God speaks through the world and the people we meet, and Ruth is the book where God acts almost entirely through people.
   - The narrator credits God directly only twice: for giving the people food (1:6) and for giving Ruth a child (4:13). Everything else happens through human kindness, choices and "chance": Ruth just "happened" to come to Boaz's field (2:3).
   - Boaz blesses Ruth in the name of the LORD, "under whose wings you have come to take refuge" (2:12). Later Ruth asks Boaz to spread his "wing" over her (3:9). It's the same Hebrew word, *kanaph*, here meaning the corner of his garment. Boaz becomes the answer to his own prayer.
4. **It's about an outsider.** Ruth is a Moabite, from a nation Israel's law kept out of the assembly (Deut 23:3), whose god was Chemosh. She chooses Israel's God ("your God my God," 1:16) and becomes David's great-grandmother and an ancestor of Jesus. That's the Bible's own opening for your interest in how God works beyond one people's borders (Positions Register, item 23).
5. **It treats lament as part of faith.** Naomi openly accuses God: "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (1:20–21). The book never rebukes her. That makes it a good first test of the honesty and intensity you want.
6. **It's a live language test.** Ruth may speak with a Moabite accent in Bethlehem (§5.7), and the greeting in 2:4 puts God's name into everyday speech (§5.5).
7. **It's small enough to make.** Few characters and few locations mean a manageable animatic.

**The rest of the plan:**

- **Mark 2:1–12** as a companion exercise, to calibrate Jesus's voice once Discussion 002 has made some progress. §12 is a first draft.
- **Joseph** as the first full arc.

A bonus for production: the hill country around Bethlehem, with its terraces, fields and threshing floors, is the setting of Ruth, of David's youth and of the Nativity. The landscape built for the pilot can be reused across the series, with the architecture updated for each era.

## 12. A worked example

Here is a first draft of Mark 2:1–12, showing the provenance system and the language rules working together. It demonstrates the method; it isn't a proposal for final dialogue, so rewrite it freely.

### 12.1 The scene

```fountain
INT. SIMON'S HOUSE — CAPERNAUM — DAY

Wall-to-wall bodies. Black basalt walls, hot air, no room to turn. [[P2: Capernaum houses]]
Kids on shoulders. People jammed in the doorway and out into the lane. [[P1: Mark 2:2]]
Near the front, seated, a few SCRIBES. They came early. [[P1: Mark 2:6]]

JESUS is mid-teaching. [[C: Mark 2:2, "he was speaking the word to them"]]

Dust sifts onto his shoulder. He stops. Looks up.

Everyone looks up.

Daylight stabs through the ceiling. A hand. A digging stick. Clods of packed mud drop onto the crowd. [[P1: Mark 2:4, "dug through"]]

SIMON
(through his teeth)
That's my roof.
[[D / P6. Depends on P4: the house is Simon's (Mark 1:29; 2:1).]]

A mat comes down on ropes. On it, a PARALYZED MAN — thin, frightened, every eye in the room on him. Above, four faces in the hole. Sweaty. Stubborn.

Jesus looks up at the four for a long moment. Something like delight. [[C: Mark 2:5, "when Jesus saw their faith"]]

He crouches beside the man.

JESUS
Son, your sins are forgiven.
[[A: Mark 2:5]]

Silence. The wrong kind.

The scribes don't speak. They don't need to. A look passes between them. One closes his eyes, as if he's heard something obscene. [[B, staged silently: Mark 2:6–7. See §12.3.]]

Jesus turns to them.

JESUS
Why are you thinking these things in your hearts?
[[A: Mark 2:8]]

The scribe's eyes open.

JESUS (CONT'D)
Which is easier — to say to this paralyzed man, "Your sins are forgiven," or to say, "Get up, pick up your mat, and walk"?
[[A: Mark 2:9]]

Nobody answers.

JESUS (CONT'D)
But so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins —
[[A: Mark 2:10]]

He turns back to the man on the mat.

JESUS (CONT'D)
I tell you: get up, pick up your mat, and go home.
[[A: Mark 2:11]]

A beat. The man's fingers move. Then his feet. He gets up — unsteady, then not. He rolls up his mat, and the crowd that wouldn't make room for him presses back against the walls to let him through. [[P1: Mark 2:12, "went out before them all"; the crowd parting is C]]

Someone starts praising God. It spreads. [[P1: Mark 2:12]]

VOICE IN THE CROWD
We've never seen anything like this!
[[B: Mark 2:12]]
```

### 12.2 What the tags show

| Tier / level | Count | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| A | 5 lines | All of Jesus's lines, rendered from the Greek, with nothing added inside them |
| B | 2 | The scribes' objection (staged silently) and the crowd's exclamation |
| C | 3 | The teaching already in progress, Jesus's delight at the friends' faith, the crowd parting |
| D / P6 | 1 | "That's my roof." |
| P4 | 1 | The house belongs to Simon |

Some of the choices explained:

- **"Son."** The Greek is *teknon*, "child." "Son" keeps the warmth without sounding patronizing in modern English. "My child" is the alternative.
- **"That's my roof."** It passes the addition test:
  - *Consistent:* nothing in the text says otherwise.
  - *Plausible:* it's a flat mud roof, and a homeowner is watching it being dug up.
  - *Purposeful:* in three words it tells a modern audience how outrageous the friends' act is, which Mark's audience felt immediately.
  - *Subordinate:* it's over before Jesus speaks.
  - *Recognizable:* the scene is still plainly Mark 2.

  The line depends on a P4 inference. If we ever decide the house isn't Simon's, the line goes.
- **The roof itself.** Mark says they "dug through" it, meaning a flat roof of beams, branches and packed mud. Luke says they lowered him "through the tiles" (Luke 5:19), and many commentators think Luke adjusted the detail for readers who knew Greco-Roman tiled roofs. That's the Bible doing functional equivalence on itself. We follow Mark, because Mark's description matches Galilean houses.
- **A harmony note.** Luke adds that the Pharisees and teachers of the law present had come "from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem" (Luke 5:17). Including that would raise the stakes, since the religious establishment has sent observers. It's a decision for `HARMONY.md`.

### 12.3 The whisper trap

The obvious move is to have one scribe whisper to another: "That's blasphemy. Only God can forgive sins." It would play well, but it's wrong. In Mark, the scribes don't say it aloud. They think it "in their hearts," and Jesus answers what they're thinking, "perceiving in his spirit" (Mark 2:8). If they whisper, Jesus has simply overheard them, and a sign of who he is disappears from the scene without anyone noticing. This is exactly the kind of tiny choice the rules exist to catch. The fix is to stage the objection silently, or as inner voice-over, so that Jesus answering it lands the way the text intends.

### 12.4 What they knew that we don't

- **Only God forgives sins,** ordinarily through the sacrifices at the Temple in Jerusalem. A teacher from a fishing town pronouncing forgiveness in someone's living room is either blaspheming or claiming God's authority. The scribes' reaction is the correct reaction to those words. The real question is whether they're right about the man saying them.
- **"The Son of Man"** evokes the figure in Daniel 7:13–14 who is given "dominion and glory and a kingdom." The scribes would have caught it.
- **Sickness and sin were widely linked** (John 9:2). So to the crowd, "your sins are forgiven" would sound as if it addressed the cause of the paralysis.
- **Jesus's question is a trap.** Which is easier to *say*? Anyone can *say* "your sins are forgiven," because no one can check it. "Get up and walk" can be checked. He does the checkable thing to vouch for the thing that can't be checked.

## 13. The writing pipeline

### 13.1 The steps for each arc

| Step | What it produces | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Research dossier | The text (with variants), historical and cultural background, archaeology, "what they knew," scholarly views, open questions | Me, with your review |
| 2. Positions check | Decisions on any register items this arc depends on | You, after discussion |
| 3. Treatment | 3–15 pages of prose covering the arc's story, shape and themes | I draft; you revise |
| 4. Beat sheet | The structure, episode by episode | Together |
| 5. Scene cards | One card per scene (template below) | Me |
| 6. Draft script | A Fountain screenplay with every line tagged | Me |
| 7. Your pass | You rewrite whatever needs changing, probably most of the dialogue | You |
| 8. Learning from your edits | I compare my draft with your version and write down the patterns: words you cut, rhythms you prefer, how you voice each character, framings your changes imply. They go into the style documents so the next draft starts closer to your voice. Anything I can't interpret, I ask about. | Me |
| 9. Translation | Any line in another language gets translated using the workflow in §5.7 | Me; specialist review before production |
| 10. Audit | The script checker plus the checklist in §13.4, including a fit check on any new interpretation your edits introduce. I flag every issue; you decide what to do about each. | Me, plus the tool |
| 11. Lock | The script is marked as locked, and any later change is logged | You |

### 13.2 Scene card template

```markdown
# S-RUT-01-03 — Naomi tells them to go home

Passage:         Ruth 1:6–14
Era / date:      Period of the judges (~12th c. BC?) — see POSITIONS
Location:        The road from Moab toward Judah
Characters:      Naomi, Ruth, Orpah
Purpose:         What this scene must accomplish
Original impact: How the first hearers felt (sardonic grief; the absurdity of her hypothetical)
What they knew that we don't:
                 Levirate marriage (Deut 25:5–10); how vulnerable widows were;
                 hostility between Moab and Israel (Deut 23:3)
What we know that they didn't:
                 Ruth becomes David's great-grandmother. No one here may hint at it.
Additions:       [P6] Orpah hesitates before kissing Naomi goodbye.
                 Addition test: consistent ✓ plausible ✓ purposeful ✓ subordinate ✓ recognizable ✓
Tier mix:        A 0 · B 6 · C 2 · D 1
Production:      Props (bundles, water skins, walking staffs); costume (widows' garments);
                 time of day (early morning, cold)
Open questions:  Does Orpah leaving read as betrayal? The text doesn't judge her, so neither should we.
```

### 13.3 Character file template

Each character file holds:

- the character's name or names, and what they mean;
- every verse where the character appears;
- facts from Scripture (P1);
- historical context (P2);
- tradition (P3);
- inferences, with the reasoning behind them (P4);
- your convictions (P5);
- inventions (P6);
- voice notes;
- their arc and relationships;
- their appearance, with the evidence for it.

### 13.4 Audit checklist

Before a script is locked, check that:

- [ ] Every Tier A line matches our rendering of the source word for word
- [ ] Every Tier B line keeps the original meaning, claims and order
- [ ] Every P5 and P6 element and every Tier C and D line has passed the addition test, and the scene card says so
- [ ] The lexicon check is clean: no banned terms, no idiom before its origin, and era-appropriate names for God
- [ ] Nobody knows anything they couldn't have known
- [ ] Any traditional image (§6) is used on purpose and labeled
- [ ] Every "what they knew" item gets across somehow on screen
- [ ] Nothing conflicts with the Positions Register
- [ ] The tier mix is reported

### 13.5 Formats and tools

- **Markdown for everything except scripts.**
- **Fountain ([fountain.io](https://fountain.io)) for screenplays.**
  - It's plain text, works well with git, and renders to a standard screenplay PDF.
  - It supports notes `[[…]]`, which hold our tags and don't appear in the PDF.
  - It supports a "boneyard" `/* … */` for material we've cut.
  - You can edit it in Highland, Beat, Slugline or any text editor. The command-line tool `afterwriting` renders PDFs.
- **Git keeps the full history of every change,** so you can see what a scene looked like before any revision.
- **An optional script checker (Phase 2).** This would be a small program that reads the Fountain files and flags:
  - lexicon violations;
  - missing tags;
  - Tier A lines that drift from the locked rendering;
  - verse references that don't exist.

  It would also report each episode's tier mix. Automated consistency checking matters across hundreds of scripts, and it's a small job to build.

## 15. From page to screen

3D production starts after the pilot script is locked. This section is a sketch, so that the writing phase sets production up well.

### 15.1 What the writing hands to production

Scene cards record locations, props, costumes, time of day and weather, so the asset lists come straight out of the scripts. Over the course of the series this becomes a production library. An asset built for one story, such as a Galilean fishing boat, a Judean threshing floor or a Roman soldier's kit, gets reused in dozens of others.

### 15.2 The Bible hands you blueprints

Some structures come with specifications:

- **Noah's ark:** 300 × 50 × 30 cubits, with three decks, a door in the side and an opening under the roof (Gen 6:14–16).
- **The tabernacle and its furniture:** Exod 25–27 and 36–38.
- **The priestly garments:** Exod 28 and 39.
- **Solomon's Temple:** 1 Kings 6–7 and 2 Chronicles 3–4.
- **Ezekiel's visionary temple:** Ezek 40–48.
- **Herod's Temple:** described by Josephus (*Antiquities* 15; *Jewish War* 5) and the Mishnah (tractate *Middot*). The 1:50 Holyland Model of Jerusalem at the Israel Museum is a good visual reference.

The text includes costume details too: tassels on the corners of garments (Num 15:38; Matt 9:20), and a ban on Israelites wearing wool and linen woven together (Lev 19:19; Deut 22:11).

### 15.3 Visual style

| Style | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Photoreal | Historical weight | The most expensive; risks the uncanny valley; dates fastest; violence reads as graphic |
| Stylized realism (painterly look, grounded proportions) | Ages well; forgiving; distinctive; violence can be felt without gore | Needs strong art direction |
| Cartoon | Cheapest | Reads as children's content and undercuts the gravity |

**Recommendation:** stylized realism, with the final decision in Phase 6. Ethnicity and appearance follow the evidence. Israelites are Semitic, and Egyptians, Cushites (Moses's wife may have been one, Num 12:1), Philistines (of Aegean origin), Persians, Greeks and Romans each look distinct.

### 15.4 The bridge: storyboards and an animatic

Before any 3D work, the pilot becomes an **animatic**: storyboards timed to scratch voice recordings and temporary music, edited into a rough version you can watch. It's cheap, and it tests pacing and dialogue with real voices. It's the single most valuable step between script and animation, because most script problems show up at this stage, while they're still cheap to fix.

### 15.5 Tools

- **Blender:** free, and covers the complete 3D pipeline (modeling, rigging, animation and rendering).
- **Unreal Engine:** real-time rendering and fast iteration; free for small creators.
- **Motion capture:** anything from capture suits (such as Rokoko) to markerless capture from phone or video, plus stock animation libraries (such as Mixamo) for background characters.
- **AI tools:** these are changing fast. For now they're most useful for exploring concepts, previsualization and developing a look. Keeping characters consistent over long sequences and precise control remain the hard parts. We'll reassess when we reach Phase 6.
- **Local versus cloud:** writing and research work fine in cloud sessions like this one. Anything that drives Blender or renders needs Claude Code running on your own machine. [`../../primordia/SETUP_GUIDE.md`](../../primordia/SETUP_GUIDE.md) already covers that setup.

### 15.6 Sound

- **Voices:** an accent strategy (§5.7), and casting that matches the characters' origins where possible.
- **Music:** built around the instruments the Bible names: lyre (*kinnor*), harp (*nevel*), frame drum (*tof*), pipe (*halil*), ram's horn (*shofar*), cymbals and trumpets (Ps 150; 1 Sam 10:5). Nobody knows exactly what the music sounded like, so the score can draw on the instruments without being bound to any reconstruction.
- **God's voice:** decided scene by scene, from the text (§7.5).

### 15.7 Scale and money

A full animated series is a studio-scale project. The realistic path for a solo creator:

1. Finish the pilot script and the animatic yourself.
2. Produce a short proof of concept: a single scene, fully rendered.
3. Use it to recruit collaborators and raise funding. *The Chosen* funded its first season largely through crowdfunding, and faith-based audiences have shown they'll back projects they believe in.

Budget options belong in Phase 6, once we know the visual style.

## 18. Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| **Scope.** The Bible is enormous, and a project this size can stall under its own weight. | Pilot first; one arc at a time; each arc complete in itself |
| **Style drift** over years of writing | The lexicon, the voices document, the script checker, and periodically re-reading the setting-2 examples (§5.2) |
| **Theological inconsistency** between arcs | The Positions Register, with every decision dated and reasoned |
| **Invention creeping up** | Tier mix reported per episode; the addition test applied to everything |
| **Costume drama** (modern minds in robes) | The "modern minds" check (§5.4, category 6) |
| **Museum piece** (playing it too safe) | The setting-2 baseline (§5.2) and the humor list in §5.8 |
| **Tradition slipping in unnoticed** | The §6 check in every audit |
| **My mistakes.** I can get a verse reference, a date or a scholar's position wrong. | Every factual claim in a dossier carries a citation you can check; the script checker validates verse references; I'll flag anything I'm not fully confident about |
| **An echo chamber.** The project expresses your views, and I could drift into just agreeing with you. | Every framing gets the strongest case against it as well as for it (§8.8), and I'll name dissonance plainly when I see it |
| **Framing drift.** Many individual interpretive choices can add up to something inconsistent, or to something that quietly changes the spirit of the story. | The fit check, the Positions Register, and occasionally re-reading the decisions together |
| **Errors in ancient languages** | Confidence levels on every translation (§5.7); specialist review before production; Aramaic and lower-confidence languages always flagged |
| **Losing context between sessions.** I don't remember past conversations; only what's written in this folder carries over. | [`CLAUDE.md`](./CLAUDE.md), FOUNDATIONS and the discussion files are written so a new session can pick up exactly where we left off |
| **Translation copyright** | Our own rendering, with a public-domain reference text (§9.3) |
| **Unintentionally giving offense,** to Jewish viewers or to other Christian traditions | Accuracy plus charity: §8.7 and the text-first discussion approach |
| **The cost of 3D** | Stylized realism; the animatic first; a proof of concept before scaling up |

## Appendix: Glossary

- **Formal equivalence / functional (dynamic) equivalence:** translating the original's form versus translating its effect (§5.1).
- **Masoretic Text (MT):** the standard Hebrew text of the Old Testament, preserved by medieval Jewish scribes known as the Masoretes.
- **Septuagint (LXX):** the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, made in the last centuries BC. It was the Bible most New Testament writers quoted.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls (DSS):** manuscripts from about the 3rd century BC to the 1st century AD, discovered near Qumran from 1947 onward. They are the oldest surviving copies of most Old Testament books.
- **Critical text:** a scholarly reconstruction of the original text based on all the surviving manuscripts. For the New Testament, this means Nestle-Aland and UBS.
- **Deuterocanon / Apocrypha:** books in the Catholic and Orthodox Old Testament but not the Protestant one: Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Sirach, Wisdom, Baruch, and additions to Esther and Daniel.
- **Pseudepigrapha:** ancient Jewish writings attributed to biblical figures that are in no mainstream canon. (1 Enoch is canonical in the Ethiopian church.)
- **Midrash:** rabbinic interpretation of Scripture, which often fills gaps in the narrative.
- **Harmony:** a single, ordered account that combines the four Gospels, or parallel Old Testament histories.
- **Theophany / Christophany:** a visible appearance of God / of Christ before his incarnation.
- **Docetism:** the ancient view that Jesus only seemed to be human.
- **Kenosis:** Christ's "self-emptying" (Phil 2:7). It bears on what he knew and did as a human being.
- **Fountain:** a plain-text screenplay format.
- **Animatic:** storyboards timed to sound; the rough cut of an animation before any animation is done.
- **Tier / P-level:** this project's provenance labels (§4).
