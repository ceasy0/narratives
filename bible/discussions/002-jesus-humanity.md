## The question

How fully human is the Jesus on our screen? Can he be surprised, afraid, wrong, changed by others? And can he sin, then reflect on it and grow?

## Your view so far

Kind and compassionate, and "tough as nails when he needs to be." His humanity, "with all of its emotions, fears, and dreams," is one of the most important things the project shows. What makes him relatable is that he "has hardships and is human and fallible, yet still able to be introspective about his mistakes and try to be a good man." God came in Jesus "to meet us where we are, as a fallible creature, and tell us that even though we make mistakes and mess up and go through hell, it will be ok." He understands that the evil people do comes from their pain, and he "understands most that he is in no way different from anyone else, which is exactly what makes him different." (Your full words are in [FOUNDATIONS §5](../canon/FOUNDATIONS.md#5-your-vision-of-jesus).)

## Where your view and the text agree, and agree strongly

Most of your vision isn't unconventional at all. It's what the texts say, and screen adaptations usually *under*-play it.

| Your view | The texts |
|---|---|

## Where the text draws a line

The New Testament says repeatedly, and in different authors, that Jesus did not sin:

- "Tempted as we are, **yet without sin**" (Heb 4:15).
- "For our sake he made him to be sin **who knew no sin**" (2 Cor 5:21).
- "He **committed no sin**, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22).
- "In him **there is no sin**" (1 John 3:5).
- "Which one of you convicts me of sin?" (John 8:46).
- "Holy, innocent, **unstained**, separated from sinners" (Heb 7:26).

In the New Testament's logic, this isn't a side detail. It's what lets his death count for others. He can carry what isn't his own (2 Cor 5:21; 1 Pet 1:19, "a lamb without blemish"; Heb 9:14). Arguably, it's also what makes your "it will be ok" possible: the one who meets us in our mess isn't sinking in it himself.

## The word "fallible": three senses

| Sense | Example on screen | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Limited.** Doesn't know everything; can be surprised, tired, afraid; learns. | He asks who touched him and genuinely doesn't know. He's stunned by the centurion's faith. | **Supported** (Mark 5:30; 6:6; 13:32; Luke 2:52; Heb 5:8) |
| **Mistaken about non-moral things.** A wrong expectation or a misjudgment, with no wrongdoing involved. | He walks to a fig tree expecting fruit and finds none. He starts to refuse a request and is moved by the answer. | **Tension.** This is genuinely debated. Two texts are the key ones. **The fig tree** (Mark 11:13): "he went to see if he could find anything on it… for it was not the season for figs." Some read this as not knowing; others read it as a deliberate prophetic sign. **The Syrophoenician woman** (Mark 7:24–30; Matt 15:21–28): he first answers "it is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs," she answers back, and he grants her request "for this statement." Some read this as testing her; others, including some evangelical scholars, as Jesus genuinely moved by her. |
| **Morally failing.** Wrongdoing that he later regrets and repents of. | He loses his temper unjustly, or wrongs someone, then reflects and makes it right. | **Contradiction** with the passages above. By the rule you set, this can't go in as stated. |

**Why I'm flagging it now:** you described Jesus as "fallible" and "introspective about his mistakes." If "mistakes" means sense 1 or 2, it's either already in the text or open for discussion. If it means sense 3, it contradicts something the New Testament says in five or more places, and does so at the center of its theology. Better to settle this before any Gospel scene is written than to build a character and discover the conflict later.

## An old version of your question

Early Christians wrestled with something close to this. Why would a sinless man receive "a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4, 9)? Matthew records John the Baptist objecting and Jesus answering, "Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness" (Matt 3:14–15).

A Jewish-Christian gospel quoted by Jerome (*Against the Pelagians* 3.2) has Jesus's mother and brothers urge him to go and be baptized. He replies, "What sin have I committed, that I should go and be baptized by him? Unless perhaps what I have just said is a sin of ignorance."

So the tension you're feeling is ancient, and it's a candidate for the extra-texts study track too.

## A craft thought

*This is my own reading, offered for discussion.*

The power you're after is a Jesus who is like us, who struggles, and who meets us where we are. That doesn't require him to sin. Hebrews actually runs the logic the other way: he can sympathize with us *because* he was "tempted in every respect as we are" (4:15).

Picture the pull fully felt and fully shown on screen: rage at the people abusing the weak, despair, the wish to strike back, the wish to quit. "Remove this cup from me" (Mark 14:36) is a man who wants out. Then show it resisted, sometimes at enormous cost.

Audiences have seen the drama of someone falling and getting back up countless times. The drama of being pulled to the edge and *not* falling is rarer. It's also closer to what the Gospels show. And it fits your last line well: Philippians 2:6–8 presents his sameness with us as a *choice*. He "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped," and took the form of a servant.

That's one option. The point of this discussion is to find yours.

## Questions for our conversation

1. **Which sense of "fallible"** do you mean: limited, mistaken, morally failing, or a mix?
2. **A scene you picture.** Describe a moment where Jesus is "introspective about his mistakes." A concrete example will let me check its fit precisely.
3. **The cross.** In your understanding, what does the cross *do*? Your answer shapes how much his sinlessness matters to the story you're telling.
4. **"All evil comes from pain."** Is this how Jesus *sees* people, as a stance of compassion, or a claim about where evil comes from? The texts also locate evil in the heart's own desires: "from within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts" (Mark 7:21–23; James 1:14–15). Both can be true at once, but the difference changes how he talks to, say, Judas or Pilate.
5. **"No different from anyone else."** How does that sit alongside the passages where he claims a unique identity: "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30), "whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9), "no one knows the Father except the Son" (Matt 11:27)?
6. **Dreams.** You mentioned his dreams. The text is silent about his personal hopes, so this is open ground for P5 and P6 material. What do you imagine?
