I graded it Supported for the empire and proposed one condition: the Passion keeps the parts the Gospels give the chief priests, with Rome over them, and Rome's the hand that crucifies. You asked: "Is that how it actually went? If thats the case, then yes. Research it as much as you can."

## The short answer

**Yes, as far as history can tell.** Three kinds of evidence meet:

1. **Rome executed him.** Crucifixion was Rome's punishment, the charge on the cross was Rome's ("King of the Jews," a claim to rule), and every source that names a judge names Pilate: the four Gospels, Tacitus, Josephus, 1 Tim 6:13 and the creeds.
2. **The temple leadership handed him over.** All four Gospels say so, independently in places (John's account of the arrest and the hearing differs from Mark's); Paul's earliest letter says Judeans killed him (1 Thess 2:14–15, though some scholars think the passage is a later addition); and Josephus, in the part of his passage on Jesus that most scholars think is genuine, has Pilate condemn him "at the suggestion of the principal men amongst us."
3. **Rome was over them.** The high priest held office at Rome's pleasure, and his robes were kept in a Roman fortress (below). In John the council's own fear is Rome ("the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation," 11:48), and at the end the chief priests say "We have no king but Caesar" (19:15).

**Two corrections to how the story is usually told,** both from the history rather than against the text:

- **Pilate was hard, not weak.** Philo and Josephus describe a governor who provoked his subjects and killed without trial, and Luke itself remembers "the Galileans whose blood Pilate had mingled with their sacrifices" (13:1). Most historians think the Gospels' reluctant Pilate is softened, or that his hesitation was politics rather than conscience (below). Either way, he's no puppet of the priests.
- **"The Jews" didn't do it.** A small circle of the priestly aristocracy did, working with the prefect. The people of Jerusalem largely liked Jesus, which is why he was arrested at night (Mark 14:1–2; Luke 22:2); the crowd at Pilate's door was small and stirred up (Mark 15:8, 11); and crowds mourned him on the way out (Luke 23:27, 48). The council itself was divided (Luke 23:51; John 7:50–51).

So your framing and the history agree: the antagonist is a system, the empire and the men who held their places in it, not a people.

Where all four agree, the history is firmest: the arrest at night by the high priest's men; a hearing before the high priest; Pilate's question "Are you the king of the Jews?"; the sentence and the title on the cross; the soldiers. Where they differ, each Gospel's own interests show: Matthew's blood cry and hand-washing appear in Matthew only, and Luke alone has Herod.

### The rest of the New Testament

- **Acts:** Peter at Pentecost, "you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (2:23), and later "you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers" (3:17); the church's prayer, "Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel" (4:27); Paul at Antioch, "those who live in Jerusalem and their rulers... asked Pilate to have him executed" (13:27–28).
- **Paul:** "None of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (1 Cor 2:8). And 1 Thess 2:14–15, the earliest written mention (about AD 50), which names "the Jews [or Judeans] who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets." Some scholars think it was added after AD 70, since it sits awkwardly with Romans 9–11; most still read it as Paul's.
- **1 Tim 6:13:** Christ "in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession."

### Outside the New Testament

- **Tacitus**, *Annals* 15.44 (about AD 116), on Nero's Christians: "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus" (Church and Brodribb's translation). Most scholars take it as genuine. It names Rome alone.
- **Josephus**, *Antiquities* 18.63–64 (about AD 94), the *Testimonium Flavianum*. Christian copyists added to it, but the consensus since the late twentieth century is that a genuine core lies under the additions, and the line about the trial is usually counted in it: "when Pilate, at the suggestion of the principal men amongst us, had condemned him to the cross" (Whiston). Josephus elsewhere calls the Jerusalem leaders he knew "the first men." An Arabic version (in Agapius, tenth century) has only "Pilate condemned him to be crucified," but it is probably a paraphrase of Eusebius, not an independent witness (Whealey, 2008).
- **Mara bar Serapion**, a Syriac letter of some time after AD 73, asks what the Jews gained by "executing their wise king." Many scholars think it means Jesus; it's vague, and it names no Roman.
- **The Babylonian Talmud** (*Sanhedrin* 43a), centuries later and polemical, has a Yeshu "hanged on the eve of Passover." It shows what later rabbis assumed, not what happened.

### Pilate, from the other sources

- **Philo** (*Embassy to Gaius* 299–305, written soon after AD 41): Pilate hung gilded shields in Herod's palace in Jerusalem, refused to remove them, and was ordered to by Tiberius. Philo has Herod's sons threaten to expose "his corruption, and his acts of insolence, and his rapine... and his continual murders of people untried and uncondemned" (302, Yonge). Helen Bond and others note that Philo's sketch is stock rhetoric against enemies of the Law, so it's a portrait with an agenda.
- **Josephus:** the standards with Caesar's image brought into Jerusalem by night, and a crowd that bared its necks at Caesarea rather than accept them (*War* 2.169–174; *Antiquities* 18.55–59); temple money taken for an aqueduct, and the protest beaten down by disguised soldiers (*War* 2.175–177; *Antiquities* 18.60–62); a crowd of Samaritans slaughtered at Mount Gerizim, after which Vitellius, legate of Syria, sent him to Rome (*Antiquities* 18.85–89). He governed ten years, about 26 to 36/37, one of the two longest terms in the province.
- **The scholars split.** Raymond Brown, Paul Winter, S. G. F. Brandon and Bart Ehrman think the Gospels soften Pilate more with each Gospel, from Mark (Pilate and the priests in agreement) to John (Pilate handing Jesus over "to them"), as the church lived more and more under Rome and apart from the synagogue. Brian McGing, Helen Bond and Warren Carter see less contradiction: in Mark, Matthew and John Pilate is skilful and manipulates the crowd; only Luke's Pilate truly finds him innocent. N. T. Wright and Craig Evans read his hesitation as fear of a Passover riot. Some have tied his caution to the fall of Sejanus in AD 31, who may have been his patron; others doubt any link.

### The ground

- **The Pilate Stone** (Caesarea, found 1961): a dedication of a building for Tiberius by "Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judaea." It's the one monument naming him, and it gives his real title, prefect, not Tacitus's procurator.
- **The Caiaphas ossuary** (Jerusalem, 1990): an ornate bone box inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas." The identification is likely but disputed.
- **Yehohanan's heel** (Givat HaMivtar, Jerusalem, 1968): a heel bone with an iron nail still through it from the side, its bent tip still caught in the wood. It's the only crucified body of the period found in the land, and it was given a proper Jewish burial.
- **The Temple warning inscription** (found 1871): death for any foreigner who passed the barrier into the inner courts. It's the one capital case Rome seems to have left to the Temple (Titus says as much in *War* 6.124–126).
- **The Royal Stoa:** Herod's basilica along the south of the Temple court, four rows of Corinthian columns, about 160 in all (Josephus, *Antiquities* 15.410–420), the likely place of the money changers and the sellers.
- **Herod's palace** on the western hill: the prefects' Jerusalem quarters at festivals (Philo, *Embassy* 299; Josephus, *War* 2.301), and most likely where Pilate tried Jesus. The Via Dolorosa's starting point at the Antonia rests on paving that turned out to be second-century.

## The people and the powers

**Rome.** The prefect lived at Caesarea on the coast and came up to Jerusalem for the festivals with troops (*War* 2.224). At Passover a cohort stood armed on the Temple's colonnades, watching the crowd; under the prefect Cumanus (AD 48–52) one soldier's obscene gesture from there set off a riot and a crush in which, Josephus says, thousands died (*War* 2.224–227; *Antiquities* 20.106–112). The Antonia fortress stood at the Temple's north-west corner, with stairs down into its courts (Acts 21:35, 40). The soldiers themselves were most likely not Italians: the units Josephus names in Judea were raised from the gentile cities of Caesarea and Sebaste, Greek-speaking locals with no love for Jews (*Antiquities* 19.356–366, for AD 44; it's probable, not proven, for Pilate's day).

**The vestments.** The high priest's robes were kept in the Antonia, under Roman guard, and handed out for the festivals; Vitellius gave them back to the priests in 36 or 37, and in 44 the procurator Fadus tried to take them again (*Antiquities* 18.90–95; 20.6–14). For the series it's the picture of "Rome over them": the high priest could put on his robes only when Rome unlocked them.

**The high priests.** Rome had appointed and removed high priests since AD 6. Annas (Ananus son of Seth) was appointed by Quirinius and served 6 to 15; five of his sons, and his son-in-law Caiaphas, all held the office after him (John 18:13). Caiaphas was appointed by the prefect Valerius Gratus about AD 18 and kept by Pilate for his whole term: eighteen years, the longest of the century, which most historians read as a working partnership. Vitellius removed him in 36 or 37, about the time Pilate went (*Antiquities* 18.33–35, 95). They and the priestly families were Sadducees, rich, and, in the words of the American Catholic bishops' guidelines for Passion plays, "collaborators with Rome" in the eyes of the people.

**Their reasoning,** in John's account of the council: "If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation," and Caiaphas's answer, "it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish" (11:48, 50). That's a man managing an empire's threat, which is also how most historians reconstruct it (below). The series already reads the line as God speaking through someone who doesn't know it (ARCHITECTURE, hour 173).

**Could they have executed him themselves?** Probably not. "It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death," they tell Pilate (John 18:31). The first prefect came "with power over life and death" (*War* 2.117); the Jerusalem Talmud says capital cases were taken from Israel forty years before the Temple fell (y. *Sanhedrin* 1:1; 7:2); and when the high priest Ananus, Annas's son, had James the brother of Jesus stoned in AD 62 while the province was between governors, the new governor wrote to him in anger and the king removed him (*Antiquities* 20.197–203). The Temple warning stone is the exception that proves the rule.

**The night hearing.** The Mishnah's rules (no capital trials at night or on the eve of a festival, *Sanhedrin* 4:1) were written down about AD 200 and reflect Pharisaic and rabbinic ideals, not the Sadducean high priest's practice in 33, so they don't show the Gospels wrong. Still, John's version, a questioning before Annas and Caiaphas rather than a full trial, is the one most historians find likeliest, and the bishops' guidelines advise "extreme caution" with a formal Sanhedrin trial on stage.

**Herod Antipas,** who had killed John the Baptist (Mark 6:14–29; Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.116–119), and who wanted Jesus dead (Luke 13:31), gets him for an hour in Luke and sends him back in a bright robe; "Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that very day" (23:12).

**The council wasn't of one mind.** Joseph of Arimathea, "a respected member of the council," "had not consented to their decision and action" (Mark 15:43; Luke 23:50–51). Nicodemus spoke for a fair hearing (John 7:50–51) and helped bury him (19:39). Later the Pharisee Gamaliel saved the apostles (Acts 5:34–39).

**The Pharisees** argue with Jesus all through the Gospels, but they're nearly absent from his death: they're with the arresting officers in John 18:3 and ask Pilate for a guard on the tomb in Matt 27:62. (The bishops' guidelines say they're mentioned only once in the Passion; that's not quite right, but the point stands.) Pharisees warned him of Herod (Luke 13:31).

**The crowd.** The leaders "feared the people" (Luke 22:2) and wanted him taken "not during the feast, lest there be an uproar" (Mark 14:2). Before Pilate it was a crowd that "came up" to the palace (Mark 15:8), which the chief priests stirred up (15:11): probably a few dozen, early in the morning, many of them the priests' own people. The people of Jerusalem as a whole weren't there, and Luke has a great crowd mourning him on the road (23:27).

## Why he was killed: what historians reconstruct

- **E. P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985): the act in the Temple was the trigger. To the high priest it threatened the Temple and the peace, at Passover, with Rome watching; the charge of blasphemy was a pretext and the reason was political.
- **Paula Fredriksen** (*Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews*, 1999): why was Jesus crucified and his followers left alone? Because Pilate didn't think him a real rebel, but a Passover crowd was hailing him as the coming king, and one public death was the cheapest way to end it.
- **Ellis Rivkin** (*What Crucified Jesus?*, 1984), a Jewish historian working from Josephus: not "the Jews" but "the Roman imperial system" crucified him, the emperor appointing the prefect and the prefect the high priest. E. P. Sanders, introducing the book, reportedly called the argument convincing. It's the nearest to your framing.
- **Raymond Brown** (*The Death of the Messiah*, 1994), the fullest study: the Jewish authorities who handed him over and the Roman who sentenced him both acted; he finds no good evidence of a regular Passover amnesty, and suggests Barabbas was one man released from a riot round-up, a release the Gospels made into a choice.
- **Helen Bond** (*Pontius Pilate in History and Interpretation*, 1998; *Caiaphas: Friend of Rome and Judge of Jesus?*, 2004): the prefect and the high priest worked together; in John, a Roman cohort is at the arrest, so Pilate knew of it beforehand.

They differ on the trigger and the motives. They agree that the charge was political, that it happened at Passover in a city Rome feared, and that both the high priest's circle and the prefect acted.

**The Barabbas custom.** All four Gospels mention a Passover release, but no other source records one. Some point to a governor of Egypt releasing a prisoner to a crowd (a papyrus of AD 85) and to a Mishnah rule about the Passover lamb of a man "promised release from prison" (*Pesahim* 8:6); others think it was one amnesty, or none. The text gives it, so the series keeps it.

## How the blame moved later

The New Testament writers are Jews arguing within Israel, and the texts blame its rulers. The step to "the Jews" as a people came after. By about AD 170 Melito of Sardis could preach that God had been murdered "by an Israelite hand" (*On the Passover* 96), and the charge of deicide fed centuries of massacre and expulsion. The churches have since unsaid it: the Catechism of the Council of Trent (1566) put the guilt on all sinners, Christians first; *Nostra Aetate* (1965) says the Passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today"; and the American Catholic bishops' *Criteria for the Evaluation of Dramatizations of the Passion* (1988) turned it into rules for staging. The Oberammergau Passion Play has been rewritten since the 1970s after decades of criticism; Mel Gibson's *The Passion of the Christ* (2004) reopened the argument. Our [RULES §8.7](../canon/RULES.md#87-the-passion-and-the-jewish-people) already holds the line: accuracy is the remedy.

The bishops' guidelines read almost as if they were written for this decision. They ask that Jesus and his followers be shown as "Jews among Jews"; that the crowd at the governor's palace stay small, not "a teeming mob"; that Joseph and Nicodemus be seen; that the Pharisees not be made party to the proceedings; and that Roman soldiers be shown "throughout the play, to represent the oppressive and pervasive nature of the Roman occupation."

## What it means for the series

My proposals, built on the decision; each is P2 or P4 (history and scholarly inference) unless it says otherwise.

1. **Rome everywhere, and over everything.** Soldiers in the streets, on the Temple's colonnades at the festivals, at the Antonia's stairs; the high priest's robes handed out from a Roman fortress. The look you asked for, stone no person can fill, belongs to Rome's own buildings: Caesarea's harbour and its temple to Rome and Augustus, the Antonia, Herod's palace and its three towers, the Royal Stoa. The columns aren't the evil; the scale is.
2. **Pilate is hard and calculating, not weak.** The recorded words stay, including his three "I find no guilt" in Luke, but they're played as a governor using a prisoner to make the high priests say "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15). That reading is Carter's and Bond's, and it fits Philo, Josephus and Luke 13:1 alike.
3. **The high priests are Rome's men, and layered.** Annas's family; Caiaphas as a man holding a nation together under an empire, who decides one man's death is the price ("one man should die for the people"), and is God's unknowing mouth as he says it (hour 173). Not a villain in black.
4. **The council is divided.** Joseph and Nicodemus are in the room and seen to object.
5. **The night is a hearing.** John's questioning before Annas, then Caiaphas, with Mark's words where the hours overlap; Gospel harmony is Position 11's question.
6. **The crowd at the palace is small and stirred;** Jerusalem mourns on the road (Luke 23:27); everyone who loves him is a Jew, as he is.
7. **The soldiers are Greek-speaking locals from Caesarea and Sebaste,** not Italians: Greek among themselves, Latin for orders and for Pilate's court (a change to the language table, RULES §5.7, if you agree).
8. **Pharisees argue with him through the ministry, but take almost no part in his death,** as in the texts.
9. **Barabbas stays,** as the text tells it, without explaining the custom.

**Matthew 27:25,** "His blood be on us and on our children," the line most used against Jews for two thousand years, recorded in Matthew only. **Kept as Matthew has it** (your decision, 2026-10-15): spoken by the small crowd at the palace, framed by everything above, and answered by the women weeping on the road. The text gives the answer its words: Jesus turns to them and says, "Daughters of Jerusalem, do not weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children" (Luke 23:28). The crowd claims the children for the blood; he grieves for the children. It joins Matthew's scene to Luke's, which is Position 11's kind of choice (the bishops warn against mixing Gospels carelessly; this mix is made on purpose, and says so).

**Still open, for before the Gospels:** the soldiers' language (point 7).

## Decision

**Decided 2026-10-15** ("If thats the case, then yes"): the Roman Empire is the series' general antagonist, at least in the Gospels, shown as massive stone and endless colonnades: an empire, a system, not a people, with Romans as layered as anyone (the centurion of Luke 7, the one at the cross, Cornelius). In the Passion the texts' parts stand: the high priests, Rome's appointees, hand him over; Herod mocks him; Pilate, hard and in charge, sentences him; the soldiers crucify him. The research confirms that this is how it went, as far as history can tell. Matthew 27:25 is kept as Matthew has it, spoken by the small crowd at the palace and answered by the women weeping on the road, where Jesus grieves for their children (Luke 23:27–28; round 2).
