## Part 1. The sons of God and the Nephilim (Gen 6:1–4)

### The text

> When man began to multiply on the face of the land and daughters were born to them, the sons of God saw that the daughters of man were attractive. And they took as their wives any they chose. Then the LORD said, "My Spirit shall not abide in man forever, for he is flesh: his days shall be 120 years." The Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and also afterward, when the sons of God came in to the daughters of man and they bore children to them. These were the mighty men who were of old, the men of renown. (Gen 6:1–4)

Four verses, placed between the genealogy of Adam's line (Gen 5) and "the LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great" (6:5). Three things in them are worth seeing before any reading:

- **"Saw that they were good, and took."** The Hebrew of 6:2 is *vayyir'u... ki tovot... vayyiqhu*: "they saw that they were good, and they took." That's the shape of 3:6, "the woman saw that the tree was good... and she took." Whoever the sons of God are, the text tells their act as a second grab.
- **The grammar doesn't quite say the Nephilim are the children.** "The Nephilim were on the earth in those days, *and also afterward*, when the sons of God came in..." The children are called "the mighty men (*gibborim*) of old, the men of renown," literally "the men of the name" (*anshe ha-shem*). Whether the Nephilim *are* those children or were simply around at the time is open. Most readers take them as the same.
- **"Men of the name."** Babel's builders say "let us make a name (*shem*) for ourselves" (11:4), and God promises Abram, "I will make your name great" (12:2). The name is a thread through Genesis 1–11, and this is where it starts.

### The words

- **"Sons of God"** (*bene ha-'elohim*). Everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible this exact phrase means heavenly beings in God's court: "the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and Satan also came among them" (Job 1:6; 2:1); "when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy" (Job 38:7). Close variants say the same (Ps 29:1; 89:6). Deuteronomy 32:8 in the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek reads "according to the number of the sons of God," where the later Hebrew has "sons of Israel."
- **"Nephilim."** Most likely from *naphal*, "to fall": "the fallen ones," or "those who fall upon others." The Greek translators wrote *gigantes*, "giants," and that's where the giants come from. The word appears in only one other place, Numbers 13:33.

### The three readings

| | Who they are | Who held it | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Divine beings** | Heavenly beings who cross the line between heaven and earth and father children on human women | The oldest reading we have: *1 Enoch* 6–16 (the Watchers, 3rd century BC), *Jubilees* 5, Philo, Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.73), some Greek manuscripts ("angels of God"), and nearly every Christian writer before about AD 250 (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian). Most critical scholars today. | It's what the phrase means everywhere else. The New Testament seems to know it: "angels who did not stay within their own position" are kept in chains, next to Sodom's going "after strange flesh" (Jude 6–7); "God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into Tartarus... and did not spare the ancient world, but preserved Noah" (2 Pet 2:4–5); the "spirits in prison, because they formerly did not obey... in the days of Noah" (1 Pet 3:19–20). | Jesus says that in the resurrection people "neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (Matt 22:30), which most take to mean angels don't marry. The judgment in 6:3 falls on *man*, not on any heavenly being. And in the series it would put the series' most fable-like image (giants born of angels) in hour 3. |
| **B. Seth's line** | The godly line of Seth marrying the line of Cain | Julius Africanus, Augustine (*City of God* 15.23), Chrysostom, Luther, Calvin. The standard Protestant reading for centuries. | It keeps everything human. Genesis 4–5 sets the two lines side by side, and 4:26 ends Seth's: "people began to call upon the name of the LORD." | Nothing in the text calls Seth's line "sons of God" or Cain's women "daughters of man." "Daughters of man" in 6:1 means all human daughters, so the contrast is between God and man, not two families. And it doesn't explain why the children are giants or men of renown. |
| **C. Kings** | Rulers who claim divine sonship and take any woman they want into their harems | The Aramaic Targums ("sons of the great ones"), *Genesis Rabbah* 26:5, where Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai curses anyone who says "sons of God" means angels; Meredith Kline and others in modern times. | Ancient kings did call themselves sons of gods. "Took any they chose" is a harem: Lamech, just before (4:19–24), is the first man with two wives and the first to boast of violence. "Mighty men" (*gibbor*) is what Nimrod is called (10:8–9), the first king. It explains the "name." And it fits the plain: Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, is "two-thirds god, one-third human" and takes the young brides of his city before their husbands (Gilgamesh I). The Sumerian King List gives the kings before the flood reigns of tens of thousands of years. | "Sons of God" never means kings anywhere else in the Bible, though "you are gods, sons of the Most High" is said to rulers in Psalm 82:6. It's a reading by context more than by the words. |

**How the Mesopotamian world told it.** The plain's own stories have something very close. Before the flood, the gods sent seven sages, the *apkallu*, half-fish and half-man, to teach the arts of civilization to the cities; Eridu's sage Adapa is the first. After the flood, the sages are "of human descent" or only "two-thirds apkallu." Scholars (Amar Annus, 2010) have argued that the Watchers of *1 Enoch* are the *apkallu* turned upside down: what Babylon celebrated as divine teachers, the Jewish writers called fallen ones. *Medium confidence*; it's an argument, not a consensus. It does show that 6:1–4 is answering a story the plain told about its own first kings.

### Was Goliath a descendant of the Nephilim?

**Not in so many words. The Bible draws a chain toward him and never closes it.** Here is the chain:

1. **The spies' report.** "We saw the Nephilim there (the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim), and we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them" (Num 13:33). Two things to know: the text calls this report "a bad report" (13:32), meant to frighten the people out of going in; and the words in parentheses read like a scribe's note. It's the only place the Nephilim appear after the flood.
2. **The Anakim and the Rephaim.** Deuteronomy calls the tall peoples of Canaan and Transjordan the Rephaim, "great and many and tall as the Anakim" (Deut 2:10–11, 20–21). Og of Bashan was "the last of the remnant of the Rephaim," with an iron bed nine cubits long (Deut 3:11).
3. **The remnant in Gath.** Joshua wipes out the Anakim from the hill country: "none was left in the land of the people of Israel. Only in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod did some remain" (Josh 11:21–22).
4. **Goliath of Gath** (1 Sam 17:4). And among the Philistine champions of David's wars, four are "descendants of the giants (*ha-rapha*) in Gath," one of them Goliath's brother (2 Sam 21:15–22; 1 Chr 20:4–8).

So the link goes Nephilim → Anakim (only in a report the text calls bad) → the Anakim left in Gath → Goliath of Gath and the "descendants of the Rapha." **Goliath is likely meant as one of the last of the Anakim or Rephaim; that those came from the Nephilim is said once, by frightened men.** And the flood is supposed to have ended the Nephilim, so either the spies' words are exaggeration, or "and also afterward" (6:4) is doing work, or (on a regional flood) some lived outside the water. The Bible doesn't settle it.

**His height** is its own question (Position 2): "six cubits and a span," about 2.9 m (9'9"), in the standard Hebrew; "four cubits and a span," about 2 m (6'9"), in the Greek, the oldest Hebrew scroll of Samuel (4QSam^a) and Josephus. Even the smaller figure makes him a giant among men who averaged about 1.6 m.

### What fits the project

- **Your rule on the devil** ([FR-19](../canon/FRAMINGS.md#fr-19--the-devil-as-what-evil-actions-are)): the devil is a role played wherever it's played, and the serpent is an animal that played it. Reading C works the same way. The sons of God are men who play the role of gods, and the grab of 3:6 is now made by kings.
- **Your rule on God's voice** ([001](./001-how-god-speaks.md)): God works through people and the world. Reading A asks for non-human persons with bodies on screen in hour 3, before anything like that appears anywhere else in the series.
- **The plain.** The Garden is on the lower plain near Eridu ([008](./008-adam-eve-and-the-garden.md)). The plain's own memory of its first age is god-born kings with impossible reigns. Hour 3 is set where that story was told.
- **The rule on ambiguous text** ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)) says to follow the most likely reading. On the words alone, the most likely original sense is A: divine beings. That pulls against my recommendation below, and I'd rather name it than hide it.

### My recommendation (mine, for you to push against)

**Stage it so the audience sees what the people of the plain believed, and what was actually there.** The kings of the cities call themselves sons of the gods; their people believe it; their temples say it; their reigns are told as thousands of years. They take any woman they choose, as Gilgamesh did. Their sons are huge, trained, bred for war: the champions, "the men of the name." The audience never sees a being come down from the sky; it sees men who claim to have, and a world that believes them. The Opening's world can come close in one glimpse (a king's face, lit by his own temple, and for a beat it isn't quite human), and that's as far as it goes.

- **Grade: Compatible** for C as the staging (Gen 4:19–24; 6:1–4; 10:8–10; Ps 82:6–7: "you are gods... nevertheless, like men you shall die"), with **Tension against A**, the most likely sense of the phrase, which this staging holds only as the people's belief. The Enoch material stays where Discussion 005 put it: bin 2, a decision for Genesis ([005](./005-beyond-the-text.md)).
- **The Nephilim:** large men, warriors, "fallers upon," not three-metre monsters. Goliath later is their echo: a champion from the last of the tall clans, 2 m in the older text.
- **The 120 years** (6:3): a limit set on human life or a countdown to the flood. I'd show it as the countdown: Noah's warning has a number.

### Questions for our conversation

1. **The sons of God:** divine beings (A), Seth's line (B), or kings who claim to be gods' sons (C, my pick), staged as the people's belief?
2. **The Nephilim:** big men and warriors, or giants in the fable sense?
3. **Goliath:** do we let the audience connect him to the Nephilim (a line, a rumor among Saul's soldiers: "the sons of Anak") or leave it unsaid?

## Part 2. The flood (Gen 6:9–9:17)

### Your view

> To me the flood was regional, but it was incredibly massive. I don't know if we know where it may have come from, but I think it was an incredibly massive tsunami or something from the Persian Gulf that wiped out civilizations on the Euphrates and around.

### What the text says about the water

- **Two sources at once.** "All the fountains of the great deep burst open, and the windows of the heavens were opened" (7:11). The "great deep" is *tehom*, the Opening's own word for the sea (1:2). So the text's flood isn't only rain. It's the sea breaking in *and* the sky opening. That's the strongest text for your idea: the water comes up out of the deep.
- **How long.** Rain for forty days (7:12); the water "prevailed" 150 days (7:24); the ark rests in the seventh month (8:4); the ground is dry a year and ten days after it began (7:11; 8:14). The water goes down "going and returning" (8:3, *halokh vashov*), back and forth.
- **How far.** "All the high mountains under the whole heaven were covered... fifteen cubits deep" (7:19–20); "all flesh died that moved on the earth... only Noah was left, and those who were with him" (7:21–23). The ark rests "on the mountains of Ararat" (8:4), Urartu, the highlands north of Mesopotamia.
- **The promise.** "Never again shall there be a flood to destroy the earth" (9:11).

### What history and science say

- **The plain floods.** Southern Iraq is nearly flat: Baghdad, 550 km from the sea, is only about 35 m above it. The Tigris and Euphrates flood every spring from snowmelt far to the north. Excavators found thick clean flood silts at several cities: Ur (Leonard Woolley, 1929, a layer of about 3 m, in the Ubaid period), Kish, Uruk, and **Shuruppak**, where a layer at the end of the Jemdet Nasr period, **about 2900 BC**, marks a flood. Shuruppak is the city of Ziusudra and Utnapishtim, the flood heroes of the Sumerian and Babylonian stories. The layers at the different cities aren't the same date, so no one flood covered the whole plain at once in the record. *Medium confidence on the dates.*
- **The plain remembers one flood.** The Sumerian King List: "Then the flood swept over. After the flood had swept over, and kingship had descended from heaven, the kingship was in Kish." That's about 2900 BC. The Sumerian flood story (Ziusudra), *Atrahasis* (about 1700 BC) and Gilgamesh XI tell it with a boat, animals, birds sent out, a sacrifice afterward and the gods smelling it (compare Gen 8:6–12, 20–21). In Gilgamesh the boat lands on Mount Nimush, in the Zagros of Kurdistan; Berossus has the Gordyaean mountains, the same country. Genesis is the same memory told about the one God.
- **The sea came in, for real, over millennia.** At the end of the Ice Age the whole Persian Gulf was a dry valley with a river and freshwater springs down its length (Jeffrey Rose's "Gulf Oasis"). The sea filled it between about 12,000 and 6,000 years ago, sometimes fast, and by about 4000 BC the shore stood near Ur and Eridu, far north of today's coast. People living in the Gulf basin were pushed north, out of a land that went under. That's a flood from the Gulf, but slow: a generation's lifetime would see the shore move, not a wave.
- **A tsunami from the Gulf is unlikely.** The Gulf is shallow (about 35 m on average) and almost closed. Big tsunamis need deep water and a large quake or landslide; the region's real tsunami source is the Makran coast off Pakistan and Iran (1945), and its waves hit the Gulf of Oman, not the head of the Gulf. And a tsunami lasts hours, where the text's water stands for months.
- **What can push the Gulf onto the plain is wind.** A great storm surge: a cyclone driving the sea up the Gulf, rare there but real (Cyclone Gonu, 2007, struck Oman and Iran), meeting the rivers in full spring flood, with rain. On land this flat the sea and the rivers become one sheet of water, and it takes months to drain because the land has nowhere lower to drain to. In Gilgamesh, the flood comes as a storm: in several translations "the south wind" blows and drowns the land. South is where the Gulf is. *Medium-low confidence on that line's wording; the storm itself is certain.*
- **One speculative candidate.** Umm al Binni, a round lake about 3.4 km across in the marshes near al-Amarah, has been proposed as an impact crater from the third millennium BC (Sharad Master, 2001). If it were one, an impact into the marshes would have been a catastrophe for the plain. It's unconfirmed; P4 at most, and I'd only keep it as a note.

### Global or regional: the case each way

**For regional.**

- "The earth" (*ha-'arets*) is also "the land." "All the earth came to Egypt to buy grain" (Gen 41:57) means the known world, not Peru. "Under the whole heaven" is used of the nations around Israel (Deut 2:25).
- The project already has people across the world for 55,000 years ([FR-01](../canon/FRAMINGS.md#fr-01--the-opening-creation-as-cosmic-and-biological-history); [008](./008-adam-eve-and-the-garden.md)). A flood that covered every mountain on earth around 3000 BC can't stand beside that, or beside Egypt, whose history runs through those centuries without a break.
- The ark grounds in the northern mountains: exactly where a southern surge would drive a drifting boat, northwest up the plain to the Zagros. Walton and Longman (*The Lost World of the Flood*, 2018) read the text as a real local event told in the language of the whole world, which is how the ancient Near East told catastrophes.

**Against it, the strongest texts.**

- "All the high mountains under the whole heaven were covered" (7:19), and the water stands fifteen cubits over them (7:20). "All flesh died" (7:21). "Only Noah was left" (7:23).
- The nations of Genesis 10 all come from Noah's sons: "from these the whole earth was dispersed" (9:19).
- If the flood was local, why the ark, and why the animals? Noah could have walked away.
- The New Testament takes it as a judgment on the whole world of its day: "the flood came and swept them all away" (Matt 24:39); "the world that then existed was deluged" (2 Pet 3:6); eight people saved (1 Pet 3:20).
- The rainbow's promise (9:11): local floods have happened ever since, so the promise has to mean a flood that destroys a whole world.

**How it holds.** The texts are written from inside the world they describe. For Noah, the land is the world: the plain from the Gulf to the mountains, its cities, and Adam's line. The water covers every high place he can see; "all flesh" in that world dies; his sons' clans are the nations of Genesis 10, the nations that world knew. The ark is needed because there's nowhere to walk to: the whole plain is sea for months. The promise is that God won't end a world again by water. **Grade: Tension, adopted on purpose:** Compatible with 7:11, 8:3–4 and 41:57's sense of "all the earth"; in tension with 7:19–23 read as the whole globe and with 9:19 as everyone alive.

### What I'd change in your version

- **Not a tsunami: the deep and the heavens together.** A storm surge from the Gulf, at the same time as the greatest spring flood the rivers ever had, and rain on the plain for forty days. On a plain that flat, that's a sea from the Gulf to the foothills, standing for months, going and returning with the wind and the tides (8:3). It keeps your source (the Gulf, *tehom*) and gives the text its year.
- **"Wiped out civilizations."** The plain's cities weren't all wiped out at once in the archaeological record; the flood layers are at different dates. The Sumerians themselves say the flood swept over, and then kingship had to come down from heaven again, at Kish. So the story can say what they said: the world of the plain ended, and a new one started. What the audience sees is the plain's world drowning; what it doesn't see is anything beyond the horizon.
- **The people outside** ([008](./008-adam-eve-and-the-garden.md)) were never in the story's world. They don't need to be mentioned.

## Part 3. The dates

You asked what I think. My opinion: **date Genesis 1–11 by the Greek Bible's numbers, as a working calendar, loosely.** That's where your Garden date has already gone.

**The two calendars.** The ages at which the fathers have their sons (Gen 5; 11) differ between the Hebrew and the Greek. Counted back from our Abraham (born 1951 BC, [TIMELINE §4](../world/TIMELINE.md#4-the-timeline-arc-by-arc)):

| | Hebrew (the default text) | Greek (the Septuagint) |
|---|---|---|
| Adam to the flood | 1,656 years | 2,242 years |
| The flood to Abraham | 292 years | 1,072 years (it includes Cainan, son of Arpachshad) |
| Adam | about 3900 BC | about 5300 BC (about 5350 if Terah was 130 at Abraham's birth, as Acts 7:4 implies) |
| The flood | about 2240 BC | about 3000 BC |
| Peleg, "in his days the earth was divided" (10:25) | born about 2140 BC | born about 2490 BC, died about 2150 |

**Why the Greek.**

1. **The New Testament uses it.** Luke's genealogy of Jesus has "Cainan, the son of Arphaxad" (Luke 3:36). Cainan is only in the Greek Genesis. Luke counted the line from the Greek. And the Greek is the Old Testament the New Testament quotes most.
2. **The Hebrew flood lands in recorded history.** 2240 BC is the age of the Akkadian empire, with written records on the plain all the way through it. A flood that ends the plain's world can't go there. The Greek puts it about 3000 BC, beside Shuruppak's flood layer (about 2900 BC) and the King List's flood before Kish. That's the best match any calendar gives.
3. **Adam at the start of the plain.** About 5300 BC is the early Ubaid, when the lower plain was first farmed by irrigation and Eridu was being built. Your new date for Adam, about 5500 BC (your answer on the Garden's gate, 2026-10-13), sits at the old end of the Greek count, within its margin.
4. **Babel in Peleg's days.** The Greek puts Peleg at about 2490–2150 BC. That's the age when Sargon of Akkad (about 2334–2279 BC) made the first empire on the plain, and "the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad" is said of Nimrod (10:10). Hour 6's Nimrod and hour 7's tower sit in the right century. *Added 2026-10-13, from the Garden's research:* with Babel at Eridu, "they left off building the city" (11:8) may be Amar-Sin's unfinished ziggurat there, about 2047–2039 BC, under the kings of Ur, a few decades before Abraham is born in Ur (1951). That's just after Peleg's lifetime on this count, and within the looseness of a calendar with gaps; if the tower is earlier, the unfinished building is an earlier Eridu platform ([LAYOUT §10.7](../story/arcs/01-beginnings/garden/LAYOUT.md)).

**Against it.**

- The Hebrew text is the project's default ([ROADMAP §9.2](../ROADMAP.md#92-base-text)), and the Greek numbers look systematically adjusted: most of the extra years come from adding a hundred to the fathers' ages at their sons' births. Scholars mostly think one side or both reworked the numbers.
- In the common Greek text, Methuselah outlives the flood by fourteen years (one manuscript family fixes it by making him 187 at Lamech's birth). We'd follow that reading.
- The genealogies may skip generations anyway (Matthew 1:8 skips three kings; "father of" can mean ancestor), so no calendar from them is exact.

**So:** a working calendar, not a claim. **Adam about 5500 BC; the flood about 3000 BC; Babel between about 2400 and 2040 BC; Abraham 1951 BC.** It's loose on purpose: Adam to the flood is about 2,500 years here, where the Greek numbers give 2,242, because the genealogies are read with gaps (the Garden's date already needs that). A flood about 3000 BC also fits Eridu, which lies drowned under silt by the end of the Uruk period. It's a decision about which text to follow, recorded case by case, as ROADMAP §9.2 asks.
