## Why this one, now

The map's hour 3 ends on "God's grief; Noah finds favor" ([ARCHITECTURE](../story/ARCHITECTURE.md)). The text:

> The LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually. And the LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart. So the LORD said, "I will blot out man whom I have created..." (Gen 6:5–7)

That's the first time the Bible tells us what God feels, and it's regret. The others on the list (suffering, providence, hiddenness, justice and mercy, prayer, doubt) are Job, Joseph, Esther, Jonah and the Gospels, all years of writing away. This one is written in the same hour as the sons of God, and the flood depends on it.

## The question

Can God regret, change his mind, or be talked out of something? And how does the series show it, when God is seen through the world and speaks in the hearer's own voice?

## What the texts say

The verb is *nacham*: to be sorry, to regret, to relent, to be moved to change course, and also to be comforted. The same verb carries both sides.

**God regrets and relents:**

- "The LORD regretted that he had made man" (Gen 6:6).
- At the golden calf, Moses argues with God, and "the LORD relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people" (Exod 32:14).
- "I regret that I have made Saul king" (1 Sam 15:11), and the chapter ends "the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel" (15:35).
- Nineveh repents, and "God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them" (Jonah 3:10). Jonah is angry precisely because he knew God would: "you are a gracious God... and relenting from disaster" (Jonah 4:2).
- Hezekiah is told he will die, prays, and gets fifteen more years (2 Kgs 20:1–6).
- God states it as a rule: "If at any time I declare concerning a nation... that I will break it down and destroy it, and if that nation turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster" (Jer 18:7–10).

**God does not regret:**

- "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind (*yitnecham*)" (Num 23:19).
- And in the very chapter where God regrets making Saul: "the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret (*yinnachem*), for he is not a man, that he should have regret" (1 Sam 15:29).
- "I the LORD do not change" (Mal 3:6); "the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (James 1:17).

1 Samuel 15 puts both in one chapter (15:11, 29, 35), on purpose. The writer saw the problem and wanted both said.

## How readers have held both

- **Accommodation.** Calvin, and most of the classical tradition: God doesn't change; Scripture describes him "as we perceive him," in human terms, the way a nurse talks to a child (*Institutes* I.17.13). Regret is how a change in God's dealings looks from below. Its risk: it can make the grief in Gen 6:6 a figure of speech, and the text says "it grieved him to his heart."
- **The threat was always conditional.** Jeremiah 18's rule: God's warnings carry an unspoken "unless." When Nineveh turns, God isn't surprised; the warning did what it was for. Jonah 4:2 says the prophet knew this all along.
- **God really responds.** Open theism (Clark Pinnock, Greg Boyd, 1990s on) takes the verses at face value: God enters time, takes risks, and genuinely changes plans. Its cost: Num 23:19 and 1 Sam 15:29 have to be read as "God doesn't lie or go back on his word," not "God never changes course."
- **Jewish tradition** has it both ways with relish. *Genesis Rabbah* 27:4 has God mourning the world before he destroys it, as a man mourns a son before the son dies. Abraham argues with God over Sodom (Gen 18:23–33) and Moses at the calf, and the tradition treats that arguing as faithfulness.

## What the project has already decided that bears on it

- **007:** the malice isn't in God; it's how a separated part with an ego perceives the whole. "With the crooked you make yourself seem tortuous" (Ps 18:26). That's accommodation, in your own form: God appears according to who is looking.
- **001:** God speaks to a person alone in their own voice, "steadier than their own, able to say what they don't want to hear and to be argued with." Moses at the calf and Abraham at Sodom *argue*. The device was built for exactly this.
- **001, again:** "the separation is real as experience and never real as objective fact." That suggests one answer: the regret is real as God's experience *with* the world, and God isn't divided by it.

## My view (mine, for you to push against)

**The regret is real, and it's grief, not a mistake.** God doesn't learn something he didn't know in Gen 6:6; he feels what the world has become. "Grieved to his heart" is the same word (*'atsav*) as Eve's "pain" in childbearing (3:16) and the man's "pain" in his toil (3:17): God takes the curse's own word on himself. That's the line I'd build hour 3's ending on: the world's pain is God's pain.

**The relenting is the warning working.** Jeremiah 18 is the rule, and Jonah 4:2 shows the prophet knew it. When God relents, nothing in God was overruled; the turn God hoped for happened. On screen: the person argues in their own voice, and the voice lets itself be argued with, and the audience can feel that it wanted to be.

**Staging for hour 3: A, then B.** The Opening's world comes back as grief, the first time it's ever been sad: the held note bending down for the first time in the series. Then it settles on one face, Noah's, and the grief is his to carry. "But Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (6:8): the hour ends on a face God looks at.

**The two kinds of text are both kept.** 1 Sam 15:29 is right that God doesn't go back on his word or change like a man; 15:11 and 35 are right that he grieves over what people do and answers them. *Grade, if adopted:* **Supported** (Gen 6:6; Jer 18:7–10; Jonah 4:2; 1 Sam 15:11, 29, 35 held together).
