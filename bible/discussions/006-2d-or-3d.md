## The question

Should the animated version be 2D (drawn) rather than 3D (modeled and rendered)? How much better could 2D make the style and the hardest visuals? And what would AI-made animation cost in each?

## Your view so far

You're open to 2D and want an honest opinion. Your stated plan is that the animation is a placeholder for an eventual live-action show or films. You still want it to look good, with bright, vibrant moments and some scenes that need very advanced effects.

## What 2D does better

- **Style that lasts.** Good hand-drawn animation ages well. *The Prince of Egypt* (1998) still looks beautiful, while most 3D from the same years looks dated.
- **Visions and glory.** Ezekiel's wheels within wheels (Ezek 1), Daniel's beasts (Dan 7), the throne room of Revelation 4, the burning bush, the Transfiguration. In 2D these can be *designed* as art, stylized and strange, instead of simulated. For the Bible's most otherworldly images, that may be the better fit.
- **Faces and emotion.** Drawn faces avoid the "uncanny valley," the unease people feel at faces that are almost but not quite real.
- **Light, fire and color.** 2D handles stylized light beautifully: *Klaus* (2019) showed how far it can go. Your bright, vibrant moments would sing in 2D.

## What 3D does better

- **Scale and camera.** Huge crowds, armies, the sea standing up as walls, sweeping camera moves through real space. *The Prince of Egypt* is the proof here too: its Red Sea and its chariots used computer animation under the drawings.
- **Consistency over hundreds of hours.** A 3D character looks the same in episode 1 and episode 80. In 2D that consistency is drawn by hand, every time.
- **Reuse.** A 3D Bethlehem, threshing floor or Roman soldier is built once and reused across dozens of episodes (archived ROADMAP §15.1). In 2D, each new shot is drawn again.
- **The bridge to live action.** This is the deciding point for your plan. 3D sets, cameras, lenses and blocking carry straight over to a live-action shoot. A real-time 3D environment can even become the background on an LED stage (archived ROADMAP §15). A 2D version is a separate work of art: none of it carries over.

## The middle road: 3D that looks painted

Many of the best recent animated films are 3D underneath and look hand-painted on the surface: *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* (2018), *Arcane* (2021), *Puss in Boots: The Last Wish* (2022). The technique is called non-photorealistic rendering. You get most of 2D's look while keeping 3D's cameras, reuse and live-action bridge.

## My view

**Keep 3D, with a painterly surface,** and use real 2D where it's strongest:

- **The pipeline is 3D,** because it's the only route that serves the live-action plan.
- **The look can be painterly** rather than photoreal. That's already the leaning in archived ROADMAP §15.3.
- **The visions can go 2D.** Ezekiel, Daniel, Revelation, perhaps creation: scenes that are meant to look unlike the ordinary world could break into a drawn or painted style. A change of medium could become the series' visual language for "this is a vision," the same way a change of color does now.
- **Development is 2D anyway.** Storyboards and the Ruth animatic (archived ROADMAP §15.4) are drawings. So 2D gets used first, cheaply, whatever we choose for the finish.

## What it costs

These are rough figures as of October 2026, and prices are moving fast.

### Traditional studio rates

| Kind | Typical price per finished minute |
|---|---|
| 2D, TV quality | About $5,000–$15,000+ |
| 3D, high-end | About $15,000–$50,000+ |

So 3D usually costs about three to five times as much as 2D at comparable quality. For a 58-minute Ruth episode, that's very roughly **$300,000–$900,000 in 2D** and **$900,000–$3 million in 3D** at studio rates. Ruth needs few effects and could come in under that. Premium series cost far more. Sources: [B2W](https://www.b2w.tv/blog/how-much-does-animation-cost), [Prolific Studio](https://prolificstudio.co/blog/2d-vs-3d-animation-cost/).

### AI video generation

| Model (2026) | Price per generated second |
|---|---|
| Veo 3.1 (Google) | About $0.50 without audio, $0.75 with; a "Lite" tier near $0.03 |
| Sora 2 (OpenAI) | About $0.10 at 720p, more at higher tiers |
| Kling 3.0 | About $0.08–$0.17 |
| Runway Gen-4 / 4.5 | About $0.05–$0.12 |

Sources: [CometAPI](https://www.cometapi.com/ai-video-api-pricing/), [Atlas Cloud](https://atlascloud.ai/blog/guides/cheapest-ai-video-generation-api-2026), [ModelsLab](https://modelslab.com/blog/api/veo-3-1-vs-kling-3-sora-2-ai-video-api-cost-2026).

**2D or 3D makes almost no difference to this cost.** The model generates pixels either way; "hand-drawn" or "3D-rendered" is a matter of style references and prompts. A 58-minute episode is about 3,500 seconds. If each usable shot takes 10 to 20 tries, which is realistic today, the generation bill comes to **roughly $3,500–$35,000 per episode,** depending on the model. Most of the real cost is human work: editing, keeping characters consistent, voices, music and sound.

### What AI generation doesn't give you

- **No 3D world.** AI video that *looks* 3D isn't 3D. There are no sets, cameras or characters to reuse, so it doesn't carry over to live action the way a real 3D build does. It's still very useful for previsualization: working out what a shot should look like.
- **Consistency is the hard part.** Keeping Ruth's face, Naomi's house and Bethlehem's gate identical across a thousand shots is what current tools struggle with most.
- **Acting control.** Getting exactly the look on Naomi's face that you described (calculation, pain, exhaustion, acceptance, in that order) is far harder to direct in AI video than with an animator or an actor.
- **Ownership.** In the US, purely AI-generated material generally can't be copyrighted; human authorship is required. Your scripts, designs and edits are yours, but the generated frames may not be protected. That matters for a project meant to become a show. It's worth checking with a lawyer before production, because the rules are still developing.

### The hybrid that I think fits

Build the key characters and sets in 3D (Unreal or Blender), animate the performances, then use AI to restyle or finish the frames: painterly looks, skies, crowds, fire. It costs more up front than pure AI generation, but it gives you control, consistency, reuse and the live-action bridge. And it gets cheaper per episode as the asset library grows.

## Questions for our conversation

1. **Does the live-action plan still come first?** If it does, I think 3D is the answer. If the animation might *become* the final product, 2D deserves a much harder look.
2. **The painterly 3D look.** Do *Arcane* or *Spider-Verse* feel close to what you're imagining, or do you picture something more like *The Prince of Egypt*?
3. **Visions in a different medium.** Does the idea of the Bible's visions breaking into a painted or drawn style appeal to you?
4. **AI's role.** Are you planning to make it mostly with AI tools, mostly with people, or a mix? That changes the budget more than 2D versus 3D does.

### Round 1 — 2026-10-02

**Your position, in your words:**

**The short answer: yes, have AI drive Blender on your computer, for anything you want to keep.** Use prompt-only AI for quick, throwaway concept images. Here's why, and how I'd do it with almost no money.

**What "AI driving Blender" means.** Claude Code runs on your own computer as well as in the cloud. There, it can write Python scripts that build a scene in Blender (terrain, buildings, lights, cameras, simple figures), render a frame, look at the result, and fix it. You describe what you want, and the AI does the button-pushing. You don't need to be good at Blender; you need to be good at saying what you see, which is what you're already doing with the scripts. Your Primordia setup guide (outside this repository) already covers installing it, and there are also tools that let the AI control a running Blender window live.

**Why that beats prompting a video generator, for your goals:**

| What you want | Prompt-only AI video | AI driving Blender |
|---|---|---|
| Show people your vision | Fast and beautiful, but you get what the model gives you | Slower, but you decide every camera and every light |
| The same Bethlehem in every shot | Hard; places drift from shot to shot | Built once, identical every time |
| Bring in artists later | Nothing for them to open; a finished video is a dead end | A Blender file any 3D artist can open and improve |
| Live action later | No sets or cameras to carry over | The same sets and camera moves preview the shoot (archived ROADMAP §15) |
| Ownership | Purely generated frames generally can't be copyrighted in the US | Your scene, built from your direction in scripts and files, is much more clearly your work |
| The crucifixion | The big commercial video tools filter graphic violence, so it may be refused or softened | No filter but your own judgment |

**What AI-driven Blender is bad at,** to be honest: faces, subtle acting, cloth and hair. Left alone, it looks like a well-lit technical demo. That's why the hybrid has a second half.

**The hybrid I'd suggest: 3D for structure, AI for the surface.**
1. **Build the shot in Blender, with the AI driving:** the landscape, the buildings, the camera move, the light and simple stand-in figures. Real terrain for the Dead Sea rift, the plateau and the Judean hills can be imported from free satellite elevation data, so the view across the rift is the real one.
2. **Render the guides:** depth, outlines and rough color.
3. **Restyle with AI, guided by those renders.** Image and video models can paint over a 3D render while keeping its shapes and camera. You get the painterly look we talked about, and every frame stays where the 3D put it. This can run on your own computer with free tools if you have a strong graphics card, or through paid services for a few dollars a shot.

**Your three reference scenes, from easiest to hardest:**

| Scene | Why | How |
|---|---|---|
| **The opening of Genesis** (your FR-01: from static to the cosmos to the Nile delta) | No characters, so nothing to keep consistent. Scale, light, particles and motion are exactly what code does well. | Almost entirely AI-driven Blender: procedural space, light, early Earth. Restyle with AI where you want a painted feel. **Start here.** |
| **Ruth: the edge of the plateau** | Three small figures in a huge landscape, and the wind. The faces are far away, which hides the hard part. | Real terrain from elevation data, simple figures with stock walking animation, grass and cloth in the wind, then a restyle pass. A good second scene. |
| **The crucifixion** | Close faces, bodies, suffering, a crowd. The hardest thing to do well, and the scene that most needs a human artist. | For now, as storyboards or an animatic: drawn or painted frames, timed to sound. If you want one rendered shot, design it to avoid close acting: a wide shot at the darkness (Mark 15:33), silhouettes, the sky. The full scene waits for collaborators. |

**For bringing people in,** the order matters more than the polish:
1. **A look book** (cost: nothing). Paintings, film stills, photographs of the real places, plus a few AI concept images clearly labeled as concept. It says "this is how it should feel."
2. **The Ruth animatic** of one scene (cost: nothing but time). Storyboard frames, timed to scratch voices and temporary music. Writers, producers and directors respond to an animatic more than to anything else, because it shows the story works.
3. **One rendered scene,** the Genesis opening, as proof of the look.

**One caution about artists and AI.** Many artists are wary of generative AI, sometimes strongly. If you want to recruit them, it helps to be clear that the AI is building previews and tests, and that the final look is theirs to make. An AI-assisted Blender scene reads as normal 3D work in progress, which artists can join. A reel of generated video can read as "I don't need you."

**Money and time.** Blender and Claude Code locally cost what you already pay, or nothing. Most of the expense in this plan is your time. AI restyling costs nothing on a strong home graphics card, or a modest monthly subscription if you use a service only when you need it. None of this needs to start until the writing is far enough along. My suggestion: one experiment now, as a test, and the rest later. A single still frame of the view across the rift from the Moab plateau would tell you within a weekend whether this way of working suits you.

**Questions:**
1. **What computer do you have,** and especially which graphics card? That decides how much of this can run at home for free.
2. **Would you like to try the one-frame test** when you next work locally?
3. **The look book:** shall I start a page of visual references for the three scenes, drawn from art history and the real locations?

### Round 3 — 2026-10-15

**Your question, in your words:**

> You put Arcane in the list. Do you think we should still do the base style as Arcane? Or should we only use it as a backup in case we cant get hyper-realism?

It came the same day as your note on rendering: "I want this to be truly realistic."

**My response.** Round 2 made two things the base: a placeholder that looks like live action, and a base style inspired by *Arcane*. They can't both be the base, and your question is the right one. My view is realism as the base and *Arcane* as the fallback, but decided by a test of the hardest case, not on faith.

*For realism:*

- **It's your end goal.** Every realistic shot rehearses the live-action version: the same sets, cameras, blocking and timing carry over. An *Arcane* series is a different film, and it would have to be redesigned for live action.
- **The Opening forces it.** The Opening ends on a real face drawing a breath in, and the Garden opens on that breath let out ([ARCHITECTURE](../story/ARCHITECTURE.md), Episode 1). Changing style between the breath in and the breath out would break the series' first join, so whatever beats 10–14 look like, the Garden has to look like too.
- **It's what the tools do best.** AI video is strongest at photoreal; asked to hold a painted look across hundreds of shots, it drifts back toward photoreal. And *Arcane*'s look isn't the cheap option: it took a whole studio about six years, hand-painting textures and drawing effects over the 3D. "*Arcane*-like" from you and AI would most likely come out as generic stylized 3D.

*Against it, the strongest case:*

- **Talking faces.** The series is screenplay-first and full of dialogue. Real-looking people talking for minutes, holding who they are and how they act from cut to cut, is exactly where AI video and CG are weakest, and where the uncanny valley bites. A painted face forgives what a real-looking one doesn't. This is the deciding question.
- **The Garden comes next, and Adam and Eve are naked.** Natural nakedness is harder to stage with modesty when it looks real, and the AI video tools refuse realistic nudity outright. The Garden will be staged with light and shade, leaves, water and framing whatever the style ([009, round 4](./009-god-in-the-garden.md#round-4-your-answer-on-their-nakedness--2026-10-14)), but realism narrows the choices.
- **The violence ahead,** from the Flood to the cross, is more graphic when it looks real. RULES already names prolonged violence as a way to fail ([§2.3](../canon/RULES.md#23-precedents)), so realism asks for restraint in the staging.
- **Ageing.** A photoreal look from 2026's tools will date sooner than a strong style; *The Prince of Egypt* is the proof (above).

*The test.* Before the Garden is made, one 30-second scene of two people talking, in close-ups and cuts, made the realistic way: our 3D layout and face, motion capture, and the AI pass. If it holds from cut to cut and stays out of the uncanny valley, realism is the base and *Arcane* the fallback. If it doesn't, *Arcane* is the base, and the Opening's last face has to meet the Garden's look halfway.

*What it changes now:* nothing. The layout is the same in either style, the Opening's beats 10–14 are already aiming at the live-action look, and the visions keep their own vibrant register either way (it stands out even more against realism). It's needed before the Garden's look development.

**My response.** I agree: make the *Arcane* look the base, built in Blender. Your reasons hold, and they outweigh mine for realism in round 3.

*Why it's right:*

- **A placeholder doesn't need to look real.** The live-action version will be real; this one only has to be good. A strong painted style is a finished thing in its own right, where a realistic placeholder is always nearly live action.
- **It removes realism's two worst risks.** One is the uncanny valley in long dialogue. The other is depending on AI video: its drift from shot to shot, its refusal of the Garden's nakedness, and its frames, which can't be copyrighted (PLAN §7). Built in Blender, every frame is ours.
- **It's cheaper and faster.** A painted look renders in EEVEE, Blender's fast renderer: the leaf look test took about 2 seconds a frame on your card. Much of it may render at home, with rented GPUs only for the heavy shots.
- **Faces and animation get easier, not easy.** A painted face forgives what a real-looking one doesn't. But a stylized face still shows bad acting, so the acting has to come from somewhere: your webcam performance for the faces, motion capture for the bodies, then cleanup by hand. I can't feel a performance; you're the actor and the judge.

*Where I'd push back:*

- **Copy the method, not the show.** How *Arcane* is made can be copied, and is, everywhere: 3D with hand-painted textures, painterly light, hand-drawn effects, painted backgrounds, a strong graphic shape language. A look on its own isn't protected by copyright; the show's characters, designs, artwork and assets are. So no textures or models taken from the show or the game, no lookalike characters, and no AI model trained on its frames. With our own designs the series looks like itself, not like an *Arcane* knock-off, which matters if it's ever shown. Our world is the Bronze Age Near East, not Piltover: the method carries over, the designs don't. (I'm not a lawyer; this is the general rule.)
- **The hardest part is the characters, not the pipeline.** I can build the shading, the light, the effects, the sets, the cameras and the rigs. The weak link is appealing stylized characters: *Arcane*'s come from first-rate concept artists and sculptors, over years. Our route is designs drawn with image tools from your direction, a starting mesh from an image-to-3D tool or a stylized base body, then cleanup in Blender. Expect this to take the most rounds.
- **Prove it before switching everything.** One style test on things we already have: the band's run (04:15–04:18, laid out and on motion capture) in the painted look, and one face, close, with one expression. If those hold, the switch is safe.

*The Opening.* It keeps its shape: the vision register of beats 1–9 comes down into the series' painted world in beats 10–14, as round 2 first had it. What changes is 10–14, painted rather than live-action-looking, and the recurring face, designed in the series' style. How the descent from the abstract into the painted world reads is the conversation to have next.
