## The question

Should the animated version be 2D (drawn) rather than 3D (modeled and rendered)? How much better could 2D make the style and the hardest visuals? And what would AI-made animation cost in each?

## Your view so far

You're open to 2D and want an honest opinion. Your stated plan is that the animation is a placeholder for an eventual live-action show or films. You still want it to look good, with bright, vibrant moments and some scenes that need very advanced effects.

## What 2D does better

- **Style that lasts.** Good hand-drawn animation ages well. *The Prince of Egypt* (1998) still looks beautiful, while most 3D from the same years looks dated.
- **Visions and glory.** Ezekiel's wheels within wheels (Ezek 1), Daniel's beasts (Dan 7), the throne room of Revelation 4, the burning bush, the Transfiguration. In 2D these can be *designed* as art, stylized and strange, instead of simulated. For the Bible's most otherworldly images, that may be the better fit.
- **Faces and emotion.** Drawn faces avoid the "uncanny valley," the unease people feel at faces that are almost but not quite real.
- **Light, fire and color.** 2D handles stylized light beautifully: *Klaus* (2019) showed how far it can go. Your bright, vibrant moments would sing in 2D.

## What 3D does better

- **Scale and camera.** Huge crowds, armies, the sea standing up as walls, sweeping camera moves through real space. *The Prince of Egypt* is the proof here too: its Red Sea and its chariots used computer animation under the drawings.
- **Consistency over hundreds of hours.** A 3D character looks the same in episode 1 and episode 80. In 2D that consistency is drawn by hand, every time.
- **Reuse.** A 3D Bethlehem, threshing floor or Roman soldier is built once and reused across dozens of episodes (ROADMAP §15.1). In 2D, each new shot is drawn again.
- **The bridge to live action.** This is the deciding point for your plan. 3D sets, cameras, lenses and blocking carry straight over to a live-action shoot. A real-time 3D environment can even become the background on an LED stage (ROADMAP §15). A 2D version is a separate work of art: none of it carries over.

## The middle road: 3D that looks painted

Many of the best recent animated films are 3D underneath and look hand-painted on the surface: *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* (2018), *Arcane* (2021), *Puss in Boots: The Last Wish* (2022). The technique is called non-photorealistic rendering. You get most of 2D's look while keeping 3D's cameras, reuse and live-action bridge.

## My view

**Keep 3D, with a painterly surface,** and use real 2D where it's strongest:

- **The pipeline is 3D,** because it's the only route that serves the live-action plan.
- **The look can be painterly** rather than photoreal. That's already the leaning in ROADMAP §15.3.
- **The visions can go 2D.** Ezekiel, Daniel, Revelation, perhaps creation: scenes that are meant to look unlike the ordinary world could break into a drawn or painted style. A change of medium could become the series' visual language for "this is a vision," the same way a change of color does now.
- **Development is 2D anyway.** Storyboards and the Ruth animatic (ROADMAP §15.4) are drawings. So 2D gets used first, cheaply, whatever we choose for the finish.

## What it costs

These are rough figures as of October 2026, and prices are moving fast.

### Traditional studio rates

| Kind | Typical price per finished minute |
|---|---|
| 2D, TV quality | About $5,000–$15,000+ |
| 3D, high-end | About $15,000–$50,000+ |

So 3D usually costs about three to five times as much as 2D at comparable quality. For a 58-minute Ruth episode, that's very roughly **$300,000–$900,000 in 2D** and **$900,000–$3 million in 3D** at studio rates. Ruth needs few effects and could come in under that. Premium series cost far more. Sources: [B2W](https://www.b2w.tv/blog/how-much-does-animation-cost), [Prolific Studio](https://prolificstudio.co/blog/2d-vs-3d-animation-cost/).

### AI video generation

| Model (2026) | Price per generated second |
|---|---|
| Veo 3.1 (Google) | About $0.50 without audio, $0.75 with; a "Lite" tier near $0.03 |
| Sora 2 (OpenAI) | About $0.10 at 720p, more at higher tiers |
| Kling 3.0 | About $0.08–$0.17 |
| Runway Gen-4 / 4.5 | About $0.05–$0.12 |

Sources: [CometAPI](https://www.cometapi.com/ai-video-api-pricing/), [Atlas Cloud](https://atlascloud.ai/blog/guides/cheapest-ai-video-generation-api-2026), [ModelsLab](https://modelslab.com/blog/api/veo-3-1-vs-kling-3-sora-2-ai-video-api-cost-2026).

**2D or 3D makes almost no difference to this cost.** The model generates pixels either way; "hand-drawn" or "3D-rendered" is a matter of style references and prompts. A 58-minute episode is about 3,500 seconds. If each usable shot takes 10 to 20 tries, which is realistic today, the generation bill comes to **roughly $3,500–$35,000 per episode,** depending on the model. Most of the real cost is human work: editing, keeping characters consistent, voices, music and sound.

### What AI generation doesn't give you

- **No 3D world.** AI video that *looks* 3D isn't 3D. There are no sets, cameras or characters to reuse, so it doesn't carry over to live action the way a real 3D build does. It's still very useful for previsualization: working out what a shot should look like.
- **Consistency is the hard part.** Keeping Ruth's face, Naomi's house and Bethlehem's gate identical across a thousand shots is what current tools struggle with most.
- **Acting control.** Getting exactly the look on Naomi's face that you described (calculation, pain, exhaustion, acceptance, in that order) is far harder to direct in AI video than with an animator or an actor.
- **Ownership.** In the US, purely AI-generated material generally can't be copyrighted; human authorship is required. Your scripts, designs and edits are yours, but the generated frames may not be protected. That matters for a project meant to become a show. It's worth checking with a lawyer before production, because the rules are still developing.

### The hybrid that I think fits

Build the key characters and sets in 3D (Unreal or Blender), animate the performances, then use AI to restyle or finish the frames: painterly looks, skies, crowds, fire. It costs more up front than pure AI generation, but it gives you control, consistency, reuse and the live-action bridge. And it gets cheaper per episode as the asset library grows.

## Questions for our conversation

1. **Does the live-action plan still come first?** If it does, I think 3D is the answer. If the animation might *become* the final product, 2D deserves a much harder look.
2. **The painterly 3D look.** Do *Arcane* or *Spider-Verse* feel close to what you're imagining, or do you picture something more like *The Prince of Egypt*?
3. **Visions in a different medium.** Does the idea of the Bible's visions breaking into a painted or drawn style appeal to you?
4. **AI's role.** Are you planning to make it mostly with AI tools, mostly with people, or a mix? That changes the budget more than 2D versus 3D does.
