## The question

The Bible says "God said" hundreds of times. What does the audience actually see and hear each time: a voice, a person, a dream, a storm, a feeling, a prophet's mouth? And what does the answer say about how God relates to the world?

## Your view so far

God speaks through the world: through nature and through the people we meet. In Genesis 1, if the speech is literal, a "voice from nowhere" makes sense. Elsewhere, much of "God said" could be God speaking through a prophet. The Bible distinguishes the prophet's words from God's, but that doesn't mean they're always different things. Whether a hearer says "God spoke" or "the prophet spoke" may depend on how they experienced it. (Your full words are in [FOUNDATIONS §6](../canon/FOUNDATIONS.md#6-how-god-speaks).)

## What the texts say: the Bible describes many modes

The Bible doesn't describe divine speech as one thing. It describes at least nine modes, and it sometimes says so itself (Num 12:6–8, below).

| # | Mode | Key passages |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Through creation** | "The heavens declare the glory of God… Day to day pours out speech… their voice goes out through all the earth" (Ps 19:1–4). "His invisible attributes… have been clearly perceived… in the things that have been made" (Rom 1:20). God "did not leave himself without witness, for he did good by giving you rains from heaven and fruitful seasons" (Acts 14:17). |
| 2 | **Through prophets** | "I have put my words in your mouth" (Jer 1:9; Deut 18:18). The prophet speaks *as* God, in the first person: "Thus says the LORD: I…" God "spoke… by the prophets at many times and in many ways" (Heb 1:1); people "spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Pet 1:21). |
| 3 | **Through a human mouthpiece, in so many words** | God tells Moses that Aaron "shall be your mouth, and you shall be as God to him" (Exod 4:16), and "I have made you like God to Pharaoh, and Aaron your brother shall be your prophet" (Exod 7:1). |
| 4 | **Through unexpected people** | Abigail stops David from committing murder, and he answers, "Blessed be the LORD, who sent you this day to meet me!" (1 Sam 25:32–33). A pagan Pharaoh, Neco, warns King Josiah, and the text calls it "the words of Neco from the mouth of God" (2 Chr 35:21–22). Caiaphas prophesies without knowing it (John 11:49–52). A donkey speaks to Balaam (Num 22:28). |
| 5 | **Dreams and visions** | Jacob's ladder (Gen 28:12–15); Joseph's dreams (Gen 37); Joseph, husband of Mary (Matt 1:20); Daniel 7; Peter's vision (Acts 10:9–16). God says plainly, "If there is a prophet among you, I the LORD make myself known to him in a vision; I speak with him in a dream" (Num 12:6). |
| 6 | **Angels, and the angel of the LORD** | The angel of the LORD often speaks *as God*, and people realize afterward who it was: Hagar (Gen 16:7–13), Abraham at the binding of Isaac (Gen 22:11–18), the burning bush (Exod 3:2–6), Gideon (Judg 6:11–24), Samson's parents (Judg 13). The Law itself is said to have been delivered through angels (Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19; Heb 2:2). |
| 7 | **A quiet, inner voice** | After wind, earthquake and fire, "a sound of thin silence" (1 Kings 19:12). "Your ears shall hear a word behind you, saying, 'This is the way, walk in it'" (Isa 30:21). |
| 8 | **An audible voice that others hear** | At Sinai the people hear the voice and beg Moses to stand between them and God (Exod 20:18–19; Deut 4:12; 5:22–27). The voice at Jesus's baptism (Matt 3:17) and at the Transfiguration (Mark 9:7). In John 12:28–29 some hear thunder and others say an angel spoke. |
| 9 | **Face to face, and in a person** | With Moses God speaks "mouth to mouth, clearly, and not in riddles" (Num 12:8; Exod 33:11). Finally, "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14), and God "has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:2). |

## What supports your view

- **God speaking through the world and through people** is all over the text: modes 1, 3 and 4 above. Two passages match your belief almost word for word: "I was a stranger and you welcomed me" (Matt 25:35–40) and "some have entertained angels unawares" (Heb 13:2).
- **God speaking through the prophets' mouths** is what prophecy *is* (mode 2). In an oracle, the prophet's voice and God's speech really are the same event.
- **Your idea about perception has real textual support.** Several scenes depend on who perceived what, and when:
  - In John 12:29 the same sound is heard as thunder by some and as an angel by others.
  - Paul's companions hear a sound but don't understand the voice (Acts 9:7; 22:9).
  - The boy Samuel hears God's call and thinks it's the priest Eli calling from the next room (1 Sam 3:4–8).
  - Hagar, Gideon and Manoah each realize only *afterward* that it was God or the angel of the LORD (Gen 16:13; Judg 6:22; 13:21–22).
  - In Genesis 18, three "men" visit Abraham, and partway through, the narrative simply says "the LORD said" (18:13). The text itself blurs the line between the visitors and God.
  - On the road to Emmaus, the disciples walk all afternoon with Jesus before they recognize him (Luke 24:16, 31).

## What pulls against it, or limits it

- **Some scenes have no human there to speak through.** In Genesis 1 there are no humans yet. In Genesis 2–3 God speaks to Adam, Eve and the serpent. Abraham is called alone (Gen 12:1) and tested alone (Gen 22:1–2). Moses is alone at the burning bush (Exod 3). At Sinai the whole people hear the voice themselves, "out of the midst of the fire" (Deut 5:24). Your approach can't apply here as "a prophet speaking," though "through nature" or "the angel of the LORD" might.
- **The prophet is the second link in the chain, not the first.** "The word of the LORD came to Jeremiah" (Jer 1:4) happens *before* Jeremiah speaks. Even if God's speech reaches the people through the prophet, the moment the prophet receives it still has to be shown somehow.
- **Moses is set apart.** Numbers 12:6–8 says God speaks to prophets in visions and dreams, "not so with my servant Moses… mouth to mouth." The text insists Moses's experience was different in kind.
- **True and false prophets.** The Bible cares intensely about the difference (Deut 18:20–22; Jer 23:16; 28; Micaiah against the 400 prophets in 1 Kings 22). If God speaks through people, then so might people who only *claim* to speak for God. The story needs a way to show the difference. That's also a rich source of drama.
- **Seeing God.** "No one has ever seen God" (John 1:18), and "man shall not see me and live" (Exod 33:20). Yet "they saw the God of Israel" (Exod 24:10), Isaiah "saw the Lord" (Isa 6:1), Jacob says "I have seen God face to face" (Gen 32:30), and Exod 33:11 says God spoke with Moses "face to face, as a man speaks to his friend," nine verses before 33:20. The text holds this tension openly, and our depiction has to choose how to honor it.

## How other readers have seen it

- **Jewish tradition** distinguishes levels of prophecy. Moses is in a class of his own, and later sources speak of the *bat kol* ("daughter of a voice"), a lesser echo of divine speech after the prophets ended.
- **Christian theology** often distinguishes *general revelation* (God known through creation and conscience, as in Ps 19 and Rom 1) from *special revelation* (Scripture, prophecy, Christ). Your view puts more weight on the first than many traditions do, but both categories are mainstream.
- **Many Christian readers** see the angel of the LORD as an appearance of the Son before his incarnation (Positions Register, item 14). If so, God speaking "through a person" goes back long before Jesus.

## Ways to show it on screen

| Approach | How it works | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Match the mode to the text** | Read each "God said" for its cues and show it in the mode the text implies | Everywhere. This is the backbone. |
| **B. Recognized afterward** | God's word arrives through a stranger, an ordinary person or an event, and the character, and perhaps the audience, realizes only later | Gen 16, Gen 18, Judg 6, Judg 13, 1 Sam 25, Luke 24; Heb 13:2 |
| **C. The same moment, heard differently** | One event, shown through different listeners' ears | John 12:29; Acts 9 and 22; Sinai |
| **D. A voice from nowhere** | A voice with no visible source | Genesis 1; scenes where the text makes the voice audible |
| **E. The prophet as the voice** | The oracle comes out of the prophet's mouth, perhaps with a change in sound design to mark it as God's word | Every "Thus says the LORD" |
| **F. Through creation** | Wind, fire, storm, silence, light, carried by sound and image | Ps 19; 1 Kings 19; Job 38 (God speaks from the whirlwind) |

These can be combined. One scene might use A and B together: Abraham's three visitors are shown as men, and the audience realizes who has been speaking at the same moment Abraham does.

## Questions for our conversation

1. **Genesis 1.** Is "God said" literal speech, or the narrator's way of describing creation by God's will and word ("By the word of the LORD the heavens were made… he spoke, and it came to be," Ps 33:6, 9; John 1:3)? And if no one is there to hear it, who is the voice for?
2. **When God speaks to someone alone,** as with Abraham in Genesis 22, what do we see and hear?
3. **Unknowing speakers.** Does "God speaks through people" include people who don't know it, like Neco, Caiaphas or Balaam's donkey?
4. **True and false.** If both a true prophet and a false one are human voices, how does the audience tell them apart, and should it be able to at first?
5. **Moses.** Does he get a different treatment, as Numbers 12:8 insists?
6. **Seeing God.** Do we ever show God visibly, in the moments where the text says people saw him (Exod 24:10; Isa 6:1; Ezek 1:26–28; Dan 7:9)? If so, how do we honor John 1:18 at the same time?
7. **Your perception idea.** Does it apply in both directions? That is, could a prophet's words be experienced as God speaking, *and* God's direct speech be experienced as a voice coming through a person?

**1. Genesis 1.**

**2. When God speaks to someone alone.**

> Unsure as of now, but to me an alone encounter with God happens when your mind is completely clear (which is when the Holy Spirit within is unblocked). An alone encounter is a deep part of yourself telling you something you didn't know. I see minds as layered, and at the top-most layer is God.

*For it:* "a sound of thin silence" (1 Kgs 19:12). "Be still, and know that I am God" (Ps 46:10). "The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit" (Rom 8:16). "The spirit of man is the lamp of the LORD, searching all his innermost parts" (Prov 20:27). "The word is very near you. It is in your mouth and in your heart" (Deut 30:14). "In him we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:28).

*Against it,* three things:

- **"A deep part of yourself" is exactly what the Bible says false prophecy is.** "They speak visions of their own minds, not from the mouth of the LORD" (Jer 23:16). "Who prophesy from their own hearts... who follow their own spirit" (Ezek 13:2–3). "The heart is deceitful above all things" (Jer 17:9). And the word that comes is usually unwelcome. Moses argues with it for two chapters (Exod 3–4), Jonah runs from it, Jeremiah calls it a fire he can't hold in (Jer 20:9), and no part of Abraham wants what Genesis 22 asks.
- **Some encounters leave marks.** A bush burns. Jacob's hip is put out (Gen 32:25). Abraham's visitors eat a meal (Gen 18:8).
- **"The Holy Spirit within" is early.** In the Old Testament the Spirit comes *upon* particular people (Judg 6:34; 1 Sam 16:13), and John says that during Jesus's ministry "as yet the Spirit had not been given" (John 7:39). What everyone has from the start is the breath of God (Gen 2:7; Job 32:8).

*Where I land (mine):* your layered picture can hold all of this with one adjustment. The topmost layer is God, and it isn't the self. It can say what the rest of the person doesn't want and didn't know, and they can argue with it. **Tension, workable.** How it's staged is still open; see round 2.

**3. Unknowing speakers.**

*Supported.* Neco (2 Chr 35:21–22), Caiaphas (John 11:49–52), Balaam's donkey (Num 22:28), Assyria, which "does not so intend" (Isa 10:5–7), and Cyrus, "though you do not know me" (Isa 45:4–5). **Settled.**

**4. True and false prophets.**

> Unsure as of now, but I would say through the consequences of their prescriptions or actions. And I'd even want this to be layered. Sometimes you can't always tell whether the consequences would be bad or not, so you don't know.

*Supported; it's the Bible's own test.* "When a prophet speaks in the name of the LORD, if the word does not come to pass... that is a word that the LORD has not spoken" (Deut 18:22). "You will recognize them by their fruits" (Matt 7:16). Micaiah stakes everything on the outcome: "If you return in peace, the LORD has not spoken by me" (1 Kgs 22:28). And your layering has its scene. In Jeremiah 28 two prophets contradict each other in public, nobody can tell which is right, and even Jeremiah says "Amen! May the LORD do so" (28:6). The answer comes months later.

*One addition.* Deuteronomy 13:1–3 gives a second test: a sign can come true and the prophet still be false, if he leads people after other gods. So the fruit is also where the word leads, and not only whether it happens. **Settled as a working rule:** the audience isn't told who's true, and finds out the way the characters do.

**"Prophets are God a lot of the time."** The prophets and apostles are asked this, and every one of them refuses.

- At Lystra the crowd calls Paul and Barnabas gods. They tear their clothes: "We also are men, of like nature with you" (Acts 14:11–15).
- Peter to Cornelius: "Stand up; I too am a man" (Acts 10:26).
- The angel to John, twice: "You must not do that! I am a fellow servant" (Rev 19:10; 22:9).
- Herod accepts "the voice of a god, and not of a man" and is struck down (Acts 12:22–23).
- "My glory I give to no other" (Isa 42:8).

There's a real case on your side too. God tells Moses, "I have made you like God to Pharaoh" (Exod 7:1). Jesus himself quotes "I said, you are gods" and explains it: "he called them gods to whom the word of God came" (John 10:34–35). "It is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you" (Matt 10:20). "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me" (Gal 2:20). We "become partakers of the divine nature" (2 Pet 1:4). The Eastern church built a whole doctrine on these, *theosis*; Athanasius wrote that God became man so that we might become god.

The difference between the two lists is one word. The texts say prophets *share in* God, speak *as* God, are *filled with* God. They never say a prophet *is* God, and they treat that sentence as the worst thing a person can say about himself (Ezek 28:2). Christ is the one of whom it's said outright. **The version that fits: when a prophet speaks God's word, God is the one speaking. The prophet takes part in God and doesn't become him.** That keeps everything you want on screen: the oracle comes out of a human mouth and it is God's speech.

## Decision

**Decided for Genesis, 2026-10-05.**

1. **Genesis 1's "God said" isn't literal speech.** It stands for God's acts and thoughts, and nothing is spoken in the Opening.
2. **God speaks through people, including people who don't know it.**
3. **True and false prophets are told apart by their fruit,** in layers; the audience isn't told in advance and finds out the way the characters do.
4. **Perception works in both directions:** a prophet's words can be experienced as God speaking, and God's speech as a voice coming through a person.
5. **When a prophet speaks God's word, God is the one speaking.** The prophet takes part in God and doesn't become him.
6. **The separation is real as experience and never real as objective fact.**
7. **An encounter alone:** the world goes quiet, the Opening's held note returns, and the words come in the hearer's own voice, steadier than their own, able to say what they don't want to hear, and able to be argued with. Provisional until Abraham.

**Deferred:** how Moses is set apart (before the Exodus), and how the great visions are shown (before Isaiah, Ezekiel and Daniel).
