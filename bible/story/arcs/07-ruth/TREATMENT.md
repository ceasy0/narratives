> The whole episode told as a short story: what happens, how it's shaped, and what it means.

**Status:** v1 draft, 2026-10-01. This is step 2 of the [pilot plan](./README.md). **Checkpoint:** react to anything at all. This is the cheapest stage at which to change something big. The choices I most want your reaction to are in §7.

## 1. At a glance

**Logline.** A widow who has lost everything comes home with the one thing she doesn't count: a foreign daughter-in-law who won't leave her. Through one harvest, one night and one morning at the town gate, the house of bread fills again.

**Shape.** One episode of about 43 minutes: a prologue and four acts, one act for each chapter.

| Part | Passage | Where | Whose eyes | Length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prologue: The House of Bread | 1:1–2 | Bethlehem | Naomi's family | About 3 min |
| Act One: Full and Empty | 1:3–22 | Moab, the road, Bethlehem | Naomi | About 13 min |
| Act Two: The Field | 2 | Boaz's field, Naomi's house | Ruth | About 11 min |
| Act Three: The Threshing Floor | 3 | Naomi's house, the floor at night | Ruth | About 8 min |
| Act Four: The Gate | 4 | The gate, Naomi's house | Boaz, then Naomi | About 8 min |

**When.** About 1152–1139 BC, in the days of Eli ([dossier §5](./DOSSIER.md#5-when-chronology-and-rahab)). At the return, Naomi is about 48, Ruth about 26 and Boaz about 45.

**The spine.** Naomi goes from full to empty to full. The women of Bethlehem frame her story, from "Is this Naomi?" to "A son has been born to Naomi!" Ruth's loyalty drives it, and Boaz answers it.

**Voices.** Naomi and Boaz sound a little more formal than Ruth, as you decided. Ruth is plain and direct, with few words.

**Language.** In Moab, Moabites speak Moabite with subtitles and Naomi's family speaks English. Ruth speaks only Moabite until the road home, where her oath is the first thing she says in Naomi's language. In Bethlehem she speaks English with a slight accent. [P6·7]

**God** never speaks and works no visible miracle. He's present in bread, in "chance," in the blessings people speak, and in a child (§6).

**The ending.** The naming of Obed, a last image facing Moab, and the text's own last line on a card (4:17).

**Expected tier mix.** No Tier A, since God doesn't speak. About three-quarters of the lines are recorded (Tier B). The invented lines (Tier D) cluster in the Moab years and the field. The script will report exact counts.

## 2. Three ways in

You decided the episode opens in Bethlehem during the famine, with a glimpse of Boaz as the one who stays. Here are three ways to do it.

| | Opening | What it does well | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | **The empty threshing floor.** *My recommendation, written out in §3.* Dawn wind over a bare threshing floor; Boaz alone in his dead barley; the family packing; the gate; the road east. | It sets up Boaz's two places, the field and the floor, empty now so they can be full later. It puts the gate on screen, so Act Four comes back to it. And it shows Naomi "full" in a hungry year. | It's the longest of the three, at about three minutes. |
| **B** | **The last meal.** The family's last supper in Bethlehem: one thin loaf split four ways, the decision to go, the boys' jokes. Boaz appears only as they pass his field at dawn. | It's the most intimate. The more we love this family, the harder their deaths land. | It needs a dialogue scene for Elimelech and the sons, whom the text never lets speak. |
| **C** | **The view east.** Naomi on her roof before dawn, looking across the rift: Bethlehem brown with drought, rain falling on the mountains of Moab. Then the departure, and Boaz in his field. | It's the most economical, and it makes the view across the rift the frame of the episode, since the last image repeats it. | We see less of the family, and the field and the floor get less setup. |

A ends on C's view, so it keeps C's best idea.

## 3. The story

### Prologue — The House of Bread

*Ruth 1:1–2 · about 3 minutes*

Black. A card: *In the days when the judges ruled.*

Dawn on the ridge east of Bethlehem. A threshing floor, a circle of packed earth worn smooth by generations of oxen, lies bare in what should be the middle of harvest. The wind comes up off the desert, finds nothing to winnow, lifts a skin of dust and old chaff, and drops it again.

On the terraces below, the barley stands stunted and white in cracked ground. A man walks the rows alone: **Boaz**, in his early thirties, a landowner with work-hardened hands. He rubs a head of barley between his palms and blows. Nothing but husk. A woman comes down the terrace with a water jar, his **wife**, and they share one mouthful each. Neither speaks, and we don't learn his name yet. **[P6·1] [P6·2]**

In town, in a courtyard, a family is loading a donkey. **Elimelech**, about forty, cinches the straps. **Naomi** packs what's left of a household into baskets and won't let anyone help. Their sons are **Mahlon**, seventeen, thin, with a cough he keeps swallowing, and **Chilion**, fifteen, who raps his knuckles on an empty storage jar so it booms.

"House of bread," Chilion says. **[P6·3]**

Naomi swats him, and laughs despite herself. Whatever else this family is short of, it isn't short of each other. That's what to see now, because years from now Naomi will say *I went away full,* and we should know exactly what she meant.

They leave by the town gate at sunrise. Two old men on the elders' bench witness Elimelech handing a neighbor the right to work his field, in exchange for a little silver for the road. "Until we come back," Elimelech says. **[P4·4]** Naomi watches the silver being weighed.

On the road east they pass the dead terraces. The man in the field straightens and lifts a hand, and Elimelech lifts his. They're kinsmen making two different choices, and the film judges neither. Naomi looks back once.

From the ridge, the land falls away into desert. Beyond the great trench of the Dead Sea, the mountains of Moab stand blue, with a grey veil of rain hanging over them. **[P6·5]** The four of them walk toward it until they're too small to see.

### Act One — Full and Empty

*Ruth 1:3–22 · about 13 minutes*

**Moab.** The plateau is green, because it has rained here. Elimelech's family settles in a village on the northern plateau as *gerim*, resident foreigners, which is the status Ruth will have in Bethlehem. A Moabite farmer takes them on as hired hands. **[P6·6]** His daughter, **Ruth**, about fourteen, watches the strangers arrive from the edge of her father's threshing floor.

From here on, Moabites speak Moabite, with subtitles, and Naomi's family speaks English. The two languages were close enough for each side to understand the other, so conversations run in both at once. Ruth speaks only Moabite, even to Naomi, until the road home. **[P6·7]**

Moab's religion is shown the way its people lived it, sincerely. **[P6·8]** Ruth's mother pours oil before a small stone figure of Chemosh in a niche of the house. The village climbs to its high place to give thanks for the rain. Naomi's family eats bread that Moab thanks Chemosh for, and nobody remarks on it.

Within a year, a fever takes Elimelech. **[P4·9]** Ruth's family mourns him the way Moab mourns: wailing on the rooftops, heads shaved, blades drawn across their forearms (Isa 15:2–3; Jer 48:37). Naomi mourns the way Bethlehem does. She tears her dress and sits in the dust with her sons. **[P6·10]** That night Ruth sees Naomi alone in the courtyard, praying, and there's no image in front of her. Ruth watches from the dark for a long time. **[P6·11]**

**The weddings.** A few years later, Naomi, now head of the household, marries her sons to Moabite girls: Mahlon to Ruth, and Chilion to **Orpah**. **[P6·12]** There are drums, a feast and Moabite songs, and Naomi dances. The text never condemns these marriages, and neither do we.

**Ten years** pass in a handful of images. Ruth and Naomi work the grinding stone and the bread oven in rhythm, laughing. Naomi teaches Ruth a Bethlehem harvest song. Mahlon's cough gets a little worse every winter, and Ruth nurses him. She loves him, and the marriage is real. Next door, Chilion and Orpah are happy more noisily. And there are no children in either house. Ruth's mother presses a little clay goddess into Ruth's hands, and Ruth prays to it with her whole heart. Naomi prays in her courtyard. Neither prayer is answered. **[P4·13]**

**The fever year.** One winter a fever runs through the village and takes both brothers. **[P4·14]** Now there are three graves side by side in Moabite ground. At Mahlon's grave, Ruth's mother cuts her own arms, then Ruth's, in the way of Moab, and Ruth lets her. **[P6·15]** Naomi tears her clothes again and sits in the dust. Of everything she brought from Bethlehem, only she is left. At this point the text stops using her name: she's simply "the woman" (1:5).

**Bread.** A trader from Judah comes through the market with news: "Yahweh has visited his people. There's bread in Bethlehem." **[C·16]** Naomi goes home and starts to pack (1:6).

**The parting.** Three women leave the village at dawn with everything they own on their backs: Naomi, Ruth and Orpah. Ruth's mother stands at the last house and watches them go, as Boaz once watched Elimelech's family go. **[P6·17]** Ruth looks back once.

The road climbs to the edge of the plateau, where it drops toward the Jordan valley. The whole land lies open across the rift in the morning light: the river, the salt sea, the hills of Judah. Somewhere on those hills is Bethlehem. **[P6·18]** Naomi stops. She has rehearsed this all night (1:8–9):

"Go back. Both of you, go home to your mothers. May Yahweh be as good to you as you've been to the dead and to me. May Yahweh give each of you rest, in a husband's home."

She kisses them, and they cry aloud. Then, together, in Moabite: "No. We're going back with you, to your people" (1:10).

Naomi's answer is the sharpest speech in the book, and we play it at full strength: grief so heavy it turns into a bitter joke (1:11–13).

"Go back, my daughters. Why come with me? Am I going to have more sons for you to marry? Go home. I'm too old for a husband. And even if I said there was hope — even if I had a husband tonight and had sons — would you wait until they grew up? Stay alone all those years for them? No, my daughters. This is far more bitter for me than for you. Yahweh's hand has turned against me."

They weep again. Orpah kisses Naomi, a real goodbye, holds Ruth's face in her hands for a moment, and walks back the way they came. She turns once to look. She has done exactly what Naomi asked, and the film never blames her.

Ruth hasn't moved. She has hold of Naomi and won't let go. The Hebrew word for it is the one Genesis uses for a husband clinging to his wife (1:14; Gen 2:24).

"Look," Naomi says. "Your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and her gods. Go after her" (1:15).

And Ruth answers, for the first time, in Naomi's language (1:16–17):

"Don't ask me to leave you, or to turn back from following you. Where you go, I'll go. Where you stay, I'll stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I'll die, and that's where I'll be buried. May Yahweh do that to me, and worse, if anything but death comes between us."

It's the first time we hear Ruth speak our language and the first time we hear her say the Name, and she says it to swear an oath. Naomi looks at her for a long time and says nothing more (1:18).

**The road.** They go down into the rift without a word between them. **[P4·19]** There's the descent and the heat; the plains where Israel once camped opposite Jericho; the Jordan, waist-deep and brown; the ruined mound of Jericho; then the long climb through the wilderness. Ruth carries the heavier load and finds the water, and on the last slopes she gets Naomi up the hill. For days, Naomi doesn't speak.

On the last morning they pass a weathered standing stone by the road: the pillar over Rachel's grave (Gen 35:19–20). **[P4·20]** Ruth doesn't know what it is. Naomi touches it as she passes.

**Bethlehem.** The house of bread has bread again. The barley stands gold on the terraces, the same terraces we saw dead in the prologue. Ruth can't know whose they are, but we do.

The whole town is stirred. The women at the well stop what they're doing and stare. "Is this Naomi?" (1:19)

Naomi's lament lifts toward a higher register (1:20–21). The names have to land for an audience without Hebrew, so she says what they mean:

"Don't call me Naomi. Don't call me sweet. Call me Mara. Call me bitter. Shaddai has made my life so bitter. I went away full, and Yahweh has brought me back empty. Why call me Naomi, when Yahweh has testified against me, and Shaddai has brought this on me?"

She says *empty* with Ruth standing right beside her, and the camera finds Ruth's face as she hears it. Nobody introduces the foreigner. The women look at her the way the town will look at her for weeks: as *the Moabite.*

Naomi's old house is a ruin. **[P6·21]** The roof has fallen in, and thistles fill the courtyard. Naomi sits down in the middle of it. Ruth sets down the bundles and starts clearing stones.

Out on the terraces, the evening light falls on ripe barley. It's the beginning of the barley harvest (1:22).

### Act Two — The Field

*Ruth 2 · about 11 minutes*

Next morning (2:2):

"Let me go out to the fields," Ruth says, "and glean behind anyone who'll let me."

"Go, my daughter." It's all Naomi has in her.

The fields at sunrise: young men cutting with sickles, young women binding sheaves behind them, and behind them the gleaners, the widows and orphans and the poorest people in town. Ruth asks the young foreman if she can gather among the sheaves, which is more than gleaners usually get. He tells her to glean behind the reapers like everyone else, because the sheaves are the master's business. **[P4·22]**

The work is brutal. The other gleaners don't make room for her. A reaper flicks a stalk at her. Another knocks her basket over, and the young men laugh while she picks up every grain. **[P6·23]** Two gleaners talk about her loudly enough for her to hear. **[P6·24]**

"A Moabite, gleaning in Bethlehem. Let her glean in Moab."

An older woman answers without looking up. "You're in Boaz's field. He's a man of worth. And his own family comes from Rahab."

"Rahab the prostitute?"

"Rahab of Jericho. And you're eating her family's barley, so mind your mouth."

"And look" (2:4): Boaz comes out from town. It's the man from the dead field, twelve years older, with grey in his beard. His first words in the story are a blessing.

"Yahweh be with you!"

"Yahweh bless you!" the reapers call back.

He notices her at once (2:5–7). "Whose girl is that?"

The foreman is careful and a little defensive. "She's the Moabite girl, the one who came back with Naomi from Moab. She asked to gather among the sheaves behind the reapers. She's been on her feet since morning. She's hardly sat down in the shelter."

Boaz goes over to her (2:8–13). Stay in this field, he tells her, close to my young women. I've told the young men not to touch you. When you're thirsty, drink from the jars.

She goes down on her face in the stubble. "Why would you notice me? I'm a foreigner." In Hebrew, *notice* and *foreigner* sound alike, and the script should look for an English equivalent.

"I've heard everything you've done for your mother-in-law since your husband died: how you left your father and your mother and the land you were born in, and came to a people you didn't know. May Yahweh repay you for it. May you be paid in full by Yahweh, the God of Israel, under whose wings you've come to take shelter."

He has no idea he's describing what he'll do himself.

"You've comforted me," she says. "You've spoken to my heart, and I'm not even one of your servants."

At the midday meal (2:14), in front of everyone, Boaz calls her over to dip her bread in the sour wine, and hands her roasted grain himself. She eats until she's full, which is the first full meal in the story, and folds what's left into her shawl. When she goes back to work, Boaz quietly gives his orders (2:15–16). Let her glean even among the sheaves, and don't shame her. Pull stalks out of the bundles and leave them for her, and don't scold her. The reaper who knocked over her basket gets the job. **[P6·25]**

At evening she beats out what she's gathered: an ephah, about thirty pounds of barley, an absurd amount for one day (2:17). She shoulders it and carries it home through town at dusk, and the town watches her go by.

Naomi sees the sack and gets to her feet (2:18–22). "Where did you glean today? Where did you work? Blessed is the man who noticed you!" Ruth gives her the grain she saved from lunch. Then she says, "The man I worked with today — his name is Boaz."

Something wakes up in Naomi. "Yahweh bless him! He hasn't stopped being kind, to the living or the dead!" Is that Yahweh's kindness, or Boaz's? The Hebrew leaves it open, and so does her delivery. "That man is close family. He's one of our redeemers."

Ruth doesn't know the word, so Naomi explains it the way you would to a foreigner. When family loses something, whether it's land or freedom, family has the right to buy it back. **[P6·26]**

Ruth warms up. "He even told me, 'Stay close to my young men until they've finished my whole harvest.'"

"His young women, my daughter," Naomi says drily. "Better that you go out with his young women, so nobody bothers you in somebody else's field."

**Seven weeks** pass, through the barley harvest and then the wheat (2:23). **[P6·27]** The young women stop staring at Ruth the day she joins in one of their harvest songs: Naomi taught it to her in Moab. One of them mentions that Boaz has been alone since the famine. The house gets a roof. The jars fill up. Naomi starts cooking again. The cuts on Ruth's arms heal pale. And Boaz is always kind, always correct, and always at a distance. He watches her across the field at the meal, and looks away when she looks up. He never comes closer than that. **[P4·28]**

Naomi notices that he doesn't. On the last day of the wheat harvest, she watches Ruth asleep by the lamp and makes up her mind.

### Act Three — The Threshing Floor

*Ruth 3 · about 8 minutes*

"My daughter," Naomi says, "isn't it time I found you a place to rest, where things will go well for you?" (3:1) It's the "rest" she wished for her daughters-in-law on the road (1:9).

Tonight, she tells her, Boaz is winnowing barley on the threshing floor (3:2–4). Wash. Put on oil, and your cloak. Go down, and don't let him see you until he's finished eating and drinking. Notice where he lies down. Then go in, uncover his feet, and lie down. "He'll tell you what to do."

"I'll do everything you say," Ruth answers.

Naomi's hands shake as she combs Ruth's hair. **[P4·29]** She knows what she's sending Ruth into: a threshing floor at night, men and wine, and a reputation that can't be won back once it's lost (Hos 9:1). If she's wrong about Boaz, she has ruined her. She does it anyway, out of love and need at once.

The washing and the oil end Ruth's mourning (2 Sam 12:20). Ruth takes off the widow's clothes she has worn since Moab and rubs oil into her arms, over the healed cuts.

At sunset, the threshing floor from the prologue is heaped with grain. Men toss forkfuls into the evening wind, and the chaff streams away gold while the grain falls. Boaz eats and drinks with his workers, laughing, and his heart is content (3:7). When the work is done, the men sleep around the floor, and Boaz lies down at the far end of the grain pile.

Ruth waits in the dark below the floor. A worker gets up and walks a few paces toward where she's hiding, relieves himself, and goes back to sleep. She waits longer. **[P6·30]**

Then she comes quietly, uncovers his feet, and lies down.

At midnight the cold wakes him. He jerks up and twists around, and there's a woman lying at his feet (3:8–9).

"Who are you?"

"I'm Ruth, your servant. Spread your wing over your servant. You're a redeemer."

She was told he would tell her what to do. Instead she tells him. And she adds something nobody asked her to say: *you're a redeemer.* She's tying her own marriage to the rescue of Naomi's family.

This is the most charged scene in the story, and the text means it to be: the dark, the wine, the bare feet, the word *lie down* again and again. We play the closeness at full strength, with breath, the scent of the oil and warmth against the cold, and nothing explicit (principle 8). What resolves it is Boaz (3:10–13):

"Yahweh bless you, my daughter. This kindness is even greater than the first: you haven't gone after the young men, poor or rich. Now don't be afraid. I'll do everything you ask. The whole town knows you're a woman of worth."

*Worth* is the word the old gleaner used of him. **[P5·31]** Then comes the catch:

"It's true that I'm a redeemer, but there's one closer than I am. Stay here tonight. In the morning, if he'll redeem you, good, let him. If he won't, then as Yahweh lives, I'll redeem you myself. Lie down until morning."

They lie awake the rest of the night, an arm's length apart, under the stars, in the wind. He doesn't spread his cloak over her. Not yet: until the morning settles who has the right, it isn't his to spread. **[P6·32]**

Before light, before anyone could recognize anyone, he says: "No one can know a woman came to the floor" (3:14–15). He has her hold out her shawl, pours six measures of barley into it, lifts it onto her back, and goes up into town.

Naomi has been awake all night. "How did it go, my daughter?" (3:16) The Hebrew says, literally, *Who are you, my daughter?*, echoing Boaz's question at midnight. Ruth tells her everything. Then: "He gave me these six measures of barley. He said, 'Don't go back to your mother-in-law empty'" (3:17).

*Empty* is Naomi's own word from the day she came home. We never heard Boaz say it on the floor; as in the text, it reaches us first in Ruth's mouth. Naomi has to sit down.

"Sit still, my daughter, until you see how it falls out. The man won't rest until he's settled it — today" (3:18).

### Act Four — The Gate

*Ruth 4 · about 8 minutes*

Boaz goes up to the gate and sits down on the elders' bench, where Elimelech once handed over his field. "And look" (4:1): the nearer redeemer comes walking by, right on cue. Boaz calls him by name, and a donkey brays right over it. **[P6·33]** The man who won't keep a dead man's name alive doesn't get his own name kept, either.

Boaz seats ten elders and lays it out (4:3–4). Naomi, back from Moab, is selling the piece of land that belonged to our brother Elimelech. You have the first right. Redeem it if you will, and if not, say so, because I'm next in line.

The man brightens. Land is land. "I'll redeem it."

"On the day you buy the field from Naomi," Boaz says, "you also acquire Ruth the Moabite, the dead man's wife, to keep the dead man's name alive on his land" (4:5).

*Moabite* lands on the crowd like a stone. The man's face does the arithmetic: the price, a field that will pass to a son who counts as Mahlon's, and two more mouths to feed. "Then I can't redeem it. I'd be ruining my own inheritance. You take my right. I can't do it" (4:6).

He pulls off his sandal and hands it to Boaz (4:7–8). A boy in the crowd asks his father why. The land is yours where your foot walks, the father tells him, so he's handing over his walking. **[P6·34]** The text stops to explain the custom too.

Boaz stands and speaks to everyone (4:9–10). He has bought from Naomi everything that belonged to Elimelech, Chilion and Mahlon. And he has acquired Ruth the Moabite, Mahlon's widow, as his wife, to keep the dead man's name alive, so that it isn't cut off from his family or from the gate of his town. "You are witnesses today."

"We are witnesses," the gate answers, and blesses him (4:11–12). May Yahweh make the woman coming into his house like Rachel and Leah, who built the house of Israel: Rachel, whose pillar Ruth passed on the road. May he do worthily in Ephrathah and make a name in Bethlehem. And may his house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah. Tamar was another woman who took a risk to keep a dead man's name alive (Gen 38).

At the house, Ruth can't sit still. Naomi grinds grain without looking up, too hard. Then a shadow fills the doorway: Boaz, with a sandal in his hand. He doesn't need to say anything. He looks at Naomi, then at Ruth. **[P6·35]**

**The wedding** (4:13). There's music in Bethlehem and a feast, and at the center of it Boaz spreads the corner of his cloak over Ruth. It's the wing she asked for on the threshing floor, and the shelter he once asked Yahweh to give her (2:12; 3:9; Ezek 16:8). **[P6·36]** Later, behind a closed door, a lamp goes out. From here on, nobody calls her "the Moabite." In the text, too, she's simply Ruth from now on (4:13).

**"Yahweh gave her conception"** (4:13). After ten childless years, Ruth is pregnant within months. We see Naomi's hand on Ruth's belly, and Naomi's face.

**The birth** comes a year after Ruth arrived, at the start of the next barley harvest. **[P6·37]** Ruth is on the birthing stones (Exod 1:16), with the midwives and the women of Bethlehem around her and Naomi gripping her hands. It's long and it's dangerous: Rachel died giving birth on the road outside this town. We don't look away. Boaz waits outside the door. Then there's a cry.

The women turn to Naomi, the same women who asked "Is this Naomi?" (4:14–15):

"Blessed be Yahweh, who hasn't left you without a redeemer today! May his name be famous in Israel. He'll bring your life back and take care of you when you're old, because your daughter-in-law who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has given birth to him."

*Better than seven sons.* Ruth hears it the way she heard *empty.* Naomi looks at her for a long moment, and this time she sees her. **[P6·38]**

Naomi takes the boy, lays him in her lap, and becomes his nurse (4:16). The neighbor women name him: "A son has been born to Naomi!" They call him Obed (4:17).

**The last image.** **[P6·39]** Dusk on the roof. Naomi holds Obed and looks east across the rift, to where the mountains of Moab are turning blue and her husband and sons are buried. Below her, the barley is ripening on Boaz's terraces. The wind comes up off the desert. Black.

A card: *Obed was the father of Jesse, the father of David* (4:17).

## 4. Additions register

Every item passes all five parts of the addition test (ROADMAP §4.3): it's **consistent** with the text and our decisions, **plausible**, **purposeful**, **subordinate** and **recognizable**. The table records the reasoning behind the two that most often fail, purpose and plausibility. Items marked **your call** are the ones in §7.

The biggest block of invention is the Moab years (items 6–17), at about seven minutes. Everything in that block exists to earn the oath and the losses, so it serves the text's climax rather than competing with it.

### Prologue

| # | Addition | Level | Why it's there | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Boaz glimpsed in his dead field, silent | P6 | Your decision to glimpse the one who stays. Keeping him silent saves his first words for the text's, a blessing (2:4). It also sets up the field Ruth "happens" into. | A landowner staying with his land. He's of Elimelech's clan (2:1, 3). |

## 5. Threads

- **Empty and full.** The bare floor and the dead field (prologue); "I went away full" (1:21); the ephah of barley (2:17); "don't go back empty" (3:17); "better than seven sons" (4:15); the house rebuilt.
- **Three views across the rift.** Bethlehem toward Moab (prologue), Moab toward Judah (the parting), and Bethlehem toward Moab again (the last image).
- **Boaz's places.** The field, the floor and the gate are each seen empty or ordinary first, then full or decisive.
- **The mirrors (FR-08, never spoken).** The one who stayed and the one who came. Opposite in age, wealth, people and standing, and alike in kindness. "Worth" (2:1, through the gleaner; 3:11). The wing (2:12; 3:9; the wedding).
- **Names.** Bethlehem, Naomi and Mara, Mr. So-and-so, "the dead man's name" (4:5, 10), Obed. And "the Moabite," which drops away after the gate, as it does in the text.
- **Language.** Ruth's switch at the oath, and her accent afterward.
- **First words.** As in the text (dossier §8), Boaz's first words are a blessing (2:4), and Ruth's first words in Naomi's language are an oath (1:16–17). The men of Naomi's family, whom the text never lets speak, get a single line apiece at most.

## 6. How God appears

- **He never speaks, and he works no visible miracle.** There's no Tier A in this episode.
- **The narrator's two statements that God acted** (1:6; 4:13) are carried by people: the trader's "Yahweh has visited his people," and the women's "Blessed be Yahweh" (4:14).
- **"Chance" (2:3) and "And look" (2:4; 4:1)** are shot the same way: ordinary moments that the camera holds a beat longer than it needs to.
- **Blessings come true through the people who speak them.** Naomi wishes her daughters-in-law "rest" (1:9) and then finds it for Ruth (3:1). Boaz prays for Yahweh's "wings" over her (2:12) and becomes the answer (3:9; the wedding).
- **Naomi's lament stands at full strength.** The answer comes through events, not argument.
- **Optional: the wind.** The same desert wind blows over the bare floor in the prologue, winnows Boaz's grain in Act Three, and comes up again in the last image. In Hebrew the word for wind, *ruach*, is also the word for spirit. Nobody remarks on it. It fits your view that God speaks through the world (Discussion 001), but it's only atmosphere, and dropping it loses nothing.

## 7. Choices for you

My defaults are in bold. Everything else in §3 is open too.

1. **The opening** (§2): **A, ending on C's view.**
2. **Ruth's language** (7): **Moabite until the oath, then English with a slight accent.** The alternative is English throughout, with only the accent.
3. **Boaz a widower** (2, 27): **yes.** In your framing, his first marriage is like Ruth's: real, unfinished, the barrier the water flows around. The alternative is to leave it unmentioned and unseen.
4. **Ruth's mourning cuts** (15): **yes.** The alternatives are a shaved head, which grows out over the seven weeks, or no mark at all.
5. **Rahab's family memory** (24): **one line from a gleaner.** The alternatives are Naomi telling Ruth during the seven weeks, or keeping it off screen.
6. **The parting and the road** (18, 19): **the edge of the plateau near home, and the road by Jericho.**
7. **Mr. So-and-so** (33): **the donkey, once.** The alternative is Boaz calling him "friend," with the credits making the joke.
8. **The ending** (39): **the last image facing Moab, then the 4:17 card.** If we add your montage of glimpses of the future later, it goes between the naming and the card.

## 8. Problems I found

No contradictions turned up. These are the places where the text is silent, ambiguous or unusual, and what the treatment does about each.

| Issue | What the text does | What the treatment does |
|---|---|---|
| Barley on the floor after the wheat harvest | Ruth gleans through the wheat harvest (2:23), but then Boaz is winnowing barley (3:2). | Threshing and winnowing went on through the summer, after the reaping, so the last of the barley can still be on the floor. |
| Boaz's marriage | Never says whether he had a wife | Widowed in the famine years (item 2) |
| How Naomi has land to sell | 4:3 assumes she has a claim but never explains it. | Elimelech handed over the field's use before leaving (item 4). |
| The men never speak | Elimelech, Mahlon and Chilion have no lines. | One line each for Elimelech and Chilion. Mahlon is all presence. |
| Moabites and the assembly | Deuteronomy 23:3 bars Moabites from "the assembly of Yahweh," yet Ruth marries in and becomes David's great-grandmother. The book never mentions that law. | Neither do we. The crowd's reaction to "Ruth the Moabite" (4:5) carries the tension without a debate. |
| Rachel's tomb | Genesis puts it near Bethlehem (35:19; 48:7), but 1 Samuel 10:2 puts it on Benjamin's border. | We follow Genesis (item 20). |
| Tamar's people | Genesis 38 never says where Tamar was from. | We call her a woman who took a risk to keep a dead man's name alive, not a foreigner. |
| Naomi's name | The pun on Naomi and Mara is lost without Hebrew. | She glosses the names in her own line ("Don't call me sweet"). That's a small expansion of a recorded line, to settle when we write the script. |
| Moabite dialogue | Moabite has no living descendant. It's reconstructed from the Mesha Stele and Biblical Hebrew. | Low confidence. A handful of short lines, each flagged for specialist review |
| A famine in Gideon's "rest" | Our chronology puts the famine inside Gideon's forty years of rest (Judg 8:28). | That's rest from enemies, not from weather (dossier §5). |
| Shamgar and the Philistines | Our arrangement has Shamgar fighting Philistines a few decades before their main settlement. | Not a problem for Ruth. It's flagged in [TIMELINE §9](../../../world/TIMELINE.md#9-updated-names-and-period-flags) for the Judges arc. |
