## 1. At a glance

**Logline.** A widow who has lost everything comes home with the one thing she doesn't count: a foreign daughter-in-law who won't leave her. Through one harvest, one night and one morning at the town gate, the house of bread fills again.

**Shape.** One episode of about 55 minutes: a prologue and four acts, one act for each chapter. It has grown from about 43 minutes, because your notes opened up the prologue and Moab.

| Part | Passage | Where | Whose eyes | Length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prologue: The House of Bread | 1:1–2 | Bethlehem | Naomi's family | About 5 min |
| Act One: Full and Empty | 1:3–22 | Moab, the road, Bethlehem | Naomi and Ruth | About 20 min |
| Act Two: The Field | 2 | Boaz's field, Naomi's house | Ruth | About 12 min |
| Act Three: The Threshing Floor | 3 | Naomi's house, the floor at night | Ruth | About 9 min |
| Act Four: The Gate | 4 | The gate, Naomi's house | Boaz, then Naomi | About 9 min |

**When.** About 1152–1139 BC, in the days of Eli ([dossier §5](./DOSSIER.md#5-when-chronology-and-rahab)). At the return, Naomi is about 48, Ruth about 26 and Boaz about 45.

**The spine.** Naomi goes from full to empty to full. The women of Bethlehem frame her story, from "Is this Naomi?" to "A son has been born to Naomi!" Ruth's loyalty drives it, and Boaz answers it.

**Voices.** Naomi and Boaz sound a little more formal than Ruth. Ruth is plain and direct, with few words.

**Language.** Everyone speaks English. Moabites, Ruth among them, speak it with a Moabite accent (ROADMAP §5.7).

**God** never speaks and works no visible miracle. He's present in bread, in "chance," in the blessings people speak, in a child, and perhaps in a wind (§5).

**The ending.** The naming of Obed, a last image facing Moab, and a fade to black.

**Expected tier mix.** No Tier A, since God doesn't speak. A little over half the lines are recorded (Tier B). Most of the rest are invented (Tier D), concentrated in the prologue and the Moab years, which your notes asked to open up. The script will report exact counts.

## 2. The story

### Prologue — The House of Bread

*Ruth 1:1–2 · about 5 minutes*

Fade in from black.

Dawn on the ridge east of Bethlehem. A threshing floor, a circle of packed earth worn smooth by generations of oxen, lies bare in what should be the middle of harvest. The wind comes up off the desert, finds nothing to winnow, lifts a skin of dust and old chaff, and drops it again.

On the terraces below, the barley stands stunted and white in cracked ground. A man walks the rows: **Boaz**, in his early thirties, a landowner with work-hardened hands. He rubs a head of barley between his palms and blows. Nothing but husk. His **wife** comes down the terrace with a water jar, and they share one mouthful each. **[P6·1]**

In town, in a courtyard, a family is packing a donkey. **Elimelech**, about forty, cinches the straps. **Naomi** packs what's left of a household into baskets and won't let anyone help. Their sons are **Mahlon**, seventeen, thin, with a cough he keeps swallowing, and **Chilion**, fifteen. Chilion lifts the lid of the bread bin, peers in for a long, theatrical moment, and announces to the courtyard: "House of bread." **[P6·2]**

"Every day," Mahlon says, without looking up from the rope he's tying. "You say it every single day."

"Because every day it's still true."

"It wasn't funny the first time." Mahlon coughs, which spoils it.

"Enough," says Elimelech. "Both of you. Mahlon, the rope. Chilion, it's empty. It was empty yesterday. Stop opening it."

Naomi flicks Chilion with a cloth on her way past, and once her back is to them, she's laughing. Whatever this family is short of, it isn't short of each other. That's what to see now, because years from now Naomi will say *I went away full,* and we should know exactly what she meant.

In the street, the neighbors watch the donkey go by, some of them sorry and some disapproving. Naomi's oldest friend catches her at the corner and presses a twist of dried figs into her hands. In a famine, that's no small gift. **[P6·3]**

"Moab, Naomi?"

"There's bread in Moab."

"There's Chemosh in Moab."

"Then Chemosh can feed us until it rains here."

They hold each other a moment too long. "Come back," says the friend. Naomi doesn't promise.

At the town gate, two elders sit as witnesses while Elimelech sells the use of his field to a neighbor, for a little silver for the road. **[P4·4]**

"That field's worth five times that," Naomi says.

"In a famine," the neighbor says, "a field's worth what somebody will pay. Nobody's paying."

Elimelech takes the silver. One of the elders speaks the formula for the witnesses: the field goes back to its family when they return, or when a kinsman buys it back. "Until we come back," Elimelech says.

On the road east, they pass the dead terraces. Boaz comes down to meet them with a half-full sack of barley on his shoulder, last year's, from his own store, and his wife walks behind him. **[P6·5]**

"God go with you, Elimelech."

Elimelech looks at the sack and shakes his head. He knows how little Boaz has left. Boaz holds it out anyway, and Naomi takes it. "Thank you, Boaz."

The two men stand there a moment: the one who's staying, and the one who's going.

"You think I'm wrong," says Elimelech.

"I think you have two sons."

There's no judgment in it either way, and a hint, for later, that Boaz has none.

Chilion eyes the sack. "Is that barley?" Mahlon elbows him.

From the top of the ridge, the land falls away east into desert. Beyond the great trench of the Dead Sea, the mountains of Moab stand blue, with a grey veil of rain hanging over them. **[P6·6]** Naomi looks back once at Bethlehem. Then the four of them walk toward the rain until they're too small to see.

Fade to black.

### Act One — Full and Empty

*Ruth 1:3–22 · about 20 minutes*

**The village.** The plateau is green, because it has rained here. Elimelech's family comes up the road into a Moabite village on the northern plateau, four dusty strangers and a donkey. **[P4·7]** The men on the threshing floor stop working. One of them says it loud enough to carry: "Israelites." Another spits. An old man reminds everyone what Israel's grandfathers did to Moab: their king Eglon, stabbed in his own upstairs room by an Israelite, and ten thousand Moabites killed at the fords of the Jordan (Judg 3:15–30). **[P6·8]** The Israelites have their own memory, of a people who wouldn't give them bread or water on the road out of Egypt (Deut 23:4). Nobody says that one out loud.

The man who owns the floor takes them on anyway, on hard terms. They'll work his fields for a share of the crop and sleep in a half-collapsed house at the edge of the village. Naomi bristles, and Elimelech accepts. The farmer is **Ruth's father**. He isn't cruel. He's a man who knows a bargain when one walks up his road.

Two girls are watching from the edge of the floor: **Ruth**, about fourteen, and her cousin **Orpah**, who lives in the same house. **[P6·9]** Orpah has opinions about the foreign boys. Ruth is watching their mother.

**Ruth's house.** That evening we stay with Ruth. **[P6·10]** Her mother pours oil before a small stone figure of Chemosh in a niche in the wall and asks him, plainly and sincerely, to keep the rain coming. At supper, the foreigners are all anyone talks about.

"You've let Israelites onto our land," says Ruth's brother.

"I've let cheap hands onto our land," says her father.

"They won't sacrifice to Chemosh," says her mother. "Their god doesn't even have a face."

"Then how do they pray to him?" Ruth asks.

"Badly," her mother says. "Look how hungry they are."

Everyone laughs except Ruth.

**A funeral.** A few months later, an old man in the village dies: Orpah's grandfather. The village mourns him the way Moab mourns, with wailing from the rooftops, beards cut off, heads shaved, and blades drawn across forearms (Isa 15:2–3; Jer 48:37–38). **[P6·11]** Ruth stands beside Orpah with a fresh cut on her own arm.

The Israelite family watches from the edge of the crowd. The boys have never seen anything like it.

"Why are they cutting themselves?" Chilion whispers.

"It's how they grieve here," says Naomi.

"Should we—?" Mahlon starts.

"No. We don't cut ourselves for the dead. We belong to the Lord." That's all the explanation she gives, and it's the same one Deuteronomy gives (Deut 14:1).

On the way back, Ruth passes Mahlon, and he can't stop looking at her arm.

"Doesn't that hurt?"

"It's supposed to."

**Elimelech.** Before the year is out, a fever takes Elimelech. **[P4·12]** Naomi and the boys bury him at the edge of the village, and nobody else comes. That isn't cruelty. In Moab, as in Israel, you mourn your own people, and he wasn't theirs. Naomi tears her dress and sits in the dust. Chilion tries to make a joke and can't finish it, and Mahlon holds on to him.

At dusk, Ruth comes up the path with a loaf wrapped in a cloth: the bread that friends bring to people in mourning (Jer 16:7; Ezek 24:17). **[P6·13]** She doesn't say much. She sets it down and sits with them.

Later that night, she finds Naomi alone in the courtyard, praying, with no image in front of her. **[P6·14]**

"Who are you talking to?"

"God."

"Where is he?"

Naomi takes a while to answer. "Everywhere, they say." A breath. "Tonight I couldn't tell you."

**The bargain.** A straight cut to three years later. It needs no caption, because Ruth is visibly older. Naomi is the head of a household now, with two grown sons, and she's at Ruth's father's door to marry them off. **[P6·15]** It's a negotiation, and she's good at it. Ruth and Orpah listen from the roof.

"Your sons have nothing," he says.

"My sons have worked your fields for three harvests at half what they're worth. You know what they're worth. You've been counting it."

"And your god? Who will my girls pray to?"

"Whoever they like. Same as now."

He names a bride-price, and she laughs at it. They settle somewhere in between, and both of them leave thinking they've won. On the roof, Orpah squeezes Ruth's hand.

The double wedding has drums, a feast and Moabite songs, and Naomi dances. The text never condemns these marriages, and neither do we.

Fade to black. *Ten years later.*

**The fever year.** Night. Mahlon coughs in the dark, as he has every winter for ten years. Ruth kneels before a little clay goddess her mother gave her and prays for a child, quietly, with her whole heart. **[P4·16]** There has been no child in either house. Naomi watches from the doorway and doesn't interrupt; she has her own prayers.

In the morning the two women bake together the way people do after ten years: without talking, each handing the other the next thing before she asks for it. Ruth sings under her breath, a Bethlehem harvest song Naomi taught her long ago. Naomi corrects one word, and then sings the line properly herself. Next door, Chilion and Orpah are arguing loudly, then laughing louder.

That week a fever comes through the village. It takes Chilion first, fast. Mahlon lasts four more days. At the end he asks Ruth for the song, and she sings it to him. **[P6·17]**

**Kin.** Mahlon and Chilion are family now, so Moab mourns them properly. At the graves, Ruth's mother cuts her own arms and then Ruth's, and Ruth lets her. **[P6·18]** Orpah does the same for Chilion. Naomi tears her clothes and sits in the dust between three graves. Afterward she takes Ruth's arm, tears a strip from her own ruined dress, and binds the cuts.

Of everything Naomi brought from Bethlehem, only she is left. At this point the text stops using her name: she's just "the woman" (1:5).

**Bread.** In the market, Naomi hears a Judean accent and follows it to a trader from the hills around Bethlehem. **[C·19]** She asks about home.

"The rain came back. God has visited his people. There's bread in Bethlehem."

She asks after a name or two. He doesn't know them. She goes home and starts to pack (1:6).

**Leaving.** Before dawn, Ruth says goodbye to her mother at the door. **[P6·20]**

"When will you be back?"

Ruth doesn't answer, and her mother understands.

"Their god took your husband. He took everything that woman had. And you'll follow her to him?"

"They're my family."

Her mother doesn't argue. She presses the little clay goddess into Ruth's hands. "Then take her. Somebody should look after you." Ruth's father stays inside the house.

Three women leave the village with everything they own on their backs: Naomi, Ruth and Orpah. Ruth's mother watches from the last house, the way Boaz once watched Elimelech's family go.

**The edge.** The road climbs to the rim of the plateau, where it drops toward the Jordan valley. The whole land lies open across the rift in the morning light: the river, the salt sea, the hills of Judah. Somewhere on those hills is Bethlehem. **[P6·21]**

Naomi stops. She has rehearsed this all night, and it still won't come out (1:8–9). She struggles to get the words out, and her eyes fill. A great deal passes over her face before she manages it: the calculation of a woman who can't feed three, pain, exhaustion, and finally acceptance. She's a determined woman, and this is the hardest thing she's ever had to be determined about.

"Go back. Both of you. Go home to your mothers." Her voice breaks, and she pushes through it. "May God be as good to you as you've been to the dead, and to me. May God give each of you rest... in a husband's home."

She kisses them, and they cry out loud. "No," they say together. "We're coming with you, to your people" (1:10).

Naomi's answer is the sharpest speech in the book, and we play it at full strength: grief so heavy it turns into a bitter joke (1:11–13).

"Go back, my daughters. Why would you come with me? Am I going to have more sons for you to marry? Go home. I'm too old for a husband. And even if I said there was hope, even if I had a husband tonight and had sons, would you wait for them to grow up? Stay alone all those years? No, my daughters. This is far more bitter for me than for you. The Lord's hand has turned against me."

They weep again. Orpah kisses Naomi, a real goodbye. Ruth presses the clay goddess into Orpah's hands: "Give her back to my mother." **[P6·22]** Orpah holds Ruth's face for a moment, then walks back the way they came with the goddess in her hands. She turns once to look. She has done exactly what Naomi asked, and the film never blames her.

Ruth hasn't moved. She has hold of Naomi and won't let go. The Hebrew word for it is the one Genesis uses for a husband clinging to his wife (1:14; Gen 2:24).

"Look," Naomi says. "Your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and her gods. Go after her" (1:15).

Ruth looks back down the road, toward the village and her mother's house. Then the wind comes up off the desert behind them, the same east wind that blew across Bethlehem's empty threshing floor, and it pushes at her back, toward the land across the rift. **[P5·23]** She turns to Naomi (1:16–17).

"Don't ask me to leave you, or to turn back from following you. Where you go, I'll go. Where you stay, I'll stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I'll die, and that's where I'll be buried. May Yahweh do that to me, and worse, if anything but death comes between us."

It's the first time we hear Ruth say the name of Naomi's God, and she says it to swear an oath. Naomi looks at her for a long time. Then she gives one small nod, direct and accepting, and says nothing more (1:18). **[P6·24]**

**The river.** They go down into the rift without a word between them. At the Jordan fords opposite Jericho, where Israel once crossed into the land, the river is waist-deep, brown and fast. **[P4·25]** Ruth goes in first, finds the footing, and comes back for Naomi. Halfway across Naomi slips, and Ruth holds her up. On the far bank stands the ruined mound of Jericho. Neither of them speaks.

**Home.** On the last morning, on the road into Bethlehem, they pass a weathered standing stone: the pillar over Rachel's grave (Gen 35:19–20). **[P4·26]** Ruth doesn't know what it is. Naomi touches it as she passes.

The house of bread has bread again. The barley stands gold on the terraces, the same terraces we saw dead in the prologue. Ruth can't know whose they are, but we do.

The whole town is stirred. At the well, a woman straightens up and stares. It's Naomi's old friend, twelve years older. "Is this Naomi?" (1:19) **[P6·27]** The other women take it up.

Naomi's lament lifts toward a higher register (1:20–21). The names have to land for an audience without Hebrew, so she says what they mean:

"Don't call me Naomi. Don't call me sweet. Call me Mara. Call me bitter. The Almighty has made my life so bitter. I went away full, and the Lord has brought me back empty. Why call me Naomi, when the Lord has testified against me, and the Almighty has brought this on me?"

She says *empty* with Ruth standing right beside her, and the camera finds Ruth's face as she hears it. Nobody introduces the foreigner, with her accent and the half-healed cuts on her arms. The women look at her the way the town will look at her for weeks: as *the Moabite.*

Naomi's old house is a ruin. **[P6·28]** The roof has fallen in, and thistles fill the courtyard. Naomi sits down in the middle of it. Ruth sets down the bundles and starts clearing stones.

Out on the terraces, the evening light falls on ripe barley. It's the beginning of the barley harvest (1:22).

### Act Two — The Field

*Ruth 2 · about 12 minutes*

Next morning (2:2):

"Let me go out to the fields," Ruth says, "and glean behind anyone who'll let me."

"Go, my daughter." It's all Naomi has in her.

The fields at sunrise: young men cutting with sickles, young women binding sheaves behind them, and behind them the gleaners, the widows and orphans and the poorest people in town. Ruth asks the young foreman if she can gather among the sheaves, which is more than gleaners usually get. He tells her to glean behind the reapers like everyone else, because the sheaves are the master's business. **[P4·29]**

The work is brutal. The other gleaners don't make room for her. A reaper flicks a stalk at her. Another knocks her basket over, and the young men laugh while she picks up every grain. **[P6·30]** Two gleaners talk about her loudly enough for her to hear. **[P6·31]**

"A Moabite, gleaning in Bethlehem. Let her glean in Moab."

An older woman answers without looking up. "You're in Boaz's field. He's a man of worth. And his own family comes from Rahab."

"Rahab the prostitute?"

"Rahab of Jericho. And you're eating her family's barley, so mind your mouth."

"And look" (2:4): Boaz comes out from town. It's the man who gave them barley on the road, twelve years older, with grey in his beard and no wife beside him now. His first words to his workers are a blessing.

"The Lord be with you!"

"The Lord bless you!" the reapers call back.

He notices her at once (2:5–7). "Whose girl is that?"

The foreman is careful and a little defensive. "She's the Moabite girl, the one who came back with Naomi from Moab. She asked to gather among the sheaves behind the reapers. She's been on her feet since morning. She's hardly sat down in the shelter."

Boaz goes over to her (2:8–13). Stay in this field, he tells her, close to my young women. I've told the young men not to touch you. When you're thirsty, drink from the jars.

She goes down on her face in the stubble. "Why would you notice me? I'm a foreigner." In Hebrew, *notice* and *foreigner* sound alike, and the script should look for an English equivalent.

"I've heard everything you've done for your mother-in-law since your husband died: how you left your father and your mother and the land you were born in, and came to a people you didn't know. May the Lord repay you for it. May you be paid in full by the Lord, the God of Israel, under whose wings you've come to take shelter."

He has no idea he's describing what he'll do himself.

"You've comforted me," she says. "You've spoken to my heart, and I'm not even one of your servants."

At the midday meal (2:14), in front of everyone, Boaz calls her over to dip her bread in the sour wine, and hands her roasted grain himself. She eats until she's full, which is the first full meal in the story, and folds what's left into her shawl. When she goes back to work, Boaz quietly gives his orders (2:15–16). Let her glean even among the sheaves, and don't shame her. Pull stalks out of the bundles and leave them for her, and don't scold her. The reaper who knocked over her basket gets the job. **[P6·32]**

At evening she beats out what she's gathered: an ephah, about thirty pounds of barley, an absurd amount for one day (2:17). She shoulders it and carries it home through town at dusk, and the town watches her go by.

Naomi sees the sack and gets to her feet (2:18–22). "Where did you glean today? Where did you work? Blessed is the man who noticed you!" Ruth gives her the grain she saved from lunch. Then she says, "The man I worked with today — his name is Boaz."

Something wakes up in Naomi. "Boaz. He gave us his own barley, the day we left." **[P6·33]** Then: "The Lord bless him! He hasn't stopped being kind, to the living or the dead!" She means the barley on the road, given to a man now dead and a woman still living. Whether the kindness is the Lord's or Boaz's, the Hebrew leaves open, and so does her delivery. "That man is close family. He's one of our redeemers."

Ruth doesn't know the word, so Naomi explains it the way you would to a foreigner. When family loses something, whether it's land or freedom, family has the right to buy it back. **[P6·34]**

Ruth warms up. "He even told me, 'Stay close to my young men until they've finished my whole harvest.'"

"His young women, my daughter," Naomi says drily. "Better that you go out with his young women, so nobody bothers you in somebody else's field."

**The last day of the harvest.** A cut to seven weeks later (2:23). **[P6·35]** The wheat is nearly in. Ruth works among Boaz's young women now, and when they start a harvest song, she sings along: it's the song Naomi taught her in Moab, the one she sang to Mahlon. They look at her differently after that. The girl binding sheaves beside her mentions that Boaz has been alone since the famine took his wife. The cuts on Ruth's arms have healed pale. At the meal, Boaz is kind, correct and at a distance, as he has been for seven weeks. He watches her across the field and looks away when she looks up. He never comes closer than that.

At home, the house has a roof now and the jars are full. Naomi is cooking again. She notices what Boaz hasn't done. That night she watches Ruth asleep by the lamp, and makes up her mind.

### Act Three — The Threshing Floor

*Ruth 3 · about 9 minutes*

"My daughter," Naomi says, "isn't it time I found you a place to rest, where things will go well for you?" (3:1) It's the "rest" she wished for her daughters-in-law on the road (1:9).

Tonight, she tells her, Boaz is winnowing barley on the threshing floor (3:2–4). Wash. Put on oil, and your cloak. Go down, and don't let him see you until he's finished eating and drinking. Notice where he lies down. Then go in, uncover his feet, and lie down. "He'll tell you what to do."

"I'll do everything you say," Ruth answers.

Naomi's hands shake as she combs Ruth's hair. **[P4·36]** She knows what she's sending Ruth into: a threshing floor at night, men and wine, and a reputation that can't be won back once it's lost (Hos 9:1). If she's wrong about Boaz, she has ruined her. She does it anyway, out of love and need at once.

The washing and the oil end Ruth's mourning (2 Sam 12:20). Ruth takes off the widow's clothes she has worn since Moab and rubs oil into her arms, over the healed cuts.

At sunset, the threshing floor from the prologue is heaped with grain. Men toss forkfuls into the evening wind, and the chaff streams away gold while the grain falls. Boaz eats and drinks with his workers, laughing, and his heart is content (3:7). When the work is done, the men sleep around the floor, and Boaz lies down at the far end of the grain pile.

Ruth waits in the dark below the floor. A worker gets up and walks a few paces toward where she's hiding, relieves himself, and goes back to sleep. She waits longer. **[P6·37]**

Then she comes quietly, uncovers his feet, and lies down.

At midnight the cold wakes him. He jerks up and twists around, and there's a woman lying at his feet (3:8–9).

"Who are you?"

"I'm Ruth, your servant. Spread your wing over your servant. You're a redeemer."

She was told he would tell her what to do. Instead she tells him. And she adds something nobody asked her to say: *you're a redeemer.* She's tying her own marriage to the rescue of Naomi's family.

This is the most charged scene in the story, and the text means it to be: the dark, the wine, the bare feet, the word *lie down* again and again. We play the closeness at full strength, with breath, the scent of the oil and warmth against the cold, and nothing explicit (principle 8). What resolves it is Boaz (3:10–13):

"The Lord bless you, my daughter. This kindness is even greater than the first: you haven't gone after the young men, poor or rich. Now don't be afraid. I'll do everything you ask. The whole town knows you're a woman of worth."

*Worth* is the word the old gleaner used of him. **[P5·38]** Then comes the catch:

"It's true that I'm a redeemer, but there's one closer than I am. Stay here tonight. In the morning, if he'll redeem you, good, let him. If he won't, then as Yahweh lives, I'll redeem you myself. Lie down until morning."

They lie awake the rest of the night, an arm's length apart, under the stars, in the wind. He doesn't spread his cloak over her. Not yet: until the morning settles who has the right, it isn't his to spread. **[P6·39]**

Before light, before anyone could recognize anyone, he says: "No one can know a woman came to the floor" (3:14–15). He has her hold out her shawl, pours six measures of barley into it, lifts it onto her back, and goes up into town.

Naomi has been awake all night. "How did it go, my daughter?" (3:16) The Hebrew says, literally, *Who are you, my daughter?*, echoing Boaz's question at midnight. Ruth tells her everything. Then: "He gave me these six measures of barley. He said, 'Don't go back to your mother-in-law empty'" (3:17).

*Empty* is Naomi's own word from the day she came home. We never heard Boaz say it on the floor; as in the text, it reaches us first in Ruth's mouth. Naomi has to sit down.

"Sit still, my daughter, until you see how it falls out. The man won't rest until he's settled it — today" (3:18).

### Act Four — The Gate

*Ruth 4 · about 9 minutes*

Boaz goes up to the gate and sits down on the elders' bench, where Elimelech once sold the use of his field. "And look" (4:1): the nearer redeemer comes walking by, right on cue. Boaz calls him by name, and a donkey brays right over it. **[P6·40]** The man who won't keep a dead man's name alive doesn't get his own name kept, either.

Boaz seats ten elders and lays it out (4:3–4). Naomi, back from Moab, is selling the piece of land that belonged to our brother Elimelech. You have the first right. Redeem it if you will, and if not, say so, because I'm next in line.

The man brightens. Land is land. "I'll redeem it."

"On the day you buy the field from Naomi," Boaz says, "you also acquire Ruth the Moabite, the dead man's wife, to keep the dead man's name alive on his land" (4:5).

*Moabite* lands on the crowd like a stone. The man's face does the arithmetic: the price, a field that will pass to a son who counts as Mahlon's, and two more mouths to feed. "Then I can't redeem it. I'd be ruining my own inheritance. You take my right. I can't do it" (4:6).

He pulls off his sandal and hands it to Boaz (4:7–8). A boy in the crowd asks his father why. The land is yours where your foot walks, the father tells him, so he's handing over his walking. **[P6·41]** The text stops to explain the custom too.

Boaz stands and speaks to everyone (4:9–10). He has bought from Naomi everything that belonged to Elimelech, Chilion and Mahlon. And he has acquired Ruth the Moabite, Mahlon's widow, as his wife, to keep the dead man's name alive, so that it isn't cut off from his family or from the gate of his town. "You are witnesses today."

He has no son of his own, and he has just agreed that his first son will carry another man's name. That's the price the nearer redeemer wouldn't pay. **[P4·42]**

"We are witnesses," the gate answers, and blesses him (4:11–12). May the Lord make the woman coming into his house like Rachel and Leah, who built the house of Israel: Rachel, whose pillar Ruth passed on the road. May he do worthily in Ephrathah and make a name in Bethlehem. And may his house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah. Tamar was another woman who took a risk to keep a dead man's name alive (Gen 38).

At the house, Ruth can't sit still. Naomi grinds grain without looking up, too hard. Then a shadow fills the doorway: Boaz, with a sandal in his hand. He doesn't need to say anything. He looks at Naomi, then at Ruth. **[P6·43]**

**The wedding** (4:13). There's music in Bethlehem and a feast, and at the center of it Boaz spreads the corner of his cloak over Ruth. It's the wing she asked for on the threshing floor, and the shelter he once asked the Lord to give her (2:12; 3:9; Ezek 16:8). **[P6·44]** Later, behind a closed door, a lamp goes out. From here on, nobody calls her "the Moabite." In the text, too, she's simply Ruth from now on (4:13).

**The Lord gives her a child** (4:13). After ten childless years, Ruth is pregnant within months. We see Naomi's hand on Ruth's belly, and Naomi's face.

**The birth** comes a year after Ruth arrived, at the start of the next barley harvest. **[P6·45]** Ruth is on the birthing stones (Exod 1:16), with the midwives and the women of Bethlehem around her and Naomi gripping her hands. It's long and it's dangerous: Rachel died giving birth on the road outside this town. We don't look away. Boaz waits outside the door. Then there's a cry.

The women turn to Naomi, the same women who asked "Is this Naomi?" (4:14–15):

"Blessed be the Lord, who hasn't left you without a redeemer today! May his name be famous in Israel. He'll bring your life back and take care of you when you're old, because your daughter-in-law who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has given birth to him."

*Better than seven sons.* Ruth hears it the way she heard *empty.* Naomi looks at her for a long moment, and this time she sees her. **[P6·46]**

Naomi takes the boy, lays him in her lap, and becomes his nurse (4:16). The neighbor women name him: "A son has been born to Naomi!" They call him Obed (4:17).

**The last image.** **[P6·47]** Dusk on the roof. Naomi holds Obed and looks east across the rift, to where the mountains of Moab are turning blue and her husband and sons are buried. Below her, the barley is ripening on Boaz's terraces. The wind comes up off the desert.

Fade to black.

## 4. Threads

- **Empty and full.** The bare floor and the dead field; "I went away full" (1:21); the ephah of barley (2:17); "don't go back empty" (3:17); "better than seven sons" (4:15); the house rebuilt.
- **Bread.** The empty bread bin; Boaz's barley on the road; Ruth's loaf for the mourners; "there's bread in Bethlehem"; the meal in the field; the six measures.
- **The song.** Naomi teaches it, Ruth sings it to Mahlon as he dies, and it wins over Bethlehem's young women.
- **Three views across the rift.** Bethlehem toward Moab (the prologue), Moab toward Judah (the parting), Bethlehem toward Moab again (the last image).
- **Boaz's places.** The field, the floor and the gate are each seen empty or ordinary first, then full or decisive.
- **The mirrors (FR-08, never spoken).** The one who stayed and the one who came. Opposite in age, wealth, people and standing, and alike in kindness. Both widowed. "Worth" (2:1, through the gleaner; 3:11). The wing (2:12; 3:9; the wedding).
- **Names.** Bethlehem, Naomi and Mara, Mr. So-and-so, "the dead man's name" (4:5, 10), Obed. And "the Moabite," which drops away after the gate, as it does in the text.
- **The chorus.** Naomi's friend says goodbye in the prologue, asks "Is this Naomi?" at the return, and is among the women at the birth.

## 5. How God appears

- **He never speaks, and he works no visible miracle.** There's no Tier A in this episode.
- **The narrator's two statements that God acted** (1:6; 4:13) are carried by people: the trader's "God has visited his people," and the women's "Blessed be the Lord" (4:14).
- **"Chance" (2:3) and "And look" (2:4; 4:1)** are shot the same way: ordinary moments that the camera holds a beat longer than it needs to.
- **Blessings come true through the people who speak them.** Naomi wishes her daughters-in-law "rest" (1:9) and then finds it for Ruth (3:1). Boaz prays for the Lord's "wings" over Ruth (2:12) and becomes the answer (3:9; the wedding).
- **Naomi's lament stands at full strength.** The answer comes through events, not argument.
- **What tells Ruth to go.** The text never says, and her oath is the only explanation she gives. So the act builds her reasons in the way the book works, through people and the world rather than a voice or a dream: the bread she brought, ten years beside Naomi, Naomi's faith in a God with no face, a husband who wanted Bethlehem's song at the end, and her own prayers that went unanswered. Then, at the moment she has to choose, the east wind comes up behind her and pushes toward the land. It's the same wind that blew over Bethlehem's empty floor, and it will winnow Boaz's grain in Act Three. In Hebrew the word for wind, *ruach*, is also the word for spirit. Nobody remarks on it. It fits your view that God speaks through the world (Discussion 001), and it's deniable, the way the book's "chance" is.
