## 1. The story in plain words

This happens in the time of the judges, before Israel had kings.

**Leaving.** A famine hits Bethlehem, a town whose name means "house of bread." A man named Elimelech takes his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, to Moab. Moab is a neighboring people east of the Dead Sea whom Israel has long distrusted.

**Loss.** In Moab, Elimelech dies. His sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. About ten years later, both sons die too, leaving no children. Naomi now has nothing but two foreign daughters-in-law.

**The road.** Naomi hears that the famine back home is over, and she sets out for Bethlehem. On the road she tells the two young widows to go back to their mothers' homes and find new husbands, since she has nothing left to give them. Orpah, in tears, kisses her and goes back. Ruth refuses to go. She binds herself to Naomi with an oath: *your people will be my people, your God my God; where you die, I will die.* Naomi stops arguing.

**Coming home.** Their arrival stirs up the whole town, and the women ask, "Is this Naomi?" Naomi tells them to call her Mara, "bitter." She left full and came back empty, and she says plainly that God did this to her.

**The field.** It's the start of the barley harvest. Ruth goes out to glean, picking up the grain the harvesters leave behind, which the law allowed the poor and foreigners to do. As it "happens," she ends up in the field of Boaz, a wealthy, older relative of Elimelech's. Boaz notices her, asks who she is, and is moved by what she has done for Naomi. He tells his workers to leave her alone, feeds her at the midday meal, and quietly has them drop extra grain for her to pick up. He blesses her in the name of Yahweh, "under whose wings you have come to take refuge." She comes home that evening with an astonishing amount of barley.

**A redeemer.** When Naomi hears the name Boaz, something wakes up in her. He is one of their "redeemers": a relative with the right and the duty to rescue family land and family members.

**The wait.** Ruth gleans in Boaz's fields through both the barley and wheat harvests, about seven weeks. Boaz does nothing more.

**The threshing floor.** So Naomi makes a plan. One night Boaz is winnowing grain at the threshing floor. Ruth washes, puts on scented oil and her cloak, and waits until he has eaten, drunk and lain down beside the grain heap. Then she uncovers his feet and lies down there. At midnight he wakes, startled, to find a woman at his feet. She tells him who she is and asks him to spread his "wing," meaning the corner of his cloak, over her. In effect, she is asking him to marry her.

**His answer.** Boaz is overwhelmed by her loyalty. But there is a relative closer than he is, who has the first right. Boaz swears he'll settle it. He keeps her safe until just before dawn, sends her home with a load of grain, and goes to the town gate.

**The gate.** At the gate, in front of ten elders, Boaz confronts the nearer relative. That man is happy to buy Naomi's family land, until he learns it comes with Ruth and the duty to give the dead Mahlon an heir. He backs out and takes off his sandal to seal the handover. Boaz buys the land and marries Ruth, and the townspeople bless them.

**A son.** Ruth gives birth to a son. The women of Bethlehem praise God and tell Naomi that this child will restore her life, because he was born to a daughter-in-law who loves her and is "better to you than seven sons." Naomi holds him in her lap. The women name him Obed. He becomes the father of Jesse, who becomes the father of David, Israel's greatest king.

## 2. Working translation

This translation is my own, made from the Hebrew (the Masoretic Text) and checked against the BSB, WEB, KJV, NRSV, ESV, NIV and JPS translations. It stays close to the Hebrew, sometimes at the cost of smoothness, because it's the base for every recorded line of dialogue (Tier B). The script will modernize the dialogue to setting 2.

**Conventions:**
- God's personal name, which most English Bibles print as "the LORD," is written **Yahweh**, following your decision.
- "Shaddai" is left untranslated. It's usually rendered "the Almighty."
- Square brackets mark words added for sense.

### Chapter 1

**1** In the days when the judges ruled, there was a famine in the land. And a man from Bethlehem in Judah went to live as a foreigner in the country of Moab — he, his wife and his two sons. **2** The man's name was Elimelech, his wife's name was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion. They were Ephrathites from Bethlehem in Judah. They came to the country of Moab and stayed there.

**3** Then Elimelech, Naomi's husband, died, and she was left with her two sons. **4** They married Moabite women. The name of one was Orpah, and the name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years. **5** Then both of them, Mahlon and Chilion, also died, and the woman was left without her two boys and without her husband.

**6** So she got up with her daughters-in-law to return from the country of Moab, because she had heard in the country of Moab that Yahweh had visited his people and given them bread. **7** She left the place where she had been living, with her two daughters-in-law, and they set out on the road to return to the land of Judah.

**8** Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, "Go. Return, each of you, to your mother's house. May Yahweh deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me. **9** May Yahweh grant that you find rest, each of you in the house of a husband." And she kissed them, and they lifted up their voices and wept. **10** They said to her, "No. We will return with you, to your people."

**11** But Naomi said, "Return, my daughters. Why would you go with me? Do I still have sons in my womb who could become your husbands? **12** Return, my daughters. Go. I'm too old to have a husband. Even if I said, 'I have hope' — even if I had a husband tonight and bore sons — **13** would you wait for them until they grew up? Would you shut yourselves away and not marry for their sake? No, my daughters. It is far more bitter for me than for you, because the hand of Yahweh has gone out against me."

**14** Again they lifted up their voices and wept. Then Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her. **15** Naomi said, "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods. Go back after your sister-in-law."

**16** But Ruth said, "Don't press me to leave you, to turn back from following you. For where you go, I will go, and where you lodge, I will lodge. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. **17** Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May Yahweh do so to me, and more, if anything but death separates me from you." **18** When Naomi saw that she was determined to go with her, she said no more to her.

**19** So the two of them walked until they came to Bethlehem. When they came to Bethlehem, the whole town was stirred because of them, and the women said, "Is this Naomi?" **20** She said to them, "Don't call me Naomi. Call me Mara, for Shaddai has made my life very bitter. **21** I went away full, and Yahweh has brought me back empty. Why call me Naomi, when Yahweh has testified against me and Shaddai has brought disaster on me?"

**22** So Naomi returned, and with her Ruth the Moabite, her daughter-in-law, who returned from the country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest.

**Notes on chapter 1:**
- **1:1** "Bethlehem" means "house of bread," so the story opens with a famine in the house of bread. "Live as a foreigner" translates *gur*, to live as a resident alien. That's the same status Ruth will have in Israel, so the story reverses itself.
- **1:2** Ephrathah is the name of Bethlehem's clan and district (4:11; Mic 5:2).
- **1:4** It's unclear whether the "about ten years" counts from their arrival or from the marriages. The text never says which son married which woman until 4:10.
- **1:5** "Her boys" is *yeladim*, normally "children." The same word, in the singular (*yeled*), is used for Obed in 4:16. Naomi loses her boys and receives a boy.
- **1:6** "Visited" (*paqad*) means attended to, took notice of. This is one of only two places where the narrator says directly that God acted; the other is 4:13. "Bread" means the house of bread has bread again.
- **1:6–22** The word "return" (*shuv*) runs through the chapter about a dozen times. In 1:22, Ruth the Moabite "returns" to a land she has never seen.
- **1:8** "Mother's house" is unusual, since the normal phrase is "father's house." It appears in contexts of love and marriage (Gen 24:28; Song 3:4; 8:2). "Deal kindly" is the book's key word, *hesed*: loyal, generous kindness that goes beyond duty.
- **1:9** "Rest" (*menuchah*) means security in a husband's household. Naomi takes it up again in 3:1.
- **1:13** "Far more bitter for me than for you" can also be read "very bitter for me because of you." The Hebrew allows both.
- **1:14** "Clung" (*davaq*) is the same verb used in Genesis 2:24, where a man "clings" to his wife.
- **1:15** "Her gods" (*eloheha*) can be plural or a singular "her god." Moab's god was Chemosh (Num 21:29).
- **1:16–17** Ruth swears an oath in Yahweh's name, using the self-curse formula ("may Yahweh do so to me, and more," as in 1 Sam 3:17). In the act of swearing, she has already taken Israel's God as her own.
- **1:19** "Stirred" can also mean thrown into an uproar (1 Sam 4:5). "The women said" uses a feminine plural verb: the town's women serve as a chorus, and they return at the end (4:14–17).
- **1:20–21** Naomi means "pleasant," and Mara means "bitter." *Shaddai* is the old name from the patriarchs' time (Gen 17:1), and its meaning is debated. "Testified against me" can also be read "afflicted me," which is how the Greek Septuagint takes it. Note the full/empty contrast.
- **1:22** The barley harvest begins in spring, around April.

### Chapter 2

**1** Now Naomi had a relative on her husband's side, a man of great worth, from Elimelech's clan. His name was Boaz.

**2** Ruth the Moabite said to Naomi, "Let me go to the fields and glean among the ears of grain behind someone who will look on me with favor." Naomi said to her, "Go, my daughter." **3** So she went. She came and gleaned in a field behind the reapers, and her chance happened upon the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was from Elimelech's clan.

**4** And look: Boaz came from Bethlehem. He said to the reapers, "Yahweh be with you!" And they said to him, "Yahweh bless you!" **5** Boaz said to his young man who was in charge of the reapers, "Whose young woman is this?" **6** The young man in charge of the reapers answered, "She's the Moabite girl who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab. **7** She said, 'Please let me glean and gather among the sheaves behind the reapers.' So she came, and she has kept at it from morning until now. She has hardly sat down in the shelter."

**8** Then Boaz said to Ruth, "Listen, my daughter. Don't go to glean in another field, and don't leave this one. Stay close to my young women here. **9** Keep your eyes on the field they're reaping, and follow behind them. I've ordered the young men not to touch you. When you're thirsty, go to the jars and drink from what the young men have drawn."

**10** She fell on her face, bowing to the ground, and said to him, "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, when I am a foreigner?" **11** Boaz answered her, "I've been told everything you have done for your mother-in-law since your husband died — how you left your father and mother and the land of your birth and came to a people you didn't know before. **12** May Yahweh repay what you have done, and may your reward be full from Yahweh, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge." **13** She said, "May I find favor in your eyes, my lord, for you have comforted me and spoken to your servant's heart, though I'm not even one of your servants."

**14** At mealtime Boaz said to her, "Come over here. Eat some of the bread and dip your piece in the vinegar." So she sat beside the reapers, and he handed her roasted grain. She ate until she was full, and had some left over. **15** When she got up to glean, Boaz gave orders to his young men: "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and don't humiliate her. **16** Pull some stalks out of the bundles for her and leave them for her to glean. Don't scold her."

**17** So she gleaned in the field until evening. Then she beat out what she had gleaned, and it came to about an ephah of barley. **18** She picked it up and went into the town, and her mother-in-law saw what she had gleaned. Ruth also took out and gave her what she had left over after eating her fill.

**19** Her mother-in-law said to her, "Where did you glean today? Where did you work? Blessed be the man who took notice of you!" So she told her mother-in-law whose field she had worked in. She said, "The name of the man I worked with today is Boaz." **20** Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, "Blessed be he by Yahweh, whose kindness has not abandoned the living or the dead!" Naomi said to her, "The man is a close relative of ours. He's one of our redeemers."

**21** Ruth the Moabite said, "He even told me, 'Stay close to my young men until they have finished my whole harvest.'" **22** Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law, "It's good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women, so that no one bothers you in some other field." **23** So she stayed close to Boaz's young women, gleaning until the barley and wheat harvests were finished. And she lived with her mother-in-law.

**Notes on chapter 2:**
- **2:1** "Relative" is spelled one way and read another (§3). "A man of great worth" is *ish gibbor hayil*. *Hayil* covers wealth, strength and character. Boaz will use the same word for Ruth in 3:11.
- **2:2** Ruth takes the initiative. "Favor" (*chen*) is the key word of this chapter (2:2, 10, 13).
- **2:3** "Her chance happened upon" is the narrator's wink. It's the language of luck, about something that clearly isn't luck.
- **2:4** "And look" (*wehinneh*) is the narrator's cut to a new shot, here with Boaz arriving just then. His first words are a blessing in Yahweh's name.
- **2:5** "Whose young woman is this?" asks who she belongs to: her family, her household, her husband. It's a social question.
- **2:7** The last sentence is one of the most obscure in the book. Literally it reads "this her sitting in the house a little." The Septuagint reads "she has not rested in the field even a little." I've taken "the house" to mean a field shelter.
- **2:8** "Listen" is literally "Haven't you heard?", a polite way of giving an instruction. "Stay close" is the same verb as "clung" in 1:14.
- **2:9** "Touch" here means harass or molest. The danger to a lone foreign woman in the fields is real (see also 2:15, 22).
- **2:10** There's a pun here: "take notice of me" (*hakkir*) and "foreigner" (*nokhriyah*) share the same root. Roughly: "why recognize me, when I'm a stranger?"
- **2:11** "Left your father and mother and the land of your birth" echoes God's call to Abraham (Gen 12:1). Ruth is an Abraham figure.
- **2:12** "Wings" (*kenafayim*) is answered in 3:9, when Ruth asks Boaz to spread his own "wing" over her.
- **2:13** "Spoken to the heart" means spoken tenderly, the way you'd win someone over (Gen 50:21; Isa 40:2; Hos 2:14). "Servant" here is *shifchah*, the lowest rank of female servant.
- **2:14** "Vinegar" is sour wine, used as a dip. Roasted grain is fresh kernels parched over a fire. "Ate until she was full, and had some left over" is the first fullness in the story after Naomi's "empty" (1:21).
- **2:15–16** Boaz's generosity is secret. He arranges it so she won't know it's charity.
- **2:17** An ephah was probably around 20 litres, though estimates vary. That's roughly 13–14 kg, or about 30 lb, of barley: a startling amount for one day's gleaning, enough to feed two women for weeks.
- **2:19** "Took notice of you" uses the same word as 2:10.
- **2:20** "Whose kindness": is it Yahweh's or Boaz's? The Hebrew allows both, perhaps on purpose. "Redeemer" (*go'el*) is the book's first use of the word. In chapter 4 the root appears over and over.
- **2:21–22** Ruth says Boaz told her to stay close to his "young men," and Naomi gently answers with "his young women." It's a small correction, with a smile or a worry in it.
- **2:22** "Bothers you" is literally "meets you," in the sense of an encounter with hostile intent.
- **2:23** The barley and wheat harvests together take about seven weeks, roughly from Passover to Pentecost (Shavuot). Synagogues still read Ruth at Shavuot.

### Chapter 3

**1** Naomi her mother-in-law said to her, "My daughter, shouldn't I look for rest for you, so that things will go well for you? **2** Now, isn't Boaz our relative, the one whose young women you've been with? Look, tonight he's winnowing barley at the threshing floor. **3** So wash, put on scented oil, put on your cloak, and go down to the threshing floor. But don't let the man know you're there until he has finished eating and drinking. **4** When he lies down, note the place where he lies. Then go in, uncover his feet, and lie down. He will tell you what to do." **5** She said to her, "Everything you say [to me], I will do."

**6** So she went down to the threshing floor and did everything her mother-in-law had told her. **7** Boaz ate and drank, and his heart was content, and he went to lie down at the end of the grain pile. Then she came quietly, uncovered his feet and lay down.

**8** In the middle of the night the man was startled. He twisted around, and look, a woman was lying at his feet! **9** He said, "Who are you?" She said, "I am Ruth, your servant. Spread your wing over your servant, for you are a redeemer."

**10** He said, "May you be blessed by Yahweh, my daughter. This last kindness of yours is greater than the first, because you haven't gone after the young men, poor or rich. **11** And now, my daughter, don't be afraid. Everything you say, I will do for you. The whole gate of my people knows that you are a woman of worth. **12** Now, it is true that I am a redeemer, but there is a redeemer closer than I am. **13** Stay the night. In the morning, if he will redeem you, good — let him redeem you. But if he doesn't want to redeem you, then as Yahweh lives, I will redeem you myself. Lie down until morning."

**14** So she lay at his feet until morning, and got up before anyone could recognize anyone else. He said, "No one must know that the woman came to the threshing floor." **15** Then he said, "Bring the shawl you're wearing and hold it out." She held it, and he measured out six [measures] of barley and put it on her. Then he went into the town.

**16** She came to her mother-in-law, who said, "How did it go, my daughter?" She told her everything the man had done for her. **17** She said, "He gave me these six [measures] of barley, because he said [to me], 'Don't go back to your mother-in-law empty-handed.'" **18** Naomi said, "Sit still, my daughter, until you find out how the matter turns out. The man won't rest until he has settled it today."

**Notes on chapter 3:**
- **3:1** "Rest" (*manoach*) takes up Naomi's wish from 1:9.
- **3:2** Grain was winnowed in the evening breeze, so the work, and the meal afterward, ran into the night.
- **3:3** Washing, anointing with oil and putting on clothes marked the end of a period of mourning (2 Sam 12:20) and were also part of a bride's preparation (Ezek 16:9–10). "Go down" is written in the Hebrew as "I will go down," but traditionally read as "you will go down" (§3).
- **3:4** "His feet" is *margelot*, literally "the place of his feet." "Feet" can be a euphemism for genitals (e.g., Isa 7:20). The narrator stacks up words with double meanings: "lie down" (*shakhav*), "know" (*yada'*, 3:3, 14), "go in" (*bo'*, 3:4, 7). Then Boaz behaves with complete honor. The charge is deliberate (§10).
- **3:5** "To me" is read aloud in the traditional reading but not written in the text, and the same happens in 3:17.
- **3:7** "His heart was content" means relaxed, satisfied, perhaps a little drunk (compare 1 Sam 25:36; Esth 1:10).
- **3:8** "Startled" is the word for a sudden trembling. "Twisted around" might also mean "bent forward" to look.
- **3:9** Ruth upgrades her own title. In 2:13 she called herself a *shifchah*, the lowest servant. Now she's an *'amah*, a maidservant of a rank who could become a wife. "Spread your wing" uses *kanaph*, meaning both "wing" and "corner of a garment," and spreading it over a woman was a marriage idiom (Ezek 16:8). By adding "for you are a redeemer," Ruth ties marriage to redemption, which the law itself didn't do. That's her own act of *hesed*.
- **3:10** "The first" kindness was her loyalty to Naomi. "The last" is choosing Boaz, and through him Naomi's family line, over younger men.
- **3:11** "The whole gate of my people" means the whole town. "A woman of worth" is *eshet hayil*, the female form of what the narrator called Boaz in 2:1.
- **3:13** "As Yahweh lives" is an oath.
- **3:14** "Recognize" (*nakar*) is the word from 2:10 and 2:19 again. A woman at a threshing floor at night could easily be taken for a prostitute (Hos 9:1), so this is about protecting her reputation.
- **3:15** The shawl is a large wrap. The unit of "six" isn't given. The standard Hebrew text says "he went into the town," while many manuscripts and ancient translations say "she."
- **3:16** "How did it go?" is literally "Who are you, my daughter?", an idiom. It echoes Boaz's "Who are you?" (3:9).
- **3:17** "Empty-handed" (*reqam*) is the same word Naomi used in 1:21: "Yahweh has brought me back empty." These words of Boaz's weren't reported in the threshing-floor scene. They appear for the first time here, in Ruth's mouth.

### Chapter 4

**1** Meanwhile Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there. And look, the redeemer Boaz had spoken of was passing by. Boaz said, "Come over here and sit down, Mr. So-and-so." He came over and sat down. **2** Then Boaz took ten men from the town's elders and said, "Sit here." And they sat down.

**3** He said to the redeemer, "Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab, is selling the piece of land that belonged to our brother Elimelech. **4** I thought I should let you know and say: Buy it in the presence of those sitting here and in the presence of the elders of my people. If you will redeem it, redeem it. If not, tell me, so I'll know. There's no one but you to redeem it, and I come after you." He said, "I will redeem it."

**5** Then Boaz said, "On the day you buy the field from Naomi, you also acquire Ruth the Moabite, the dead man's wife, to raise up the dead man's name on his inheritance." **6** The redeemer said, "Then I can't redeem it for myself, or I'll ruin my own inheritance. You take my right of redemption yourself, because I can't redeem it."

**7** Now this was the custom in former times in Israel, concerning redemption and exchange: to confirm any transaction, a man would pull off his sandal and give it to the other party. This was how agreements were witnessed in Israel. **8** So the redeemer said to Boaz, "Buy it yourself," and pulled off his sandal.

**9** Then Boaz said to the elders and all the people, "You are witnesses today that I have bought from Naomi everything that belonged to Elimelech and everything that belonged to Chilion and Mahlon. **10** And I have also acquired Ruth the Moabite, Mahlon's wife, to be my wife, to raise up the dead man's name on his inheritance, so that the dead man's name will not be cut off from among his kin or from the gate of his town. You are witnesses today."

**11** All the people at the gate, and the elders, said, "We are witnesses. May Yahweh make the woman who is coming into your house like Rachel and like Leah, the two of whom built the house of Israel. May you do worthily in Ephrathah and make a name in Bethlehem. **12** And through the offspring Yahweh gives you by this young woman, may your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah."

**13** So Boaz took Ruth, and she became his wife. He went in to her, and Yahweh gave her conception, and she bore a son. **14** Then the women said to Naomi, "Blessed be Yahweh, who has not left you without a redeemer today! May his name be famous in Israel. **15** He will restore your life and sustain you in your old age, for your daughter-in-law who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has given birth to him."

**16** Naomi took the child, laid him in her lap, and became his nurse. **17** The neighbor women gave him a name, saying, "A son has been born to Naomi!" They named him Obed. He was the father of Jesse, the father of David.

**18** These are the generations of Perez: Perez fathered Hezron, **19** Hezron fathered Ram, Ram fathered Amminadab, **20** Amminadab fathered Nahshon, Nahshon fathered Salmon, **21** Salmon fathered Boaz, Boaz fathered Obed, **22** Obed fathered Jesse, and Jesse fathered David.

**Notes on chapter 4:**
- **4:1** "Went up" because the town gate is higher than the threshing floor. "And look": the redeemer appears right on cue. "Mr. So-and-so" is *peloni almoni*, the Hebrew equivalent of "what's-his-name." The narrator withholds the man's name, so the man who won't preserve a dead man's name doesn't get his own preserved either.
- **4:2** Ten elders is a quorum of witnesses.
- **4:3** "Is selling" could also be read "has sold." How a destitute widow still held a claim to land is never explained (§10).
- **4:4** "Let you know" is literally "uncover your ear." It's the same verb, "uncover," as in 3:4. The standard Hebrew text reads "if he will not redeem," and many manuscripts read "if you will not." I've followed the sense.
- **4:5** This is the book's biggest textual question. The written Hebrew reads "**I** acquire Ruth," and the traditional reading says "**you** acquire Ruth." Each produces a different scene (§3, §10). I've followed the traditional reading here.
- **4:7** The narrator stops to explain a custom that had already died out by the time of writing. It differs from the ceremony in Deuteronomy 25:9, where a widow pulls off the sandal of a brother-in-law who refuses to marry her, and spits.
- **4:10** This is the first time we learn that Ruth's husband was Mahlon.
- **4:11** "Do worthily" uses *hayil* again: a third "worth," after Boaz (2:1) and Ruth (3:11).
- **4:12** The blessing invokes Perez, born after Tamar's bold, risky initiative with Judah (Gen 38). Tamar was another outsider woman whose boldness preserved Judah's line.
- **4:13** "Yahweh gave her conception" is the second and last time the narrator says God acted (with 1:6). It comes after about ten childless years with Mahlon.
- **4:14** "A redeemer" here means the baby, or Boaz. The Hebrew could point to either.
- **4:15** "Restore your life" is literally "bring back your soul," and the verb is *shuv*, "return," one last time. "Better to you than seven sons": seven stands for perfection, so Ruth outweighs the ideal family. "Who loves you" is the only place the book says anyone loves Naomi.
- **4:16** "Nurse" is *omenet*, caregiver or guardian. The masculine form is used for guardians of children elsewhere (Num 11:12; Isa 49:23). Some read the gesture as an adoption.
- **4:17** This is the only place in the Bible where neighbors name a child. "A son has been born to Naomi" means empty has become full. Obed means "servant" or "worker."
- **4:18–22** The genealogy has ten generations from Perez to David. Boaz is seventh and David is tenth, positions often given weight in ancient genealogies, as with Enoch and Noah in Genesis 5. Salmon is spelled two ways in the Hebrew (Salmah in 4:20, Salmon in 4:21).

## 3. The text itself

- **Manuscripts.** The standard Hebrew text is the Masoretic Text. Four fragmentary copies of Ruth were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls (labeled 2QRuth a–b and 4QRuth a–b), and they agree closely with it. The Greek Septuagint is also close. Ruth is a textually stable book.
- **Written versus read.** In a handful of places, Jewish scribes preserved one spelling in the written text (the *ketiv*) and a different reading for reading aloud (the *qere*). The ones that matter for us:

| Verse | Written | Read aloud | Matters? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2:1 | "acquaintance" (*meyudda'*) | "relative" (*moda'*) | Slightly. How close a kinsman is Boaz? |
| 3:3, 3:4 | "I will go down," "I will lie down" | "you will go down," "you will lie down" | Probably not. The written forms are best explained as an old-fashioned spelling of "you." But see §8: Naomi's speech has archaic features. |
| 3:5, 3:17 | — | "to me" added | No |
| 4:5 | "**I** acquire Ruth" | "**you** acquire Ruth" | **Yes.** See below. |

- **Other variants.** In 3:15, manuscripts differ on whether "he" or "she" went into town. In 4:4, they differ between "if he" and "if you."

**The 4:5 choice:**
- **Read aloud ("you acquire Ruth"),** the version most translations follow. Boaz tells the nearer relative that buying the field obliges him to marry Ruth and produce an heir for Mahlon. The relative refuses, because the land would eventually pass to that heir and he'd have spent his money on someone else's inheritance (4:6).
- **Written ("I acquire Ruth").** Boaz announces that *he* is marrying Ruth to raise up Mahlon's name. The relative suddenly realizes that any son of Boaz and Ruth would claim the land he's about to pay for, so he withdraws. This version makes Boaz a strategist who wins in public with a single line.

Both readings are "the text." **Decided (2026-10-01): the read-aloud version, "you acquire Ruth,"** which is your reading and most translations'. The difference turns out to be smaller than it first looks. In both versions, the relative withdraws because an heir would claim the land, and Boaz marries Ruth. What changes is Boaz's tactic in one line: in the read-aloud version he reveals the condition, while in the written version he announces his own intention. Verse 4:10 doesn't decide between them (Boaz says "I have acquired" *after* the refusal in either case), but the read-aloud version is well supported and is how the verse has been read aloud for centuries.

## 4. How the book is built

- **Four chapters, four settings:**
  1. The road: loss and return.
  2. The field: two kinds of kindness meet.
  3. The threshing floor: night, and a proposal.
  4. The gate: law and resolution, then the birth and the genealogy.

  Each chapter ends on a pause that hands off to the next (1:22; 2:23; 3:18).
- **Mirror structure.**
  - Chapters 1 and 4 mirror each other: empty becomes full, and the women of Bethlehem frame the story ("Is this Naomi?" in 1:19 becomes "A son has been born to Naomi" in 4:17).
  - Chapters 2 and 3 share a pattern: Naomi and Ruth talk, then Ruth meets Boaz, then Ruth comes home and reports to Naomi.
- **Key words that carry the story:**
  - "return" (*shuv*), from 1:6 to 4:15;
  - "kindness" (*hesed*: 1:8; 2:20; 3:10);
  - "redeem" (*ga'al*), all through chapter 4;
  - "wing/corner" (*kanaph*: 2:12; 3:9);
  - "worth" (*hayil*: 2:1; 3:11; 4:11);
  - "cling/stay close" (*davaq*: 1:14; 2:8, 21, 23);
  - "notice/foreigner/recognize" (root *nkr*: 2:10, 19; 3:14);
  - "empty/full" (1:21; 3:17);
  - "rest" (1:9; 3:1);
  - "name" (4:5, 10, 11, 14, 17).
- **Mostly dialogue.** Well over half the verses are speech. Characters reveal themselves through their first words (§8).
- **How God appears.** The narrator credits God directly only twice, with bread (1:6) and conception (4:13). Everywhere else, God is in people's mouths:
  - Nearly every character blesses someone else in Yahweh's name (1:8–9; 2:4, 12, 19–20; 3:10; 4:11–12, 14).
  - The blessings come true through the people who speak them. Boaz prays for God's "wings" over Ruth (2:12) and becomes the answer himself (3:9). Naomi's wish for "rest" (1:9) is fulfilled through her own plan (3:1).

  This is the Bible's clearest picture of God working through people, and that's your belief (Discussion 001).
- **Humor and irony:**
  - "Chance" (2:3).
  - "Young men" against "young women" (2:21–22).
  - Mr. So-and-so (4:1).
  - The nearer relative's eager "I will redeem it" (4:4), followed by his quick retreat (4:6).

## 5. When: chronology, and Rahab

**Decided (2026-10-01):** the series uses the text-first chronology in [`world/TIMELINE.md`](../../../world/TIMELINE.md), with the Exodus in 1446 BC and the fall of Jericho around 1406. Ruth's story runs from about 1152 to 1139 BC, "in the days of Eli," as Josephus puts it. **Rahab is Boaz's ancestor several generations back, not his mother.**

The book itself gives only "in the days when the judges ruled" (1:1).

### The story's timeline

All dates are approximate. The ages are inferences (P4) built from the text's hints: Naomi is "too old to have a husband" (1:12), the marriages last "about ten years" (1:4), and Boaz is evidently older than Ruth (3:10). He calls her "my daughter" (2:8; 3:10–11).

| When (BC) | What | Ages |
|---|---|---|
| 1406 | Jericho falls. Rahab is spared and lives on in Israel (Josh 6:25), as the ancestress of Salmon's line (Matt 1:5). | — |
| c. 1152 | Famine. Elimelech's family leaves for Moab. | Elimelech about 40, Naomi about 36, Mahlon about 17, Chilion about 15. Ruth, in Moab, about 14. Boaz about 33. |
| c. 1151 | Elimelech dies (1:3). | |
| c. 1149 | The sons marry Moabite women: Mahlon marries Ruth, and Chilion marries Orpah (1:4; 4:10). | Ruth about 17 |
| c. 1144 | Eli becomes high priest at Shiloh (1 Sam 4:18). | |
| c. 1140, winter | Mahlon and Chilion die (1:5). | |
| c. 1140, spring | Naomi and Ruth arrive in Bethlehem at the start of the barley harvest (1:22). | Naomi about 48, Ruth about 26, Boaz about 45 |
| c. 1140, early summer | The harvest ends. The threshing floor, the gate, the wedding (3–4). | |
| c. 1139 | Obed is born (4:13–17). | |
| c. 1100 | Jesse is born. | Obed about 40 |
| 1040 | David is born, Jesse's youngest son (1 Sam 16:10–11). | Jesse about 60, "old in the days of Saul" (1 Sam 17:12) |

**The wider world.** Under this arrangement, Ruth falls during Gideon's forty years of "rest" (Judg 8:28). That's rest from enemies, not from weather: a drought can come in peacetime. The Midianite raids that stripped the land (Judg 6:3–6) came a generation earlier, so Bethlehem's older people remember them. Ehud's killing of Eglon, king of Moab (Judg 3), is about 180 years in the past: old history, but the kind a village keeps. The Philistines have been on the coast for about 25 years and haven't yet pressed inland.

### How the genealogy works

- **Boaz → Obed → Jesse → David is direct,** as the story insists: "Obed, the father of Jesse, the father of David" (4:17).
- **Salmon → Boaz is compressed.** About 220 years separate Rahab's rescue from Boaz's birth. Biblical genealogies often skip generations. Matthew does it in the same list: "Joram fathered Uzziah" (Matt 1:8) leaves out Ahaziah, Joash and Amaziah (compare 1 Chr 3:11–12). So "Salmon fathered Boaz by Rahab" (Matt 1:5) names the couple who founded Boaz's line, and Ruth 4:21 and 1 Chronicles 2:11 compress the same stretch.
- **Earlier links hold without gaps** on the short stay in Egypt, given the long lives the text records for that era (Exod 6:16–20): Hezron goes down to Egypt with Jacob (Gen 46:12), and his great-grandson Nahshon leads Judah in the wilderness (Num 1:7).

### Rahab in the story

- **No cameo.** She's been dead for more than two centuries.
- **A family memory.** Bethlehem knows that Boaz's line goes back to the woman of Jericho who hid Israel's spies. That's clan knowledge, the kind kept in genealogies like 4:18–22. The treatment has one gleaner say it in passing (P6). Boaz himself never mentions it.
- **Why it matters.** A Canaanite woman who chose Israel's God stands at the root of Boaz's family, and a Moabite woman who chooses the same God marries into it. Matthew names both (Matt 1:5).

### The road not taken

Under the late Exodus date (the 1260s), Rahab could have been Boaz's literal mother. Ruth would fall around 1165–1160, and Rahab might even have been alive when Ruth arrived, at about 80. But that model reads 1 Kings 6:1's "480 years" and Jephthah's "300 years" (Judg 11:26) non-literally and squeezes the Judges period into about 170 years (TIMELINE §3.1). Not adopted.

### Other traditions (P3)

- **Josephus** puts Ruth in the days of Eli (*Antiquities* 5.318). Our chronology agrees.
- **The Talmud** identifies Boaz with Ibzan, a judge from Bethlehem with thirty sons and thirty daughters (Judg 12:8–10; *Bava Batra* 91a). In our timeline Ibzan judges about forty years after Ruth's wedding, so the identification doesn't fit. The Talmud also says Rahab married Joshua (*Megillah* 14b). Neither is used.
- **The name.** Matthew spells it *Rhachab*, while Hebrews and James spell it *Rhaab*. A few scholars have wondered whether Matthew means a different woman, but nearly all identify her with Rahab of Jericho.

## 6. Where

- **Bethlehem.**
  - About 9 km (5–6 miles) south of Jerusalem. Jerusalem was then called Jebus, a Canaanite town that wasn't yet Israelite.
  - Set on a ridge about 775 m above sea level, among terraced hills, with grain fields in the valleys to the east and the Judean desert beyond them.
  - **On clear days, the mountains of Moab are visible across the Dead Sea from the hills east of town.** Naomi could have looked across at where her husband and sons are buried.
- **Ephrathah** is Bethlehem's clan and district name (1:2; 4:11; Gen 35:19; Mic 5:2).
- **Moab.**
  - A high plateau east of the Dead Sea, around 900–1,000 m above sea level. Its heartland lies south of the Arnon gorge (Wadi Mujib). The land north of the Arnon was disputed (Num 21:26).
  - "The country of Moab" (*sedeh Moav*) is literally "the fields of Moab."
- **The road.**
  - One route ran east from Bethlehem down through the desert, or north via Jericho, to the Jordan fords, then across and up onto the plateau. The other ran around the southern end of the Dead Sea.
  - Either way, it dropped more than 1,200 m into the Dead Sea basin, the lowest place on earth (about 430 m below sea level today), and climbed back up.
  - It took roughly a week on foot, and it was dangerous for two women traveling alone.
- **The threshing floor** was a patch of open, packed ground near the town, placed to catch the wind. Naomi says "go down" (3:3), which fits a hilltop town with the floor lower down.
- **The gate** was where legal business was done in front of the elders (Deut 21:19; 22:15; 25:7). Early Iron Age Bethlehem was probably a small village, and its "gate" may have been a modest opening with benches.

## 7. The world

### 7.1 Famine, and a world falling apart

Under our chronology, the famine (around 1152 BC) falls at the tail end of the **Late Bronze Age collapse** (about 1200–1150 BC). In those decades:
- the Hittite empire fell;
- Mycenaean Greece's palaces burned;
- Ugarit was destroyed;
- Egypt fought off the "Sea Peoples."

Pollen preserved in sediment cores from the Sea of Galilee and the Dead Sea points to a severe dry period from about 1250 to 1100 BC (Langgut, Finkelstein and Litt, 2013). Rain fell unevenly, so one district could starve while another, a few days' walk away, still had grain. A Bethlehem family walking to Moab for food fits that pattern exactly.

### 7.2 Moab and Israel

From Israel's side, Moab came with a lot of baggage:

| Text | What it says about Moab |
|---|---|
| Gen 19:30–38 | Moab descends from Lot and his elder daughter, born after she got her father drunk and slept with him. It's a night scene in which a woman takes the initiative with an older man who has been drinking, which Ruth 3 echoes deliberately (§10). |
| Num 22–24 | Balak, king of Moab, hires Balaam to curse Israel. |
| Num 25 | Moabite women draw Israelite men into the worship of Baal of Peor. |
| Deut 2:9 | God tells Israel not to harass Moab, because he gave Moab its land too. |
| Deut 23:3–6 | No Moabite may enter "the assembly of Yahweh," even to the tenth generation. |
| Judg 3:12–30 | Eglon, king of Moab, oppresses Israel for 18 years until Ehud assassinates him. |
| 1 Sam 22:3–4 | Later, David leaves his parents under the protection of the king of Moab: a quiet echo of his Moabite great-grandmother. |

Moab's god was **Chemosh** (Num 21:29). The Moabite language was very close to Hebrew. We know it from the Mesha Stele (about 840 BC), whose king writes about Chemosh in terms strikingly like Israel's: Chemosh is angry with his land, then restores it.

Rabbinic law later resolved the tension between Ruth and Deuteronomy 23:3 by ruling that the ban applies to Moabite *men*, while Moabite women could join at once (Mishnah, *Yevamot* 8:3). That's P3, but it shows the question was felt.

**The original hearers would have registered "Ruth the Moabite" (1:22; 2:2, 21; 4:5, 10) as a shock every time.**

### 7.3 Widows, foreigners and the poor

The law protects "the sojourner, the fatherless and the widow." That includes the right to glean, meaning to gather what reapers miss or drop, along with the corners of the field (Lev 19:9–10; 23:22; Deut 24:19–22). Ruth falls into two of those categories at once: she's a foreigner and a widow.

A childless widow had no one to provide for her and no one to protect her. Gleaning kept people alive, but it was humiliating, and dangerous for a woman alone (2:9, 15, 22).

### 7.4 Redemption and marriage

- **The redeemer (*go'el*).** The nearest male relative had the right and the duty to:
  - buy back family land that had been sold (Lev 25:25);
  - buy back a relative sold into slavery (Lev 25:47–49);
  - avenge a murdered relative, as the "avenger of blood" (Num 35:19).

  The principle behind it: "The land is mine," says God (Lev 25:23). Land stays in families.
- **Levirate marriage (Deut 25:5–10).** If a man died without a son, his brother married the widow, and their firstborn carried on the dead man's name. A brother who refused was shamed at the gate: the widow pulled off his sandal and spat in his face. Judah and Tamar show the custom in action (Gen 38), and Ruth 4:12 cites them.
- **Ruth's case fits neither cleanly.** Boaz isn't a brother, and land redemption normally didn't include a marriage. **Ruth herself joins the two together** at the threshing floor (3:9), and Boaz carries that through at the gate. The custom of the sandal in Ruth 4:7–8 is different too: the man takes off *his own* sandal to transfer his right, with no shaming.
- **Marriage signals:**
  - Spreading the corner of a garment over a woman meant marrying her (Ezek 16:8).
  - Washing, anointing with oil and dressing marked the end of mourning (2 Sam 12:20) and a bride's preparation (Ezek 16:9–10).

### 7.5 The harvest

- **The calendar.** The barley harvest came in April, around Passover, and the wheat harvest in May and June, ending around Pentecost: about seven weeks in all (2:23).
- **In the field:**
  - Men cut the grain with sickles. In the early Iron Age these were still often bronze or flint-toothed, with iron just arriving.
  - Workers bound the stalks into sheaves, and gleaners followed behind.
  - Water was kept in large jars (2:9).
- **The midday meal** was bread dipped in sour wine, and roasted grain (2:14).
- **Threshing.** The sheaves were spread on the threshing floor and trampled by oxen or crushed under a weighted sledge.
- **Winnowing.** In the evening breeze, workers tossed the grain into the air with forks or shovels. The wind blew the chaff away and the grain fell into a pile. Owners slept beside their grain to guard it.
- **Its reputation.** The threshing floor at harvest time was a place of feasting and drink, with a sexual reputation (Hos 9:1: "you have loved a prostitute's wages on every threshing floor").

### 7.6 Daily life in the hill country (early Iron Age)

- **Houses** were "four-room" pillared houses, with animals and storage on the ground floor, people living above, and a shared courtyard and a flat roof.
- **Equipment:**
  - Water came from cisterns cut into the rock.
  - Bread was baked in clay ovens (*tabun*).
  - Light came from small clay oil lamps.
  - Grain and oil were stored in big "collared-rim" jars, a signature find of the period.
- **Clothing:**
  - a tunic, with an outer cloak (*simlah*, 3:3) that doubled as a blanket at night;
  - a large shawl or wrap (*mitpachat*, 3:15);
  - sandals.

  Widows wore distinctive clothing (Gen 38:14, 19), though we don't know exactly what it looked like.
- **Money.** There were no coins. Silver was weighed out, and grain worked as currency too.
- **Food** included bread, olive oil, wine, lentils, chickpeas, figs, grapes, pomegranates, dates, cheese and yogurt, with meat only rarely.
- **Animals** included donkeys, sheep and goats, and oxen for plowing and threshing.

## 9. What they knew, and what we know

### What the first audience knew that we don't

| Moment | What they felt | How we can get it across |
|---|---|---|
| A Bethlehem family moves to *Moab* | Moving to enemy country, and into the territory of the people descended from Lot's daughters | Bethlehem villagers' reactions; a Moabite marketplace that's foreign but alive |
| "Ruth the Moabite" | A shock and a stigma, every time | Townspeople's looks and whispers, and the foreman's careful phrasing |
| Gleaning | The bottom rung of society, and dangerous for a woman alone | The threat shown in the field, and Boaz's orders as protection |
| "Redeemer" | A legal and family obligation everyone understood | One line of setup early on (a naturally motivated explanation, §7.2 of the ROADMAP), or Naomi working it out aloud |
| Washing, oil and a cloak | She's ending her mourning, or dressing as a bride | Visual ritual: the widow's clothes come off |
| A woman alone at a threshing floor at night | Scandal, and the risk of being taken for a prostitute | Other workers sleeping nearby; Boaz's urgency about secrecy (3:14) |
| "Spread your wing over me" | A marriage proposal | Boaz's stunned reaction |
| The sandal | An old custom even when the story was written (4:7) | Show it carefully and let the elders' recognition carry it |
| "Father of Jesse, father of David" | The punchline: the foreign widow is King David's great-grandmother | The ending's final image (§12) |

### What we know that they didn't

- **No character knows about David.** Ruth, Naomi and Boaz never hint at it. The ending can let the audience see it.
- **No one knows about Jesus** (Matt 1:5).
- **Characters know customs, not books.** They talk about what's done, not about Deuteronomy as a written text.
- **The town knows Boaz's lineage.** His family descends from Rahab, the Canaanite woman of Jericho. That's not hindsight; it's clan memory (§5).

## 11. Framings and decisions that apply

- **FR-08: Boaz as Ruth's "relative opposite."** It stays unspoken unless it feels right to say. It's staged through mirroring: "worth" (2:1 and 3:11), the wing (2:12 and 3:9), *hesed* on both sides, and opposite circumstances with matching character. The marriage to Mahlon is loving on Ruth's side but unfinished: the barrier the water flows around ([FRAMINGS](../../../canon/FRAMINGS.md#fr-08--marriage-to-ones-relative-opposite)).
- **Discussion 001: God working through people.** God never speaks in Ruth, so there's no God-voice to decide. The narrator's two direct statements can be carried by characters: Naomi hears the news "Yahweh has visited his people" (1:6), and the women say "Blessed be Yahweh…" (4:14). Providence shows up as "chance," harvest and human kindness ([Discussion 001](../../../discussions/001-how-god-speaks.md)).
- **The Name used in full.** "Yahweh" is spoken about 16 times, in 1:8, 9, 13, 17, 21 (twice); 2:4 (twice), 12 (twice), 20; 3:10, 13; 4:11, 12, 14. Naomi also uses "Shaddai" (1:20, 21), the older name, which sets her voice apart.
- **God among the nations (light version).** Ruth leaves "her gods" for Yahweh (1:15–16), and Orpah goes back to hers without condemnation. Moab is shown as foreign but sincere (§12).
- **Language (approach C).** Ruth, Naomi and Boaz speak English. Moabites speaking among themselves could use reconstructed Moabite, which would have to be modeled closely on Biblical Hebrew, since Moabite has no living descendant. That's low confidence, so it should be kept to a few lines if used. Ruth's slight accent is a note for casting.
- **The intensity ceiling (principle 8).** The grief in Moab, the danger in the fields and the charge of the threshing floor can all play at full strength. Nothing is explicit.
- **Modern words, ancient minds.** Naomi doesn't "process grief," and Ruth's loyalty is astonishing *because* it cuts against every family and ethnic obligation she has.
